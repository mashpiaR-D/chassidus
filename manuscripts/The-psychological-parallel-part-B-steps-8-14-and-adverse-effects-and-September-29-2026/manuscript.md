**What this is, and what it is not.** This is a parallel academic map for the builders: a guideline and a check. It is not the guide's voice and it does not replace the Chassidic content. The guide never speaks in therapy terms. It never sends anyone to therapy outside real danger. It is not mindfulness. Nothing here claims that psychology "proves" Chassidus. Where the two meet, we say so. Where they only look alike, we say exactly how they differ.

**How to read the strength labels.** - **Meta-analysis**: many studies pooled. Strongest, but only as good as the studies in it. - **Registered replication**: a large, pre-planned repeat of a known experiment, often across many labs. - **Experiment**: people randomly put into conditions, usually in a lab and usually small. - **Correlational**: measures that go together, with no proof of cause. - **Qualitative**: interviews and close description. - **Measure validation**: a paper that builds and tests a questionnaire. - **Theory or review**: argument and synthesis, no new data.

**A general warning that applies to every step.** Most of this research was done on Western university students or online samples. Almost none was done on people doing conceptual contemplation of God's unity. A parallel tells the builders what to watch for. It does not tell them what a Chassidic sitting does.

## Step 8 · Weeping and joy in one heart

**The step.** Two things that should not fit together are held at once: the bitter cry at one's distance from Him and joy at His nearness, "one arousal, only one comes from the far side" (Kuntres HaHitpa'alut). The Mitteler Rebbe's test for real contemplation of the unity is itself a pair: "as much joy, exactly that much bitul" (Gate of Unity 5). The mind cannot join the two; it keeps both true and says both to Him.

**Psychological terms.** - *Mixed emotions* (also called emotional complexity or co-activation): feeling a pleasant and an unpleasant emotion at the same moment, such as happy and sad together. - *Poignancy*: the mixed feeling that comes at a meaningful ending. - *Being moved*, and its cross-cultural form *kama muta* ("moved by love" in Sanskrit, the researchers' name): a tearful, warm, often mixed feeling set off by a sudden sense of closeness or belonging. - *Dialectical thinking*: being willing to hold a contradiction without forcing it to resolve. - *Affect labeling*: putting a feeling into words. - *Expressive suppression*: hiding or damping a feeling once it has started.

**What the research says.** - People can feel happy and sad at once, though it is not the everyday norm. In typical moments most people felt one or the other, but many felt both after a bittersweet film, moving out of a dormitory, or graduating (Larsen, McGraw & Cacioppo, 2001). *Experiment and field survey.* - The mixed state is not just rapid switching between the two, and not just an artifact of being asked about both. Continuous moment-by-moment ratings and open-ended reports both showed real co-occurrence (Larsen & McGraw, 2011). *Experiments, six studies.* - Across 63 experiments, mixed emotions were reliably produced, with a moderate-to-large effect (d = 0.77), whatever the theory of emotion used or the pair of emotions studied (Berrios, Totterdell & Kellett, 2015). *Meta-analysis.* - Mixed feelings rise when people are reminded that something meaningful is ending (Ersner-Hershfield, Mikels, Sullivan & Carstensen, 2008). *Experiment and field study.* - In 47 adults in psychotherapy, feeling happiness and sadness together was linked to later gains in well-being, beyond the effect of either feeling alone, and changes in mixed feeling came before the gains (Adler & Hershfield, 2012). *Correlational, longitudinal; small sample.* - Being moved is chiefly made of sadness and joy together; it is felt as intense even at low arousal, and people rate themselves as neither causing the event nor able to change it (Menninghaus et al., 2015). *Survey studies.* Across 19 countries and 3,542 people, the moved-to-tears state (kama muta) appears as a distinct emotion with warmth in the chest, moist eyes, chills, and a pull toward devotion and commitment to the relationship (Zickfeld et al., 2019). *Measure validation, cross-cultural.* - Tears can come from intense *positive* feeling too, and may help regulate an overwhelming emotion (Aragón, Clark, Dyer & Bargh, 2015). *Experiments.* A review proposes that crying soothes the one who cries, while noting the findings on mood after crying are mixed (Gračanin, Bylsma & Vingerhoets, 2014). *Review.* - Holding a contradiction as acceptable makes mixed feeling more likely: when dialectical thinking was raised experimentally, emotional complexity rose (Spencer-Rodgers, Peng & Wang, 2010). *Experiment.* - Putting a feeling into words dampens the brain's alarm response to it (Lieberman et al., 2007). *Brain-imaging experiment.* Hiding a feeling after it arises (suppression) goes with *less* positive feeling, *more* negative feeling and worse relationships (Gross & John, 2003). *Correlational, five studies.*

**Measures.** The Evaluative Space Grid, a one-item grid that rates positive and negative feeling separately, so both can be high at once (Larsen et al., 2009). The KAMMUS Two scale for kama muta (Zickfeld et al., 2019). The Emotion Regulation Questionnaire for habitual suppression versus reappraisal (Gross & John, 2003).

**Match.** Strong on the surface. Chassidus and the research agree that two opposed feelings can be real at once, that neither cancels the other, and that the mixed state is not a failure of feeling. The research's "being moved" (sudden closeness, tears, a pull to devotion) is close to the Zohar's weeping on one side of the heart and joy on the other. Chassidus's "pour out both to Him in your own words" is close to affect labeling, and the counterfeit "numbing both feelings and calling it peace" is close to what the research calls suppression.

**Where they differ.** The research studies a mix of *moods* about an event. Chassidus describes one arousal with one object (the One), seen from two sides: distance and nearness. The joining, the sources say, is His, "the perfection of His Essence bears all opposites", not a skill of the person. The Gate of Unity's pair is also not happy-and-sad: it is joy and *bitul* (self-giving), and bitul is not an emotion in the research's sense at all. Mixed-emotion research has no construct for "joy in exact proportion to self-giving".

**What it gives the guide.** - Never ask the person to choose between the two feelings, and never tidy one away. The research finds the mixed state real and, in one small study, a sign of coming improvement. This fits the guide's "don't trade one for the other". - Saying both, in plain words, to Him (the sitting's own words-to-Him stage) is the Chassidic act; the research on affect labeling suggests that naming each side lowers its grip without cancelling it.

**Risks the research flags.** Suppression: damping both feelings to feel calm is linked to worse outcomes, which matches the step's counterfeit ("numbing both and calling it peace"). Tears in themselves are not proof of anything: the research finds they come from joy, grief and overwhelm alike.

## Step 9 · Alone: where the mind stops (rung three)

**The step.** Before Him as He is, He is *yachid*, truly alone, above *echad* (parts joined). The mind's last moves, in the Rashab's order: grasping by negation, knowing the wonder, recognition, and, if it comes, seeing. From inside: a hush, nothing to hold. The Piaseczner did not know what his soul saw, or whether it saw. The edge: no grasp reaches the Essence, not even negation; its use is the thirst it leaves. Counterfeit: thinking you have reached the top; a cold detachment; a spacey, unreal feeling (the remedy: stop, do something plain with your hands).

**Psychological terms.** - *Awe*: the response to something so vast that it does not fit current understanding. Its two core parts are *perceived vastness* and a *need for accommodation* (having to stretch one's mental frame). - *The small self*: the sense of one's own self shrinking in the face of something vast. - *Ineffability* and *noetic quality*: two classic marks of mystical experience; it cannot be put into words, and yet it feels like knowing. - *Self-transcendent experience*: a passing state with less focus on the self and more connection. - *Non-dual awareness*: a Buddhist-derived term for awareness without the split between subject and object. - *Ego dissolution* and *depersonalization*: loss of the ordinary sense of self, the first often sought, the second a clinical symptom (feeling unreal, detached, a watcher of oneself).

**What the research says.** - Awe is defined by vastness and a need to accommodate what does not fit (Keltner & Haidt, 2003). *Theory.* Awe experiences are focused on the thing outside, diminish the sense of self, and in people prone to awe go with a lower need for quick closure (Shiota, Keltner & Mossman, 2007). *Experiments and correlational.* - Awe shrinks the felt self in daily life, in the lab and in the field, across collectivist and individualist cultures, and this small self accounts for a greater sense of belonging to the group (Bai et al., 2017, six studies, N = 2,137). Inducing awe raised generosity and ethical choices, partly through the small self (Piff et al., 2015, five studies, N = 2,078). *Experiments.* Large preregistered replications of the awe-to-generosity effect are something we could not confirm (unverified). - Self-transcendent experiences (awe, flow, mystical-type states, some forms of love) share lower self-salience and higher connectedness (Yaden, Haidt, Hood, Vago & Newberg, 2017). *Review.* The same review notes that self-loss also appears in illness, such as depersonalization. - The classic mysticism measure is built on Stace's categories, including ineffability, a noetic sense, and unity (Hood, 1975). *Measure validation.* - In one line of Tibetan Buddhist practice, non-dual awareness is described as preceding concept and intention; early brain data are preliminary (Josipovic, 2014). *Review with small studies.* - Experienced meditators showed quieter activity in brain regions tied to self-referential thinking and mind-wandering (Brewer et al., 2011). *Cross-sectional brain imaging; small groups, cannot show cause.*

**Measures.** The Awe Experience Scale (AWE-S), with six factors: altered time, self-diminishment, connectedness, vastness, physical sensations, need for accommodation (Yaden et al., 2019). The Hood Mysticism Scale (Hood, 1975). The revised Mystical Experience Questionnaire, MEQ30 (Barrett, Johnson & Griffiths, 2015; built with psilocybin, so use with care outside drug studies). The Ego-Dissolution Inventory (Nour et al., 2016; also built for drug experiences). **As a safety check, not an outcome:** the Cambridge Depersonalisation Scale (Sierra & Berrios, 2000).

**Match.** Real but partial. The Rashab's "knowing of wonder" is close to awe's need for accommodation: the mind meets what it cannot fit. The hush and the Piaseczner's "not knowing what was seen" are close to ineffability. The small self is close to "less full of yourself". The research also finds that awe points outward, to the thing and to others, and this matches the ladder's insistence that the top leads down into the world.

**Where they differ.** This is the step where flattening does most harm. - **Bitul is not ego dissolution.** Ego dissolution is a *loss* of the sense of self, measured by how much of it goes. *Efes* ("nothing apart from You") is a *recognition about Him*: that nothing stands apart from Him, including me. The person does not vanish; the Chassidic self goes on to pray, eat and act. The self in Chassidus is not an illusion to be seen through; it is real from where it stands and "held". - **Yachid is not non-dual awareness.** Non-dual awareness is about the structure of *experience* (no subject-object split). Yachid is a claim about *Him*, which the sources say no experience reaches: "not even grasp by negation". Chassidus explicitly says the rung is seen only "from behind". - **Awe research measures a feeling.** In Chassidus the goal of this rung is not the feeling but what it leaves: thirst for Him, and the return down. - The mysticism scales assume a peak experience that can be rated. The sources warn that the deepest moment may be the one least felt, and that examining it spoils it.

**What it gives the guide.** - Describe the hush and the not-knowing as possible, never as the goal, and never ask the person to rate how "unified" they felt. The research tools that do this were built for peak states and drugs. - Keep the step short and always follow it with something plain and physical (the manual's washed dish). The research on depersonalization gives this rule a second reason.

**Risks the research flags.** Changes in the sense of self are one of the seven domains of reported meditation difficulty (Lindahl et al., 2017), and signs of dissociation (which includes feeling unreal or detached) were linked to lasting bad effects (Britton et al., 2021). This matches the step's counterfeit (spacey, unreal) and its remedy exactly. A second risk is the "cold detachment" of treating the world as not counting, which the research calls spiritual bypassing (see the counterfeits section).

## Step 10 · The heart's simple will: where you and He meet (the summit)

**The step.** The yechidah, the single one at the bottom of the soul, is always one with His Essence. The mind stops; what reaches Him now is *re'uta deliba*, the heart's simple will, an essential bond like a son's with his father, whose plainest sign is the martyr's refusal to be separated from the One. From inside: the song that sings itself, "the deeper, the less he feels it". Do not examine it while it happens. Counterfeit: "I am one with God" turning into grandness or a mission; watching oneself for signs; making the arousal the main thing ("serving oneself").

**Psychological terms.** - *Hypo-egoic states*: states with less self-watching and less self-concern, in which a person acts more naturally (flow is one). - *Quiet ego*: a self-identity that is secure but not defensive, balanced between self and others. - *Flow*: full absorption in an activity, with loss of self-consciousness. - *Sacred values*: commitments people treat as absolute and will not trade for anything. - *Attachment to God*: the relationship with God experienced as a bond like a child's with a parent, a safe haven and secure base. - *Ironic monitoring*: the finding that watching a mental state in order to control it can produce its opposite. - *Absorption*: a trait of being easily and deeply taken up in experience.

**What the research says.** - Deliberate self-control sometimes defeats itself. Hypo-egoic states (flow, transcendence) involve lowered self-awareness, and people can choose to regulate "hypo-egoically" when effortful control would get in the way (Leary, Adams & Tate, 2006). *Theory and review.* A handbook gathers the field (Brown & Leary, 2016). - Trying to control a mental state sets up a monitoring process that searches for failure; under load, it can produce the opposite of what was intended (Wegner, 1994). *Theory with experimental support.* - Monitoring one's own happiness while listening to music lowered reported happiness, and trying to be happy lowered mood (Schooler, Ariely & Loewenstein, 2003). *Small experiment; the authors call it preliminary and in need of replication.* Valuing happiness highly made people react less happily to a happy emotion induction, through disappointment at their own feelings (Mauss, Tamir, Anderson & Savino, 2011). *Experiment and correlational; women only.* - Sacred values resist trade-offs: people react with outrage to trading them for money (Tetlock, 2003, *review*), and such values drive costly commitment regardless of likely outcome (Atran & Ginges, 2012, *review*). - A believer's felt relationship with God meets the criteria of an attachment bond, with two routes into it: a secure one that mirrors good care, and a compensating one that can build "earned security" (Granqvist, Mikulincer & Shaver, 2010). *Review.* - **Two cautions from the research.** Yoga and meditation practice, rather than quieting the ego, raised self-enhancement measured after practice sessions (compared with times without practice), because the practiced skill became central to the self (Gebauer et al., 2018, two studies following practitioners over weeks). And a measure of "spiritual superiority" was linked to communal narcissism and to self-worth that depends on spiritual achievement (Vonk & Visser, 2021, three studies). *Experimental and correlational.* - People with a trait of absorption report sharper imagery and more unusual spiritual experiences, and these grow with prayer practice (Luhrmann, Nusbaum & Thisted, 2010; the trait from Tellegen & Atkinson, 1974). *Ethnography with measures.*

**Measures.** The Quiet Ego Scale (Wayment, Bauer & Sylaska, 2015). The Flow State Scale, including its loss-of-self-consciousness subscale (Jackson & Marsh, 1996). The Attachment to God Inventory, with its avoidance and anxiety subscales (Beck & McDonald, 2004). **As safety checks:** the Spiritual Superiority measure (Vonk & Visser, 2021) and the Tellegen Absorption Scale (Tellegen & Atkinson, 1974) to know who is more prone to vivid, unusual experience.

**Match.** Two genuine matches, one of them strong. - *Strong:* "the deeper, the less he feels it" and "do not examine it" (Esh Kodesh) line up with the research on ironic monitoring and self-defeating happiness-watching. Both say that watching a deep state for signs spoils it. The Mitteler Rebbe's "serving oneself", wanting the arousal more than Him, is close to Mauss's finding that valuing the feeling itself breeds disappointment. - *Real:* the martyr's will that will not be separated from the One has the shape of a sacred value, and the "son with his father" bond has the shape of attachment to God.

**Where they differ.** Deeply. - Hypo-egoic states and flow are about a *less active* self-concept. The yechidah is not less self; it is the deepest self, which Chassidus says *is* the power of the Essence in the soul. No psychological construct holds that claim, and none should be made to. - Sacred-value research is about *values* held by people and groups, and much of it studies conflict. The heart's simple will is not a value one holds; it is, in the sources, what one *is* at the bottom. - Attachment research measures a *felt* relationship and its security. The yechidah's bond is said to be there always, felt or not, in the unlearned and scholars alike. - Flow needs a task with rising challenge. The summit has no task and no skill.

**What it gives the guide.** - Never ask, during or right after, "did you feel it?" or "how close were you?" Both the sources and the research on monitoring say the checking itself lowers what is checked. Check later, by the fruit (see step 14). - Offer the summit through something simple the person does not have to judge: a plain prayer, the Shema, a hummed tune, or naming what they would never give up. Do not grade it. - Because practice can raise self-enhancement rather than lower it, the guide should never praise a person for having reached this rung.

**Risks the research flags.** Grandiosity and spiritual self-enhancement (Gebauer; Vonk & Visser), which is the step's own counterfeit. People high in absorption may have vivid experiences that feel like the summit (Luhrmann). The research does not show this is harmful, but it means intensity says nothing about depth. At the extreme, "a special mission" with sleeplessness is a sign of illness, not of the yechidah (see the counterfeits section).

## Step 11 · The plainest thing: the world held, one with Him (the turn)

**The step.** The top is not the world erased. The truest unity is that the world's own existence is one with Him; the Shema says *echad*, not *yachid*. The mind comes back and sees the plain thing as plain and His: the bread is bread, and it is His. The Rashab warns this is harder than rapture. Counterfeit: "the bread is glowing" (a new high), or "everything is holy, so I can indulge". Act: eat one thing slowly, with its blessing or a thank-you.

**Psychological terms.** - *Sanctification*: perceiving an ordinary part of life as holy, either as a manifestation of God (theistic) or as having sacred qualities. - *Savoring*: attending to and drawing out a good experience. - *Gratitude*: noticing and appreciating the good received, and the disposition to do so. - *Daily spiritual experience*: ordinary, non-peak moments of spiritual awareness in everyday life. - *Moral licensing*: the idea that a good act gives people a sense of permission for a worse one.

**What the research says.** - People can see almost any part of life as holy, and doing so goes with investing in it, protecting it, and spiritual emotion (Pargament & Mahoney, 2005). *Theory and review.* Couples who saw their marriage as sanctified, including seeing God as present in it, reported better adjustment and less conflict (Mahoney et al., 1999, 97 couples). *Correlational.* - Keeping a gratitude list raised well-being on several but not all measures; the effect on positive mood was the most robust (Emmons & McCullough, 2003). *Experiments.* Across trials, gratitude interventions beat a measure-only control on well-being (d = .31) and beat neutral activities modestly (d = .17), but did no better than other active psychological exercises (Davis et al., 2016). *Meta-analysis; small effects.* - People differ reliably in their belief that they can savor the good, and this belief predicts enjoying real events (Bryant, 2003). *Measure validation.* - Ordinary spiritual experiences, such as a sense of God's presence in daily life, deep inner peace, or joy that lifts one out of the mundane, can be measured reliably (Underwood & Teresi, 2002). *Measure validation.* - A meta-analysis estimated moral licensing at d = 0.31 (Blanken, van de Ven & Zeelenberg, 2015), but three high-powered replications of a key study failed (Blanken et al., 2014), and correcting for publication bias shrank the effect to between roughly zero and d = 0.18 (Kuper & Bott, 2019). *Meta- analyses and replications; the effect is doubtful.*

**Measures.** The Daily Spiritual Experience Scale (Underwood & Teresi, 2002). Sanctification measures of the kind used by Mahoney et al. (1999), adapted to eating or daily life. The Gratitude Questionnaire, GQ-6 (McCullough, Emmons & Tsang, 2002). The Savoring Beliefs Inventory (Bryant, 2003).

**Match.** Good, and it is the best parallel in the descent. "Theistic sanctification", seeing an ordinary thing as a manifestation of God, is very close to "the bread is bread, and it is His". The Daily Spiritual Experience Scale is one of the few tools that measures what this step aims at: holiness *without* a peak. Saying a blessing before eating is, in research terms, a brief gratitude practice.

**Where they differ.** Sanctification research measures a *perception*, which can be stronger or weaker. Chassidus says the world's existence *is* one with Him whether or not it is perceived; the perception is our side only. Savoring aims to *enhance* the pleasure. The Chassidic plainness does not add anything: it warns that making the bread glow is a new high, which is a counterfeit. Gratitude research treats thanks as an intervention for well-being. The blessing is owed and said whether or not it lifts the mood.

**What it gives the guide.** - Keep the act at this step plain. Do not ask the person to notice how good or special the food is (that is savoring, and it tends to the counterfeit). Ask only for the blessing or the thank-you, and for eating slowly. - Do not promise a mood lift from the thank-you. The research shows the effect is small and no bigger than other exercises; the guide's reason for it is not the mood.

**Risks the research flags.** "Everything is holy, so I can indulge" has the shape of moral licensing, but the evidence that licensing is a real, reliable effect is weak after replication. So the risk is better named from the sources than from the research. Chasing a special glow in ordinary things is a quieter form of the need for peak experiences.

## Step 12 · Coming down: the dark place turned

**The step.** The unity is shown most fully by turning the dark, not escaping it: first subduing (*iskafya*), then turning (*is'hapcha*). Do not argue with the pull or answer it; act as though you did not hear it (Tanya 28), and add strength to what you are doing. Tell Him the small "no" in your own words. From inside: sharp, then lighter; a broken heart that becomes a vessel. Counterfeit: "since darkness turns to light, let me go into the dark"; pride in the win; a crushing self-inspection on a low day.

**Psychological terms.** - *Thought suppression* and its *rebound*: trying not to think a thought, which can make it come back more. - *Redirection* (focused distraction): turning attention to a chosen alternative instead of fighting the thought. - *Ego depletion*: the claim that self-control is a resource that runs out. - *Implementation intentions*: "if situation X, then I will do Y" plans. - *Guilt versus shame*: guilt is about the deed ("I did a bad thing"), shame about the whole self ("I am bad"). - *Self-compassion*: meeting one's own failure with kindness rather than harsh self-judgment. - *Redemption sequence*: a life story in which something bad turns into something good. - *Religious coping*: using faith to meet a stressor, in positive or negative ways.

**What the research says.** - Trying not to think of a white bear failed, and afterward the thought came back more (Wegner, Schneider, Carter & White, 1987). But in the second experiment, people given a *specific* other thought to turn to showed less later preoccupation. *Experiments.* Across controlled studies, the rebound after suppression is small to moderate (Abramowitz, Tolin & Street, 2001). *Meta-analysis.* A later meta-analysis found rebound regardless of mental load (Wang, Hagger & Chatzisarantis, 2020). *Meta-analysis.* - The popular claim that self-control is a tank that runs dry did not replicate: across 23 labs and 2,141 people the effect was near zero (d = 0.04) (Hagger et al., 2016). *Registered replication.* - "If-then" plans that tie a response to a cue help people meet goals, with a medium-to-large effect across 94 tests (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006). *Meta-analysis.* - Guilt tends to lead to repair (confession, apology, undoing the harm); shame tends to lead to defensiveness, withdrawal and anger (Tangney, Stuewig & Mashek, 2007). *Review.* - Treating oneself kindly after a failure *increased* motivation to make amends and improve, compared with boosting self-esteem or doing nothing (Breines & Chen, 2012). *Experiments.* - In life stories, redemption sequences (bad turning good) go with higher well-being, and contamination sequences (good turning bad) with lower (McAdams et al., 2001). *Correlational.* - The paper distinguishes positive and negative patterns of religious coping (negative: for example, feeling punished or abandoned by God) and gives a brief 14-item measure of both; negative coping went with worse outcomes, such as more post-traumatic symptoms among Oklahoma City residents after the bombing (Pargament, Smith, Koenig & Perez, 1998). *Correlational; summary taken from secondary sources, as the abstract was not available to us.* - Self-reported "growth" after trauma did not match measured change before and after the event, and was linked to *more* distress (Frazier et al., 2009). *Prospective study.*

**Measures.** The Self-Compassion Scale (Neff, 2003). The Brief RCOPE for positive and negative religious coping (Pargament et al., 1998). Redemption coding of narratives (McAdams et al., 2001). The Posttraumatic Growth Inventory (Tedeschi & Calhoun, 1996), but read with Frazier et al. (2009): it records perceived, not actual, change.

**Match.** Strong at the level of method. - The Tanya's "act as if you did not hear it, and add strength to what you are doing" is *not* suppression. It declines to argue and turns to a chosen, specific action. Wegner's second experiment found exactly this kind of focused redirection reduced the rebound. The research gives the Tanya's instruction a clear reason, and a clear warning against the version that fights the thought. - "One small 'no', told to Him in your own words" is a concrete, cue-bound plan with a named response; it has the form of an implementation intention. - The warning against "a crushing self-inspection on a low day" matches the research on shame versus guilt and on self-compassion: harsh global self-judgment tends to produce hiding, not change.

**Where they differ.** *Is'hapcha*, turning the dark itself into light, has no real parallel. Redemption narratives and post-traumatic growth are about how a person *tells* the story afterward. Chassidus claims the evil itself is made good and the animal soul turned "from opposite to opposite". The research also has no place for iskafya as an offering: in psychology, self-control serves the self's own goals.

**What it gives the guide.** - Never tell a person to "push the thought away". Say instead: don't argue with it, turn to the line or to the act at hand. Give them *one specific* thing to turn to. - Frame the small "no" as a ready plan: "when the pull comes, this is what I'll say to Him, and this is what my hands will do." - On a low day, keep the step about the deed ("I did this"), never about the whole self ("I am this").

**Risks the research flags.** Suppression rebound, if the step is taught as "don't think it". Shame spirals, if it is taught as self-examination on a heavy day. Overclaiming "growth" from pain: the research warns that people often report growth that has not happened, which fits the counterfeit "pride in the win".

## Step 13 · Coming down: the home below, this ordinary day

**The step.** He desired a dwelling in the lowest realm, and this is it: the kitchen and the Tuesday are the plain man's house the king comes to live in (Tanya 33). The soul was bound to the body to make everything one. From inside: the plain man's joy. Counterfeit: "everything I do is holy, so it doesn't matter what I do"; or escaping upward and neglecting the people in front of you. Act: a coin in the charity box before breakfast.

**Psychological terms.** - *Sanctification of daily life* (as in step 11). - *Meaning in life*: the sense that one's life has purpose and coherence. - *Prosocial spending*: spending on others rather than on oneself. - *Spiritual bypassing*: using spiritual ideas or practice to avoid ordinary emotional or relational work. - *Intrinsic versus extrinsic religiousness*: faith lived for its own sake versus faith used for comfort or social standing.

**What the research says.** - Seeing a sacred quality in ordinary relationships and activities goes with investing in and protecting them (Pargament & Mahoney, 2005; Mahoney et al., 1999). *Theory and correlational.* - Spending on others predicted greater happiness in a national survey and a windfall study, and people told to spend on others were happier than those told to spend on themselves (Dunn, Aknin & Norton, 2008). *Correlational and small experiment.* A preregistered replication supported the effect, but found it smaller and dependent on how the study is done (Aknin et al., 2020). *Registered replication.* - Acts of kindness raise the doer's well-being with a small-to-medium effect (δ = 0.28), with no sign of publication bias (Curry et al., 2018). *Meta-analysis.* - Spiritual bypassing was named by Welwood (1984) as using spiritual ideas to avoid unresolved psychological needs. *Theory.* It has since been measured as two linked parts: psychological avoidance and spiritualizing (Fox, Cashwell & Picciotto, 2017), and described in interviews as a defensive posture that puts spiritual belief or experience above psychological needs (Picciotto, Fox & Neto, 2018). *Measure validation and qualitative.*

**Measures.** The Daily Spiritual Experience Scale (Underwood & Teresi, 2002). The Meaning in Life Questionnaire, presence and search subscales (Steger et al., 2006). The Spiritual Bypass Scale-13 (Fox et al., 2017), as a check on the counterfeit.

**Match.** Good. The daily-life focus of sanctification and daily-spiritual-experience research fits "the home below". The counterfeit "escaping upward and neglecting the people in front of you" is almost word for word what the research calls spiritual bypassing. The coin before breakfast is a small prosocial act, and the research gives modest support for kindness doing the doer good.

**Where they differ.** For the research, meaning is something a person *finds or makes*. For Chassidus the purpose of the lowest place is His, given, and true whether or not the person feels meaning in it. The charity coin is not a well-being intervention: its point is that it is the first thing done in His house. The research's reasons (the doer is happier) are a side effect, and the guide should not offer them as the reason.

**What it gives the guide.** - Make the descent land on a named person or a named task today, not on a general feeling of holiness. This is the guide's own rule (ends in one act) and the research's best defence against bypassing. - If a person uses the unity to wave away a real hurt or a duty ("it's all His, so it doesn't matter"), treat that as the counterfeit the sources name, and bring them back to the plain thing in front of them.

**Risks the research flags.** Spiritual bypassing, which the scale research links to avoidance of difficult emotion. The research is newer and mostly Western; its scales were not built for Jewish practice.

## Step 14 · Coming down: the deed in the hand

**The step.** The will in the mitzvah has no reason, so only it brings the Essence itself into place and time. The line is done with the hand, slowly. The Piaseczner: when a good stirring comes, clothe it at once in a mitzvah or in learning. From inside, afterward, not during: a little quieter, an insult that doesn't sting, "I find myself another man". Counterfeit: rote doing called "the unity made actual"; doing it to be seen; or arousal with no deed, "a flame flying in the air". Then back to step 1, one rung deeper.

**Psychological terms.** - *Intention-behaviour gap*: the distance between meaning to do something and doing it. - *Implementation intentions* (as in step 12). - *Ritual*: a fixed, formal sequence of actions whose meaning is not in its practical effect. - *Habit and automaticity*: behaviour repeated in a stable context until it runs without deliberation. - *Intrinsic versus extrinsic religious orientation*. - *Behavioural activation*: a clinical method that changes mood by scheduling valued actions.

**What the research says.** - Changing intentions changes behaviour, but by less: a medium-to-large change in intention (d = 0.66) produced a small-to-medium change in behaviour (d = 0.36) (Webb & Sheeran, 2006). *Meta-analysis of experiments.* This is the "flame flying in the air" in numbers. - If-then plans that tie the act to a cue close much of that gap, with a medium-to-large effect (d = .65 across 94 tests) (Gollwitzer, 1999; Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006). *Theory and meta-analysis.* - Rituals regulate emotion, performance states and social connection, through both their physical form and their meaning (Hobson et al., 2018). *Integrative review.* - Repeating an act daily in the same context makes it more automatic, following a curve; the time to plateau ranged from 18 to 254 days, and missing one day did not derail it (Lally et al., 2010). *Longitudinal field study.* Habit strength can be measured by self-report (Verplanken & Orbell, 2003). - Kindness acts give a small-to-medium lift to the doer (Curry et al., 2018). *Meta-analysis.* In clinical depression, scheduling valued activity works about as well as cognitive therapy (Cuijpers, van Straten & Warmerdam, 2007). *Meta-analysis; clinical, cited only as a distant parallel.* - Religion lived for its own sake (intrinsic) differs in its correlates from religion used for social or personal benefit (extrinsic); in the original study the extrinsic group was more prejudiced (Allport & Ross, 1967), and the measure was later revised to split the extrinsic side into social and personal motives (Gorsuch & McPherson, 1989). *Correlational and measure validation.*

**Measures.** A simple behaviour count (did the act happen, today) is the best outcome, and matches the sources' "judge by the fruit". The Self-Report Habit Index for whether the act has become rote (Verplanken & Orbell, 2003). The Intrinsic/Extrinsic-Revised scales for "doing it to be seen" (Gorsuch & McPherson, 1989). The Quiet Ego Scale (Wayment et al., 2015) for the aftermath: less defensive, less stung.

**Match.** Strong on method. The Piaseczner's "clothe the stirring at once in a mitzvah" and the Mitteler Rebbe's "flame flying in the air" are, in research terms, a warning about the intention-behaviour gap and a remedy close to an implementation intention. The sources' test of the aftermath ("an insult that doesn't sting", "are you a little more your own master?") is a behavioural test, not a self-report of feeling, which is the kind of outcome researchers trust most.

**Where they differ.** - Habit research treats automaticity as the goal. The sources treat rote doing "with the mind elsewhere" as a counterfeit. The Chassidic deed is simple but not absent-minded: the servant's yoke, done with the hand, *slowly*. - Ritual research explains ritual by what it does for the person (calms, focuses, binds). Samach Vav says the mitzvah's will has *no* reason and its purpose is itself. The research's functions may be real side effects, but the guide must not present them as the point. - Behavioural activation chooses actions for their effect on mood. The deed here is not chosen for its effect.

**What it gives the guide.** - Every sitting ends with *one* named act, tied to a cue ("right after the sitting", "before breakfast"), small enough to do today. This is already the manual's rule; the research says the cue is what carries it. - Test later, by the fruit and by behaviour, never by how intense the sitting felt. - Watch for the slide into rote: vary nothing in the act, but keep it slow and with the hand.

**Risks the research flags.** Rote automatism (habit without intent). Extrinsic motives, "doing it to be seen". And, from the ritual literature, the pull to explain the deed by its benefits, which would turn a mitzvah into a technique.

## Adverse effects and counterfeits

The sources already warn in both directions: against false fire, and against killing the fire by doubting every spark (`../manual/part4-inside.md`, "Real and counterfeit"). The research on meditation-related difficulty now says much the same, from the other side. This section lays the two side by side.

## What the research on meditation difficulties has found

- **They are not rare.** In a large survey of 1,232 regular meditators, 25.6% reported a particularly unpleasant experience they thought their practice may have caused (Schlosser et al., 2019). *Cross-sectional survey.* In a US population sample, 32% said yes to a general question about adverse effects and 50% endorsed at least one specific effect; 10.4% had one lasting a month or more; 10.6% reported some loss of functioning, lasting a month or more in 1.2% (Goldberg et al., 2022). *Population-based survey.* Across 83 studies with 6,703 people, the pooled rate was 8.3%, but 3.7% in experiments against 33.2% in observational studies (Farias et al., 2020). *Systematic review and meta-analysis.* The rate depends heavily on how carefully one asks.
- **What they look like.** Interviews with Western Buddhist practitioners and teachers yielded 59 kinds of meditation-related experience in 7 domains: cognitive, perceptual, affective, somatic, conative (will and motivation), sense of self, and social. The same experience could be welcomed by one person and distressing to another, depending on interpretation and support (Lindahl et al., 2017). *Qualitative with follow-up survey.* The most common adverse effects are anxiety, depression and cognitive anomalies (Farias et al., 2020), and in the US sample anxiety, re-experiencing of trauma and emotional sensitivity (Goldberg et al., 2022).
- **Even in standard programs.** After an 8-week mindfulness-based program (n = 96), 37% had some negative impact on functioning, and 6-14% had lasting bad effects, linked to signs of dysregulated arousal (hyperarousal and dissociation). The rate was similar to other psychological treatments (Britton et al., 2021). *Structured interview study.*
- **Who is more at risk.** Repetitive negative thinking, practising only deconstructive meditation (such as insight/vipassana), and having attended a retreat raised the odds; being religious and being female lowered them (Schlosser et al., 2019). Childhood adversity raised risk (Goldberg et al., 2022).
- **Psychosis is rare but documented**, usually in vulnerable people, sometimes as a relapse and sometimes as a brief first episode (Kuijpers et al., 2007). *Case report with literature review.*
- **Two more effects of practice on the self.** Practice can *raise* self-enhancement rather than quiet it (Gebauer et al., 2018), and spiritual superiority is a measurable form of narcissism (Vonk & Visser, 2021).
- **Spiritual bypassing**: using spiritual ideas to avoid psychological or relational work (Welwood, 1984; Fox et al., 2017; Picciotto et al., 2018).
- **Scrupulosity**: religious obsessions (fear of having sinned, fear of God's punishment) can be measured, and highly devout people score higher, though in the validation sample devout Jews had fewer such fears than devout Protestants or Catholics (Abramowitz et al., 2002). *Measure validation.*
- **The other error, over-pathologizing.** Psychiatry has tended to ignore or treat as illness the religious and spiritual side of life; a diagnostic category for religious or spiritual problems (including mystical experiences) was proposed to reduce harm from misdiagnosis (Lukoff, Lu & Turner, 1992). *Theory and proposal.* This is the research's version of the Piaseczner's "don't kill the fire".

**Caution on transfer.** Nearly all of this is about Buddhist-derived meditation: breath, body and open awareness, often in long retreats. Hisbonenus is short, conceptual, aimed at a line of teaching, done by religious people, and ends in a deed. By the research's own risk factors (deconstructive-only practice, retreats, not being religious), it is probably lower risk. That is our inference, not a finding. No study of hisbonenus has been done.

## What the research suggests the builders check

These are checks on the guide's design, for the builders to weigh. They do not change the owner's rules.

1. **Duration as a sign.** The research's clearest mark of harm is an effect that *lasts*: weeks of feeling unreal, of heightened arousal, of not sleeping. The manual's danger line names sleeplessness and a special mission but not lasting depersonalization. The builders may wish to consider whether "feeling unreal or detached for days after sitting, and it isn't passing" belongs on the danger line.
2. **Trauma.** Re-experiencing of trauma is one of the commonest adverse effects in the population data, and childhood adversity raises risk. The guide's rule, "no turning a hard time into material", already protects here. Keeping sittings short and ending in a deed, as the manual does, fits the research's picture of lower risk.
3. **Ask carefully, not leadingly.** Rates of difficulty rise sharply when people are asked about them in detail. The guide's "described, never demanded" stance is right. The research adds: do not suggest specific unusual experiences, and do not treat their absence as failure.
4. **Two-directional warnings are right.** The research, like the sources, finds both errors real: taking ordinary arousal or illness for depth, and taking genuine religious experience for illness. The manual's "when each fits" (the anxious doubter hears reassurance, the chaser of highs hears the Mitteler Rebbe's signs) matches the research's picture of different people at different risk.

## The strongest and weakest parallels in steps 8-14

**Strongest.** - *Step 10, "do not examine it while it happens"*, and the research on ironic monitoring and self-defeating happiness-watching. Different vocabularies, same mechanism: watching a deep state for signs lowers it. (Caveat: the direct happiness-monitoring experiment is small and preliminary.) - *Step 14, "a flame flying in the air"*, and the intention-behaviour gap and implementation intentions. Meta-analytic, well replicated, and it fits the manual's "one act, tied to a moment" closely. - *Step 12, "act as if you did not hear it"*, and the thought-suppression research, including Wegner's finding that a specific alternative thought reduces rebound. - *Steps 8, 9 and 13 counterfeits*, and spiritual bypassing. - *Step 11*, and theistic sanctification plus the Daily Spiritual Experience Scale.

**Weakest (look alike, differ at the root).** - *Step 10*: the yechidah as the power of the Essence in the soul. Hypo-egoic states, flow and attachment to God touch its edges; none holds the claim, and none should be stretched to. - *Step 9*: efes and yachid set beside ego dissolution and non-dual awareness. The research describes a change in experience; the sources describe Him, beyond any experience. - *Step 12*: is'hapcha, the dark itself made light. Redemption narratives and post-traumatic growth are stories told afterward; the research even warns that perceived growth often isn't real change. - *Step 14*: the reasonless will of the mitzvah set beside ritual and habit research, which explain ritual by its uses.
