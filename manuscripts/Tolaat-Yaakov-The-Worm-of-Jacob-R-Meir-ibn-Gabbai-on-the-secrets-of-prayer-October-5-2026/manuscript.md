## What the book is

A book on the secrets of prayer and of the mitzvot of the day, "illuminating the prayers of the whole year in four ways: plain meaning, hint, homily and secret" (title page, p. 1). Ibn Gabbai finished it on 12 Nisan 5267 (spring 1507), and says at the end that he wrote it "while I was twenty six" (p. 95). It is his first book, written about a quarter century before Avodat HaKodesh, which names it (Avodat HaKodesh line 2200: the mitzvot are "a need on high ... as I explained in the book Tola'at Yaakov"). Derekh Emunah names it too (¶70, ¶81).

He explains the title from the Midrash: "Why is Israel likened to a worm? A worm's strength is only in its mouth; so Israel's strength is only in their mouths", in prayer (p. 9).

1. **The weekday** (pp. 10-45): rising at midnight, washing, the morning blessings, tzitzit and tefillin, the synagogue, the verses of song, Kaddish, the Shema and its blessings, the Amidah, falling on the face, Aleinu, study, the afternoon and evening prayers, the bedtime Shema.
2. **Shabbat and the festivals** (pp. 45-84): Shabbat eve and day, the three meals, Havdalah, the New Moon, Passover, the Omer, Shavuot, Rosh HaShanah and the shofar, fasting, Yom Kippur, Sukkot, the four species, Shemini Atzeret.
3. **Blessings** (pp. 85-95): blessings on food, grace after meals, blessings on fragrance, circumcision, and the wedding blessings.

It opens with a long preface on faith and on why prayer works (pp. 4-10).

## The unity of G-d

**The light so bright it is darkness.** The preface opens with the hidden Name: "He is His Name; it is the great light that no host above or below can contain, from the greatness of its brightness ... and it is like darkness in relation to Him, 'He made darkness His hiding place'" (p. 4, our reading). *Ours.* This matches the Chabad teaching that the light is concealed not by lack but by excess (Tanya ch. 48, on the light too great to be received).

**Why the world was made.** "Before the emanation, the world of the Holy One was the primordial light, alone ... and since there is no recognition of His glory without the will", He emanated Wisdom and Understanding, down to "the end of the upper Thought, which went deep to make, and found a vessel to pour into the lower world, for the existence of beings, through the complete man who joins the creatures" (p. 4). This is the same account as Avodat HaKodesh line 2201 and Derekh Emunah ¶35, in the voice of a young man.

**Nothing is added to Him, and yet the bond needs service.** The preface says plainly that the Ein Sof gains nothing:

> ואולם לא יועיל לו דבר ולא יחסר לו כמו שלא יועיל המאכל והמשתה לנשמה העליונה ואולם ע"י המאכל והמשתה יתמיד החבור, וכן ע"י העבודות יתמיד השפע ויתפשט על התחתונים (p. 10, our reading of the scan)
> Yet nothing benefits Him and nothing is lacking to Him, just as food and drink do not benefit the upper soul; yet through food and drink the bond continues, and so through the services the flow continues and spreads over the lower ones.

*Ours.* This is his clearest line on tzorech gavoah (the need on high), and it answers the obvious objection. The Infinite gains nothing. What the service keeps alive is the bond, the way eating keeps soul and body together. Derekh Emunah (¶28) repeats the same image twenty years later.

**The Shema: gather the seventy branches into the root.** The Shema is "the root of faith" (p. 27). "Shema" means to gather, "not to cut between One and One, from the Ein Sof" (p. 27). And the word שמע is שם ע: the seventy branches. "When Israel intend the unity below and unite all the lights included in this verse ... then all seventy branches are united in the root" (p. 27). The third Name in the verse holds the six directions, "and all is one ... one knot from the beginning of the Thought to the tzaddik who ties the end of the Thought to its beginning" (p. 28).

**Every limb crowns the King.** The Shema has 248 words, like the 248 limbs (pp. 29-30). So in the Shema a person must search himself, "to see whether a man makes his Maker king over him, without any mixture of other thoughts" (p. 30). He quotes the Zohar: "One who wants to unite the Holy One with His Shechinah must strip off from himself all thoughts, which are shells" (p. 30). And:

> ומי שממליך את המקום ב"ה על אבריו כאלו ממליכו על כל העולם (p. 30, our reading of the scan)
> One who makes the Omnipresent king over his limbs, it is as if he made Him king over the whole world.

*Ours.* This is very close to the Tanya's reading of the Shema as bittul and of the limbs as a chariot (Tanya ch. 23, 41). The guide can use it as it stands.

**The flame's colors come from the wick.** The Rosh HaShanah section says the three sections of the Musaf are "one thing, no multiplicity ... like a flame tied to a coal" (p. 73). This is the teaching Derekh Emunah expands (¶101-102).

## Tzorech gavoah: the need on high

This book is where ibn Gabbai first makes the claim that runs through all his later work. In the section on tzitzit:

> וגם תצטרך לדעת שאין המצות צורך הדיוט בלבד אבל צורך גבוה הם ג"כ, וזה מצינו לחכמי האמת באמרם שכינה בתחתונים צורך גבוה (p. 14, our reading of the scan)
> And you must also know that the mitzvot are not only a common need, but a need on high as well; and so we find the sages of truth saying: the Shechinah among the lower ones is a need on high.

He meets the Midrash head on: "What does the Holy One care whether one slaughters from the neck or the back? The mitzvot were given only to refine the creatures" (Bereishit Rabbah 44:1). He answers that the need is not for the One Lord Himself but "for the need of the Glory, to pour onto the upper heads ... and this is an upper need, a complete need, and this is the secret of 'to refine the creatures': the joining and binding of the lower ones to the upper" (p. 14). "One who keeps one mitzvah adds strength and completes the spiritual thing" (p. 14), and "whoever keeps My mitzvot, I count it as if he made Me" (p. 14, from the Midrash).

**The purpose of man, in the words of the morning prayer.** On "Blessed is our G-d who created us for His glory", and the Zohar on "everyone called by My Name, for My glory I created him" (Isaiah 43:7):

> טעם ביאת האדם לזה העולם והכוונה בבריאתו ... שתקון הכבוד הזה ושלמותו תלוי בתקון בני אדם החסידים (p. 41, our reading of the scan)
> The reason man comes into this world and the purpose of his creation ... is that the repair of this Glory and its completion depend on the repair of pious human beings.

"This is the purpose of creation, for this is the whole of man" (p. 41). And the right intention follows from it: "we must intend nothing but to do His will, which is the repair of the Glory ... and not mix in other things", "not to intend any benefit or reward in it, but our intent in all our deeds is to repair the Glory" (p. 42).

*Ours.* This is the whole later system in seed: Avodat HaKodesh line 2209 ("a need on high, nothing else") and Chabad's avodah lishmah as serving for His sake alone (Tanya ch. 41, "for the sake of the Shechinah"). The Tanya, like ibn Gabbai here, asks the servant to serve not for reward but so that G-d's will be done.

**"There is a servant who gives peace to his master."** Prayer strengthens the upper powers: "When the righteous unites the holy lights, he is the strength of G-d", "the Holy One rides the heavens only with the help of Israel" (p. 8; again p. 40). "As one arouses below, so one arouses above" (p. 8). *Ours.* This is itaruta diltata (arousal from below) in its plainest form.

## The power of thought and the inner work of prayer

**Thought reaches its source.** In the section on the first request of the Amidah, "You grant knowledge", ibn Gabbai gives a teaching on how intention works:

> ידוע לבעלי החכמה האמיתית כי מחשבת האדם היא ממקור הנפש החכמה כי נמשכה משם ויש בה כח לעלות ולהשיג עד מקום מוצאה ובהגיעה עד המקור אז היא נדבקת באור העליון שנמשכה ממנו ונעשות היא והוא דבר אחד (p. 36, our reading of the scan)
> It is known to the masters of true wisdom that a person's thought comes from the source of the wise soul, drawn from there, and it has power to rise and reach its place of origin; and when it reaches the source, it cleaves to the upper light from which it was drawn, and it and that light become one thing.

Then the thought returns downward, "and all becomes like one line", and the light spreads to the place where the thinker sits (p. 36). This is how the early pious drew blessing, as with Elisha's jug of oil (p. 36). And the first thing we ask for is knowing, "to show that his longing is for his G-d, to know His ways" (p. 36).

*Ours.* This is one of the most useful passages for the guide. It describes a movement of the mind: up to its source, one with it, then back down, bringing light into the place where one sits. It is the ratzo vashov (running and returning) of Chassidus and of Avodat HaKodesh (line 2001), described as a practice of thought. We propose it below.

**The mind must be empty of other thoughts.** "One who prays must clear his heart of all thoughts; you already know that thought can disqualify" (p. 32). Stray thoughts are like "nakedness" before the King, and a prayer mixed with them "does not enter" (p. 32). The one who prays should see himself "as if he were in the place of the priest standing to serve" (p. 32).

**Voice and breath carry the prayer.** Thought alone is not enough; the lips must move (p. 34). From the Zohar: the words of prayer "split the air and rise" to the King (p. 34). Prayer is "thought, will, voice and word; these four are knots" (p. 34).

**Bow at "Blessed", stand at the Name.** One bows at "Blessed" to draw down, and straightens at the Name "to unite her with her beloved" (p. 34).

**Giving the soul in the falling on the face.** In tachanun, "one must intend to hand over his soul, that he gives it into the hand of the Holy One with complete love, ... as if He took his soul from him and he died" (p. 39). One who gives his soul this way "merits that his soul be bound in the bond of life" (p. 40). *Ours.* This is mesirut nefesh (giving over the soul) in prayer, which Chabad places at "One" in the Shema and in tachanun (Tanya ch. 25, 41, as we recall it). The guide already holds this; ibn Gabbai adds that it is done "with complete love", not with fear.

**Pray as mercy, not as a fixed duty.** "Do not make your prayer fixed, but mercy and supplication" (p. 40), and "we must not hang our request on our merit, but on His great goodness" (p. 40).

## The day as a life of unity

**Midnight.** The book begins at night. At midnight the Holy One "has no rest until He enters the garden to delight in the souls of the righteous"; one who studies Torah at night draws "a thread of kindness" by day (p. 11). David rose at midnight; he "slept only like a horse" (p. 11).

**Washing hands opens the fountain.** Lifting the hands "opens the fountain to draw the light from the source of all" (p. 12).

**The body is the upper man.** On "who formed man with wisdom": man has 248 limbs like the upper man, "and see how deep the thoughts of our Maker went, to include all the worlds in man" (p. 13).

**Tefillin is accepting the kingdom.** Tefillin mean "accepting the yoke of the kingdom of heaven ... as a servant is bound to his master" (p. 17).

**A hundred blessings.** One who blesses opens the fountain; one who does not "separates between the powers" (p. 19). (Derekh Emunah ¶167 takes this up.)

**Eating as service.** In Part Three: one who enjoys this world without a blessing robs G-d and Knesset Yisrael (p. 85). "The upper desire and will is that they bless His great Name for all He formed" (p. 85). When one eats, "he should intend that his body be healthy so that the soul can do its work through it; and so his soul will be bound above" (p. 86). Torah at the table is like eating "from the radiance of the Shechinah" (p. 86), and feeding the poor at one's table atones like the altar (p. 87).

**Charity opens the fountain.** On the guests of the sukkah: their portion belongs to the poor (p. 80). "The rich man who gives charity to the poor below causes the upper Rich One to be aroused" (p. 81), and "the poor are the vessels of the Holy One" (p. 81).

**Shabbat is the bride.** Shabbat's partner is Knesset Yisrael (p. 46). One should welcome it with joy, and Vayechulu makes one "a partner of the Holy One in the work of creation" (p. 49). Joy on Shabbat is itself service (pp. 47, 50).

**The shofar: the lower voice wakes the upper.** The shofar-blower "must know the reason of the thing ... for he is the go-between for Israel and their Father in heaven" (p. 70). "How can the lower voice arouse the upper? Know that the lower level has nothing of her own, and receives only through the arousal of the lower ones" (p. 71).

**Return is older than the world.** "When a person returns in teshuvah he repairs what he twisted"; and "were it not for this repair, the world would not stand" (p. 75).

## States of consciousness

- **Thought returned to its source** (p. 36). Proposed below.
- **Stripped of other thoughts** (p. 30, p. 32). Ties to the index's states of bittul before prayer.
- **The King crowned in every limb** (p. 30). Ties to the chariot state (Tanya ch. 23).
- **Giving the soul in love** (pp. 39-40). Ties to the mesirut nefesh at Echad state already in the index.
- **Serving for the Glory, not for reward** (p. 42). Ties to lishmah states (Tanya ch. 41).

## Meditation and practice

- **Thought up to its source and back** (p. 36). Proposed below.
- **Crowning Him over each limb in the Shema** (p. 30). Proposed below.
- **Eating so the soul can work** (p. 86). Proposed below.
- **Rising at midnight** (p. 11). Already in the Chabad practice of tikkun chatzot; the guide keeps it as an option, not a rule.

## The big human questions

**Why was I made?** "The repair of this Glory and its completion depend on the repair of pious human beings" (p. 41).

**Does G-d need my prayer?** Nothing is added to Him; but as food keeps the soul in the body, service keeps the bond alive (p. 10).

**Why does my mind wander in prayer?** Because stray thoughts are shells; strip them off and make Him king over every limb (p. 30).

**How can my small voice matter?** The lower level "receives only through the arousal of the lower ones" (p. 71).

**What if I fall short?** Pray as mercy, not on your merit (p. 40). And ibn Gabbai himself ends by asking forgiveness for any errors, "for I am young and without understanding; I wrote it when I was twenty six" (p. 95).
