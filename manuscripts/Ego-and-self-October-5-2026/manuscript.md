<a id="c29"></a> ## C29 · I'm fine; I don't need to change

*7 findings from Sha'arei Teshuvah, Imrei Binah, Kuntres HaAvodah, Kuntres HaTefillah, Kuntres Umaayan, Sefer HaSichos (part 2 of 2), Sha'arei Avodah*

> All is well. I avoid serious sins, I feel nothing missing, and nothing holds me back. If anyone is sick, it's the others. I don't notice that my heart is somewhere else entirely.

**What is really going on.** The library reads 'I'm fine' as blindness, not health. Sha'arei Teshuvah and Sha'arei Avodah: without knowledge (daas) a person cannot tell light from dark and imagines himself whole; adding knowledge brings feeling, and feeling brings return. Imrei Binah: one who has not seen the light takes darkness for light, so a heart split between prayer and craving feels no contradiction. Kuntres Umaayan: 'even if I do it, nothing is broken' is a lie that hides the separation he could never accept. Kuntres HaTefillah: being 'whole in yourself' keeps everything outside; a broken heart is the vessel. Kuntres HaAvodah: feeling no Master leaves the senses open to everything. Sefer HaSichos (Rayatz): the gravest illness of spirit is fantasy, sure it is healthy, left alone by all. The remedy is mostly light, not accusation; once the dark is seen, turning follows.

**The false conclusions, and what the works set against them**

- *"I keep away from serious sins, so I'm fine."* → Without light he doesn't feel he is in darkness and takes darkness for light; his mouth is for God in prayer while all day his heart follows its desires, and he sees no contradiction. `IB·Q24` · `IB·Q25` · `IB·Q29`
- *"Even if I do it, I'm still connected; nothing's broken."* → That is the inclination's persuasion. The act does separate, and that separation is what he could never accept. `KU·Q05`
- *"I understand it and enjoy it, so I have it."* → Understanding that doesn't give birth to love and awe is mere thought. Someone whole in himself stays external; what he hears never enters. `SA·Q04` · `KT·Q23` · `KT·Q24`
- *"Nothing's wrong with me; the others are the sick ones."* → There are sick people who not only don't know it but are sure they are healthy; the core illness of spirit is fantasy, and it takes root because no one worries for them. `SHS2·Q05` · `SHS2·Q06` · `SHS2·Q07`
- *"Being free means doing whatever I feel like."* → The open senses come from feeling no Master. Restraint is heavy only at first, and then brings rest from confusion. `KA·Q03` · `KA·Q01` · `KA·Q02`

**Where the works agree.** All read complacency as not seeing, not as health. Most agree the cure is light rather than accusation: knowledge and contemplation reveal the gap, and turning follows (Sha'arei Teshuvah, Sha'arei Avodah, Imrei Binah). Several insist that what is seen must reach feeling and conduct, and Sha'arei Teshuvah that seeing the gap must lead at once to joy.

**Where they differ.** Kuntres Umaayan tells one whose soul does not shine to stop reasoning and act the opposite by stubbornness, since intellect limits will; Sha'arei Teshuvah prescribes adding knowledge. Kuntres HaAvodah puts accepting the yoke before contemplation. Kuntres HaTefillah calls for long accountings, fasts and rebuke; Sha'arei Teshuvah warns against lengthening bitterness at joy's cost. The Rayatz sends him to an outside physician.

**Order and timing.** Begin with light: contemplate greatness until felt, and let it show the gap. Keep bitterness brief and move straight into joy. Take on the yoke and guard the senses daily. Where reasoning keeps losing in one area, act the opposite without debate. Long accountings only rarely, and with a guide.

- This medicine is built to create a sense of lack. Depressed, anxious, ashamed and scrupulous people are not complacent, and they will over-apply it. - 'Seeing oneself as truly bad', the harlot imagery (Imrei Binah), fasts, mortification and rebuke (Kuntres HaTefillah) can do real harm to someone fragile. - 'Whatever he wants by nature, he will not do' (Kuntres HaAvodah) is not a rule for self-punishing people or those with eating disorders. - A guide must never diagnose another person as complacent or as living in fantasy; the texts ask for self-examination. - Holy stubbornness is not treatment for addiction or compulsion, and 'do the opposite' can become a ritual for someone with OCD. - Hard-won calm after trauma or illness, and healthy self-acceptance, are not this condition.

**Truths for the guide** *(candidates, each checked against its passages; see CHECKS.md)*

- **You can't see the dark without light** · Feeling fine can mean you are fine, or that you haven't seen clearly yet. Gaps show only as the light grows, and the more you see, the smaller the things you notice. *Check: yes.*
- **Stop negotiating with the pull** · When you keep losing the argument with an urge, stop arguing. A decision that stands below reasons can hold where reasons keep giving way. *Check: yes.*
- **Nothing gets in if you're already complete** · If you feel whole in yourself, what you hear stays outside you. Some opening, some sense of lack, is what lets anything enter and change you. *Check: yes.*

**What the core works do not cover.** No way to tell complacency from healthy contentment or hard-won peace, and no guidance for approaching a complacent person from outside. Grandiosity that may be clinical goes unaddressed. KA:P02 (unguarded senses) belongs with desire and restraint; KU:P03 (rationalizing a sin) with self-deception in temptation.

<a id="c37"></a> ## C37 · I feel above others and entitled to more

*7 findings from Tanya, Torah Ohr (part 2 of 2), Likkutei Torah (part 2 of 3), Sha'arei Orah, Toras Chaim (part 2 of 4), Toras Chaim (part 4 of 4), Hayom Yom*

> When things go well I feel above everyone and assume it will last. My wants matter more than God's. I know I'm right. Nothing is ever enough; I always feel I deserve more.

**What is really going on.** The works find one root under pride, entitlement, craving and anger: felt selfhood, counting oneself a 'something'. Toras Chaim says every bad desire and every sense of being owed grows from it, and a person is content with his lot only when it softens. Sha'arei Orah traces lust and anger to the moment the self first 'felt itself', but insists on some holy pride too, or 'who am I' stops all service. Torah Ohr calls coarseness of spirit the wall between a person and his Father. It says arrogant thoughts come from outside, from 'Haman', as garments on the spark, to be wept over rather than identified with. Tanya says every kindness received should make one lower, and that one has never stood in another's place. Likkutei Torah (Nitzavim) says no one is complete without his fellow. Hayom Yom says every 'I say, I hold' divides hearts. Toras Chaim (Tetzaveh) says a high will not last: after a rise comes a descent.

**The false conclusions, and what the works set against them**

- *"I was blessed and succeeded, so I'm someone."* → Every kindness should make a person lower in spirit. The nearer to God, the more one is as nothing. `TAN·Q25`
- *"I'm obviously better than him; I don't need him."* → You have not reached his place: his livelihood and what his eyes face every day. You have no completeness without your fellow. `TAN·Q12` · `LT2·Q29`
- *"I'm on top, and it will stay this way."* → This is a world of change. Do not imagine success lasts all your days; after a rise comes a descent. Trust belongs to God alone. `TC4·Q07` · `TC4·Q08`
- *"I deserve more; nothing is enough."* → The root of bad desires is counting oneself a something, a great thing. The surplus craving 'only for himself' is the covering over the heart. `TC2·Q26` · `TC2·Q15`
- *"This is what I say; this is my position."* → Every 'I' and every sense of self is a source of bad and divides hearts. The strong-minded must leave his thoughts. `HY·Q30`
- *"These arrogant, defiant thoughts are who I am."* → Such thoughts come to a person from outside, like garments on the divine spark. The love is certainly hidden beneath them. `TO2·Q24` · `TO2·Q25` · `TO2·Q19`

1. **Lowliness by exactness** · *from Tanya, Likkutei Torah* (1) When blessed or saved, say: 'I am too small for all the kindnesses.' Each kindness brings nearness, and nearness asks for more lowliness. (2) Before judging someone, ask about his place: his livelihood, what his eyes see all day, his temperament. (3) Ask whether you have ever fought a war as hard as his. (4) Remember that he completes something lacking in you. (5) Refuse to lord it over others or speak against them. Answer softly. *When:* At the moment of swelling or judging, and at a fixed hour of reckoning. *For:* The successful, the learned, the habitual judge. *Not for:* Not a mood of worthlessness; Tanya rejects that as sadness. Never used to excuse harmful conduct by others. `TAN·Q25` · `TAN·Q12` · `LT2·Q29`
2. **Hold steady through rises and falls** · *from Toras Chaim* (1) When you rise, don't be carried away or imagine it will last. (2) When you fall, remember this is a world of change and ascent follows descent. Don't say 'it's hopeless'. (3) Trust God alone, not the high or the low. *When:* Whenever a high or a fall comes, spiritual or material. *For:* People inflated by success, or battered by repeated setbacks. *Not for:* Not a substitute for practical action or help during real crises. `TC4·Q07` · `TC4·Q05` · `TC4·Q06` · `TC4·Q08`
3. **Soften the root: sanctify the permitted and lower the self** · *from Toras Chaim, Sha'arei Orah* (1) Name the covering: craving surplus pleasure for yourself, even permitted pleasure. (2) Hold back a little even in what is permitted, so the heart is not pulled so strongly. (3) Let the heart break over its distance from God. (4) Humble yourself; don't wait for others or events to do it. (5) Let self-nullification, drawn from understanding, reach the heart until the craving falls away. *When:* An ongoing stance, deepened in the bitterness before prayer. *For:* A basically stable person whose hardness comes from comfort and self-focus. *Not for:* Anyone already crushed, shamed, depressed or abused. For them, 'lowering oneself to dust' can deepen the harm. `TC2·Q15` · `TC2·Q16` · `TC2·Q26` · `SO·Q04` · `SO·Q10`
4. **Esther's way: separate yourself from the arrogant thoughts** · *from Torah Ohr* (1) Know the love is certainly there, hidden in the inner point of your heart. (2) Name the thorns that hide it: worldly things done with all your heart and soul. (3) Treat arrogant, defiant thoughts as a garment on the spark, not as your self. (4) Plead for mercy on the spark clothed in those garments. (5) When your mind is empty, cry out from the distress itself, and hold to faith as if you saw. *When:* In prayer. *For:* Someone caught in arrogant or defiant thoughts, or who feels no love at all. *Not for:* Not a way to excuse the thoughts. The work still asks for weeping over them and taking on the yoke. `TO2·Q19` · `TO2·Q20` · `TO2·Q21` · `TO2·Q24` · `TO2·Q22` · `TO2·Q23`
5. **Eyes down, heart up: humility balanced with holy pride** · *from Sha'arei Orah* (1) Keep the mind humble. (2) Let the heart rise in fire and longing, or 'who am I' will stop all service. (3) Afterwards, temper that lift through Torah study, which quiets the self. (4) Keep only a small portion of self-feeling; more becomes real arrogance. *When:* Fervor in prayer, then Torah study after it; daily. *For:* Both the paralyzed-humble and the self-inflated, each at a different step. *Not for:* Do not stress the humility steps to someone already crushed. `SO·Q25` · `SO·Q28` · `SO·Q26` · `SO·Q27`

- From *"I was saved and blessed, so I'm someone."* to **Every kindness from God should make you lower. The nearer you are, the more you are as nothing.** Nearness is defined as self-nullification, so a blessing truly received produces humility, and pride shows the kindness went astray. *(Tanya)* `TAN·Q25` - From *"I'm obviously better than him."* to **You have not reached his place. Seeing that steadies the person in both directions. *(Toras Chaim)* `TC4·Q07` · `TC4·Q06` · `TC4·Q08` - From *"I deserve it; I need a lot."* to **The sense of being owed comes from feeling oneself a 'something'. As that softens, contentment comes.** Treating the root, self-importance, rather than each desire lets the desires fall away together. *(Toras Chaim)* `TC2·Q26` - From *"This is how I say it; this is my position."* to **Every 'I' and self-assertion is a root of bad and divides hearts.** It shows attachment to one's own view as the source of separation, which lets the person loosen it for the sake of connection. *(Hayom Yom)* `HY·Q30` - From *"These arrogant, defiant thoughts are who I am."* to **They are garments over the divine spark, to be pitied and prayed over, not identified with.** Separating the self from the thoughts lets the person have compassion on his own spark instead of identifying with the thoughts or despairing. *(Torah Ohr)* `TO2·Q24` · `TO2·Q25` - From *"A broken heart means I must break myself."* to **What breaks is the wall of coarseness between you and your Father, not you. **Where the works agree.** One root lies under pride, entitlement, craving and anger: an over-strong sense of self. Real nearness makes a person lower, not higher. The remedy goes to the root rather than chasing each branch, and it ends in contentment and connection with others, not in self-contempt.

**Where they differ.** Toras Chaim (vol. 2) and Torah Ohr ask for active self-lowering 'to dust'. Sha'arei Orah insists on some holy pride, lest the heart be paralyzed. Tanya rejects worthlessness as sadness and works by exactness: 'his place'. Torah Ohr puts arrogant thoughts outside the self. Hayom Yom and Likkutei Torah work through relationship: leave your opinion; you need your fellow.

**Order and timing.** At the moment of success: 'too small for the kindnesses', and remember that highs change. At the moment of judging: 'his place' and 'he completes me'. In prayer: separate from the arrogant thoughts, and let the heart rise. After prayer: Torah study to quiet the self. Over time: soften the root; keep only a small portion of self-feeling.

- 'Lower yourself to dust' and 'break the heart' can deepen harm in anyone depressed, shamed, abused or already crushed. Sha'arei Orah itself says not to stress humility to the crushed. - Reading suffering as a cure for pride (Toras Chaim, Torah Ohr) must not be offered to someone who is suffering. - 'Leave your opinion' must not silence a person whose voice is already suppressed, or someone who needs to set boundaries. - Humility here never means worthlessness; Tanya calls that sadness.

<a id="c07"></a> ## C07 · After a spiritual high I crash into pride, anger or craving

*6 findings from Likkutei Torah (part 1 of 3), Toras Chaim (part 1 of 4), Toras Chaim (part 2 of 4), Toras Chaim (part 3 of 4), Pelach HaRimon*

> Right after an intense prayer I snap at people, feel superior, or get pulled into desire. Sometimes the high turns giddy and then empty. My fervor seems to feed the very things I'm fighting.

**What is really going on.** The works locate the crash in what is missing after the fervor, not in the fervor itself. Likkutei Torah: fervor in prayer with no Torah study after it gives rise to irritability, anger and ego. Toras Chaim and Pelach HaRimon: 'running' with no 'returning' into self-lowliness falls straight into coarseness, pride and hot desire. Toras Chaim (Bereishis) goes further. A felt sense of self stood out inside the fervor, and when the flame dies its residue becomes anger at opponents and self-righteous zeal, which he mistakes for holiness. Craving after prayer comes because the natural soul was excited too, and the heart was never broken. Toras Chaim also says joy bigger than the contemplation behind it is just the heart opening up, and it drains into frivolity and emptiness. Pelach HaRimon adds that emotion felt as self-feeling throws off waste, and a quieter contemplation of transcendence does not.

**The false conclusions, and what the works set against them**

- *"My fervor shows I'm closer to God than others, so my anger at them is holy zeal."* → That sense of having arrived beyond others is the root of the fall. Anger after prayer is the residue of self-feeling, a physical foreign fire that only seems holy. `TC1·Q19` · `TC1·Q20` · `TC1·Q21` · `PR·Q28`

On beginners: Sha'ar HaYichud has them start broad, while Kuntres HaTefillah warns that beginners fool themselves in long contemplation. On tone: the Mitteler Rebbe's diagnosis is severe ('not serving God at all'), while Sha'arei Avodah and Likkutei Sichos protect the self from collapse and keep the light.

**Order and timing.** First diagnose by the signs, gently. Then ground devotion in detailed contemplation with one intention, in daily prayer. Inside prayer, humility before awe, and awe before zeal. Where feeling is absent or has turned to self, carry the yoke in plain deed with joy. Keep the two-sided view (outside nothing, inside a portion of God) all day. After success, shrink the self-feeling and keep the light.

- The self-suspicion tests can become endless checking for someone scrupulous, anxious or with OCD; give them only with a guide. - The 'test it with insult' sign can wound someone with trauma or abuse history, whose pain at insult comes from injury, not pride. - 'You are no entity' or 'you cannot reach heights' must never be given alone to someone depressed or with low self-worth; always pair it with the inner view. - Do not tell a grieving or distressed person that their tears are 'only bodily heat'. - Never use these signs to judge another person's devotion; the text itself forbids thinking ill of or envying others. - Do not strip a beginner of the joy of first feeling; the tests are for those long in the path.

<a id="c39"></a> ## C39 · Who am I to do this? Too small, too simple, too ordinary

*12 findings from Torah Ohr (part 2 of 2), Imrei Binah, Sha'arei Orah, Kuntres HaTefillah, Kuntres Umaayan, Sefer HaSichos (part 1 of 2), Sefer HaSichos (part 2 of 2), Hayom Yom, Inyanah Shel Toras HaChassidus, Likkutei Sichos (part 1 of 3), Sha'arei Avodah*

> Real inner work is for special souls, not for someone busy, simple and weak like me. I'm ashamed I don't even understand what I pray. Being humble means being nothing. Starting feels beyond me.

**What is really going on.** The works agree that 'who am I' is usually a false humility. Imrei Binah and Kuntres HaTefillah call it the inclination's drop of bitterness. Each person, even one who can only read a book, can work at his own measure, and effort of the mind is within anyone's control. The Rebbe Rayatz notes that no one asks 'who am I' when counting profits. Torah Ohr, Sha'ar Orah and Sha'arei Avodah add a psychology: service needs a lifting of the heart, and a beginner's sense of self is the husk that guards the seed. From outside a person is nothing; from inside his portion is God. Likkutei Sichos: self-effacement is a doorway to joy, not a doormat. Inyanah and Hayom Yom: Godliness is explained through one's own soul, so an ordinary mind can grasp it, and the weak person holds every virtue in hidden form. Kuntres Umaayan honours the simple man's prayer. Sha'ar Orah: every beginning is hard and needs encouragement beyond the usual.

**The false conclusions, and what the works set against them**

**Where the works agree.** All reject humility that paralyses. The work is within reach of an ordinary, busy, simple person at his own measure. Starting small is legitimate. What is needed is already in the soul. Humility of mind has to be joined by a lifted heart and ends in joy, not erasure.

**Where they differ.** Imrei Binah and Kuntres HaTefillah frame 'who am I' morally, as the inclination's trick. Sha'ar Orah and Hayom Yom treat it gently, as smallness that needs encouragement and patience. Torah Ohr allows ego as a temporary husk; Sha'ar Orah keeps a small measure permanently. The bar varies from an hour or two daily (Rashab) to a quarter hour and a verse between customers (Rayatz). Kuntres Umaayan prizes simple faith over understanding; the others insist on contemplation by capacity.

**Order and timing.** Begin with encouragement and a lifted heart, not with lowering. Set a small fixed time with simple contemplation, and carry a verse into the working day. Dig patiently, retrying after failure. Only once there is footing, practise self-effacement as a doorway to joy, keeping the inner view alongside the outer.

- In depression, 'who am I' and 'I can't start' may be symptoms; calling them 'the inclination's trick' can deepen shame. Lead with encouragement and lower the bar. - Do not prescribe an hour or two a day to an exhausted caregiver or someone in crisis; start from minutes. - Self-effacement teaching must not reach someone in an abusive situation as 'be a doormat'; the text itself forbids that reading. - The 'ego as protective husk' teaching can license pride in someone already inflated (see C08); give it to the frozen, not the puffed-up. - Do not use Hayom Yom's patient-digging image to imply that someone with a disability or illness just isn't trying.
