This part carries two short books of the Tanya. The first is *Shaar HaYichud VehaEmunah*, the Gate of Unity and Faith, which opens with a small introduction called *Chinuch Katan*, "a small education." It takes the world in front of you, the sky, the table, a stone, and shows it being spoken right now by the word that made it. Then it walks the mind up to its own wall, where only faith goes on, and back down again to the stone. The second is *Iggeret HaTeshuvah*, the Letter on Return. It turns the same truth on a person who has done wrong. It starts with the plain law of fasting and forgiveness and then goes to the root: the soul is breath blown from His inside, a rope tied above that nothing in the world can block. Only acts against His will fray it, and return ties it again, stronger at the knot. Each section follows the same shape: the chapter retold in plain words, the unity in it, a short sitting, and the Hebrew line it rests on. The line numbers point to the Sefaria-Kehot Hebrew text, and every one was checked. Chapter divisions follow the Sefaria text the Unity Index uses.

## Chinuch Katan · Two loves, and the ground under both

It starts with a proverb: "Train a child in his way; even when he is old, he will not leave it." The Tanya pauses on the words "in *his* way." A child's way is not yet the whole truth. So what is so good about never leaving it?

First it lays the base. All service of G-d grows from two roots, awe and love. Awe is the root of turning from bad. Love is the root of doing good. And the Torah says something odd about love: "which I command you *to do*, to love G-d." How do you *do* a love? Love lives in the heart.

The answer: there are two loves. The first is the soul's own yearning for its Maker. When the mind wins over the body and the body bows, the soul catches fire. It is a flame that rises by itself, a delight in G-d beyond words. The people who reach it are called tzaddikim, the fully righteous. Not everyone gets there. It takes a very refined body, much Torah and good deeds, and a higher level of soul.

The second love is one that *anyone* can reach. You reach it by thinking, well and deeply, "in the depth of the heart," about the things that wake love up. The broadest one is this: He is our very life. You love your own life. Nobody has to teach you that. So when you take to heart that G-d is *your true soul and your very life*, the same love turns toward Him. Then there are the finer ways: thinking about His greatness, as far as your mind can carry it and past it. And thinking about His love for us, how He came down into Egypt, "the nakedness of the land," to pull our souls out of the "iron furnace" and bind us to His own Name. He raised us from the lowest place to the holiest. "As water shows a face back to a face," love answers love.

This second love is the one Moshe wanted to plant in every heart. That is why the Torah calls it something you *do*: it is a love made in the heart by understanding. The first love, the flame that rises by itself, can't be commanded at all. It is a gift, the reward of the righteous, a taste of the world to come.

Then the Tanya comes back to the child. "A righteous man falls seven times and gets up." A person is called a *walker*, not a *stander*. He is meant to climb from level to level. Between one level and the next, before he reaches the higher one, he is in a kind of "fall." But it is a fall only compared to where he was, not compared to other people. Something of the old level stays in him, a mark, an imprint. And mostly what stays is the love he was raised in and got used to as a child, before he ever became great. That is "even when he is old, he will not leave it."

And under everything that wakes love and awe, it puts one stone: pure, faithful belief in His oneness. That is what the rest of this book is about.

*The Tanya speaks here of the Jewish soul and its particular root; this adaptation applies it to every person.*

The life you already love was never yours alone. When you see that it is His, loving your life and loving Him turn out to be one love.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Whatever your heart feels toward G-d today, even nothing, let it be what it is. This love doesn't start from a feeling. - **The line, softly:** "He is my true soul and my very life." - **Think it through.** You want to live another day. You reach for bread without being told to. That wanting is love of life. Now follow it one step: the life inside you is His. The love doesn't need to be new. It needs to find its real address. - **Where the picture breaks.** "My life" sounds like something I own. It isn't. As far as you are alive, it is Him. - **From inside.** Not a blaze, a quiet warmth. A love made, like a meal, out of what was already in the house. - **The edge.** The flame that rises by itself can't be forced. Don't reach for it. This love is enough, and it is yours to make. - **Into the day.** Before you eat, say quietly: "The life I want from this bread is His life in me."

## Chapter 1 · The word is still being said

The chapter opens with a verse Jews say often: "Know today and bring it back to your heart, that G-d is the G-d in the heavens above and on the earth below; there is nothing else." The Tanya asks a plain question. Who would ever think there is some other god living in the water under the earth? Why does the Torah have to warn us so strongly, "bring it back to your heart"? It leaves that question hanging, and answers it five chapters later.

It starts instead with the Baal Shem Tov. The verse says, "Forever, G-d, Your word stands in the heavens." The Baal Shem Tov read it plainly. The words G-d said on the second day, "Let there be a firmament," did not stop. Those very words and letters still *stand* inside the sky. They are dressed inside it, and they keep it alive. If the letters left for one instant and went back to their source, the whole sky would not crumble or fall. It would simply be nothing, as if it had never been, exactly as before "Let there be a firmament."

The same is true of everything in every world, high and low. It is true even of this solid earth and of the most lifeless things. If the letters of the Ten Sayings that made the earth left it for an instant, it would go back to nothing, just as before the six days.

That is why the Arizal taught that even a stone, dust, or water has a soul, a spiritual life. Its soul is the letters of speech from the Ten Sayings, dressed inside it, keeping it in being out of nothing.

But the word "stone" isn't in the Ten Sayings. How does a stone get its life? The Tanya answers: the letters get rearranged. They are swapped and shuffled, level after level, until out of the Ten Sayings comes the combination *even*, the Hebrew word for stone. That word is the stone's life. The chapter explains this with the Sefer Yetzirah's 231 "gates" of letter-pairs; this retelling keeps only the idea. The same holds for every creature. Its name in the Holy Tongue is the very letters that come down to it and keep it alive.

Why so many steps down? Because a single small creature could never hold the full force of the Ten Sayings themselves. That life is far too great. So it comes down level by level, each one smaller, until it is small enough to become one particular thing. A thing's name is a vessel for the bit of life poured into it. And that life comes from the Torah's Ten Sayings, which have the power to make something from nothing and keep it forever, because "the Torah and the Holy One are all one."

The world is not a finished thing sitting next to G-d. It is being said by Him, this moment, and without the saying there would be nothing.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Let the room be as solid as it looks. Don't try to make it feel different. - **The line, softly:** "Your word stands in the heavens." - **Think it through.** The sky above you is being said. The table under your hands is being said. Say the name of one thing near you, slowly: cup, wall, stone. That name is the life of it, still coming. - **Where the picture breaks.** Our words leave our mouths and fade. His don't leave. They stay inside what they say. - **From inside.** The room feels less heavy and more near. Things stop looking like they stand on their own. - **The edge.** Where the word comes from, the chapter doesn't open yet. Stop at "without the word, nothing." - **Into the day.** Pick one object today, a spoon or a stone, and say its name slowly, as the word that keeps it here.

## Chapter 2 · Not the silversmith's cup

This chapter goes after a mistake. Some people deny that G-d watches over each thing, and doubt the wonders in the Torah. Their error comes from a picture. They picture G-d's work like a craftsman's work. A silversmith makes a cup. After that the cup doesn't need him. He takes his hands off it and goes to the market, and the cup stays exactly as he made it. That, they think, is how G-d made heaven and earth.

The Tanya says their eyes are sealed to a huge difference. The silversmith makes *something from something*. He only changes a shape, from a lump of silver to a cup. G-d makes heaven and earth *from nothing*.

Then it gives its own picture, the splitting of the sea. The Torah says G-d moved the sea "with a strong east wind all night," and the water stood up "like a wall." If G-d had stopped the wind for one moment, the water would have run back down, as water does. It would never have stood like a wall. Yes, even water's nature was created too. A wall of stones stands by itself, with no wind. Water's nature is not like that.

Now take the lesson much further. Making something from nothing is above nature. It is a far greater wonder than splitting the sea. So if the Creator's power left a creature, even for an instant, it would go back to being truly nothing. *Rather, the power of the Maker must be in the made, always, to give it life and keep it in being.* And that power is the letters of the Ten Sayings from the last chapter.

The sages read a line from the prayers, "You give life to them all," in a sharper way: "You *bring them into being*." Even the word "You" (*atah*) hints at it: its letters stretch from alef to tav, the whole alphabet, plus the letter hei for the five places in the mouth where speech is formed.

But G-d has no body. How can He "speak"? The Torah says it outright, again and again: "G-d spoke," "G-d said." It means the twenty-two letters above, revealed to the prophets and dressed in their minds, visions and words. As David said, "The spirit of G-d spoke in me; His word was on my tongue." Something like that is how the letters dress themselves inside created things: "By the word of G-d the heavens were made." Only, for creation, the letters come down through very many steps, all the way to physical things.

You were never left to keep yourself going. There is no moment the Maker steps back, because there is nothing to fall back on without Him.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Rest your hands on the table. Feel that they are there. That's all. - **The line, softly:** "The power of the Maker is in the made, always." - **Think it through.** Picture the smith's cup on the shelf after he's gone. Now picture the sea standing up in the night wind. Your hands are not the cup. They are the wall of water, held. - **Where the picture breaks.** Even the sea had its own nature to fall back into. You have none apart from Him. Not ruin, but nothing. - **From inside.** Held, with no gap in it. Humbling and safe at the same time. - **The edge.** Something from nothing can be said but not pictured. Let the mind say it and stop. - **Into the day.** When you set something down today and it stays, say: "Held, not left."

## Chapter 3 · Sunlight inside the sun

After all this, the Tanya says, anyone who thinks will see it plainly: every created thing is truly counted as nothing next to the power that is saying it into being.

So why does the world look so real to us? Only because our eyes of flesh can't see the power of G-d and the breath of His mouth inside each thing. *If the eye were allowed to see the life and spirit inside every creature,* flowing into it from G-d's mouth, *we would not see its physical body, its stuff, its weight at all.* It would vanish into the life inside it. Because without that life it would be nothing, as before the six days. So in truth there is nothing besides Him.

Then the picture: sunlight. Sunlight shines on the earth and fills the air. Everyone can see it. Now think. That same light is also inside the sun itself, up in the sky. If it can shine so far away, surely it shines right where it starts. But there, inside the sun, the light counts for nothing. It is swallowed up in the sun that makes it. It is only a glow of the sun. Down here, where the sun's body isn't, the light looks like a real thing and we call it "something." Inside the sun you couldn't call it "something" at all. Only the sun is there.

That is exactly how all creatures are next to G-d's flow that makes them. They are like light spreading out from Him. Next to their source they are nothing. We call them "something" only with our eyes of flesh, because we can't see the source at all. That is why the world looks so solid to us, the way sunlight looks solid when it's away from the sun.

And then the Tanya says honestly where its picture breaks. In the picture, the sun really isn't down here where the light seems real. But creatures are *always* inside their source. The source just isn't visible to eyes of flesh. So why don't creatures vanish into it? The chapter stops there: "To understand this, we must first explain." The answer comes next.

Seen from its source, the world is like sunlight inside the sun: there, but not a separate thing. It looks separate only from where we stand.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Look at whatever is in front of you. Let it be as real as it seems. - **The line, softly:** "In its source, it isn't even called light." - **Think it through.** Sunlight on a wall. Now picture that same light inside the sun, where it is lost in the fire it comes from. Now the wall itself: if you could see the word inside it, the paint and weight would fall away from sight, like a sunbeam folded back into the sun. - **Where the picture breaks.** The sun is far from the wall. He is never far from anything. So why does anything stand out at all? Hold the question. Don't answer it yet. - **From inside.** A soft loosening of the grip things have on your eyes. The world stays, but it looks borrowed. - **The edge.** The mind has found a real question. Let it stay open. - **Into the day.** When you see sunlight on a wall today, say: "In its source this isn't even called light."

## Chapter 4 · The sun and its shield

Here is the answer. A verse says, "G-d is a sun and a shield." The shield is like a sheath over the sun, so that creatures can stand it. The sages said that in the future G-d will take the sun out of its sheath. So the Name *Elokim* is a shield over the Name *Havayah*.

What do the Names mean? Havayah (the four-letter Name) means He *brings everything into being*, from nothing, all the time. Its first letter makes it a word of the ongoing present. It is the life flowing into every creature every single moment. It wasn't enough for things to be made during the six days. They need to be made now.

The prayers praise G-d as "the great, the mighty." "Great" is His kindness, *chesed*: life spreading out without end, making worlds and creatures from nothing, for free. It comes from His own greatness, and "it is the nature of the good to do good." No creature can do this. No creature can even understand it. Making something from nothing is above every created mind. And He and His traits are one, so just as no mind can grasp Him, no mind can grasp His traits.

"Mighty" is His *gevurah*, His strength. And His strength is in holding back. It keeps the life from showing openly. The life still comes, but it hides inside the thing. G-d's face is hidden, and so the creature seems to be *a thing by itself*, not rays of light from a sun. It isn't really a thing by itself. *But that is exactly G-d's might: He can do anything, even contract and hide the life flowing from His mouth, so that the creature is not dissolved out of being.* No created mind can grasp what this contraction and hiding are, just as none can grasp making something from nothing.

The chapter then adds a note in Kabbalah language. The holding back is called "vessels" and the life is called "light," because a vessel covers what's inside it. The vessels are the letters. It traces their roots to five "strengths" in the mouth that shape sound into twenty-two letters, and higher up to the very highest levels of might and kindness. This retelling keeps only the main point: the covering is His, all the way up.

The feeling "I stand by myself" is not proof that He is absent. It is His strength, hiding Him, so that you can be.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Notice how much you feel like your own person, separate and solid. Don't fight it. - **The line, softly:** "He hid so I could be." - **Think it through.** The sun, too bright to bear. Over it, a sheath, so we can live under it. Now you: you feel like a thing on your own. That feeling is the sheath. It is something He is doing. - **Where the picture breaks.** A sheath is separate from the sun. This hiding is His own act. Nothing outside Him does it. - **From inside.** Gratitude for being allowed to be a self. The distance stops being a wall and becomes a kindness. - **The edge.** How He hides Himself, no mind can grasp. Leave it with Him. - **Into the day.** When you catch yourself thinking "I did this all alone," add quietly: "He hid so I could."

## Chapter 5 · Seams of light

A short chapter. The sages said: "At first it rose in G-d's thought to create the world with strict justice. He saw the world could not last, and joined mercy to it." The Tanya reads this in light of the last chapter. Strict justice is the holding back, the hiding. A world made only of hiding could not last. So mercy was joined to it. What is that mercy? *Godliness revealed through tzaddikim, and through the signs and wonders in the Torah.* The hidden light breaks through here and there.

Then it quotes the Zohar: "Above, on the side of holiness, there is a right and a left." That is kindness and might. Both are G-d's own traits. Both are far above what creatures can grasp, because He and His traits are one. Even Moshe, the greatest prophet, didn't see them directly. He saw them only as they were dressed in lower worlds and in lower traits. (The chapter names these levels in Kabbalah terms; here it is enough to say that even Moshe saw them clothed.)

What the righteous get as their reward in Gan Eden, the world of souls, is to grasp a little of the light that spreads out from these two traits. That is the food of souls who learned Torah for its own sake. From kindness and might a sky is stretched over the souls. That sky is called "the secret of the Torah," and it holds the secret of the Torah's twenty-two letters. From that sky, dew drips down to feed the souls: knowing the secret of the letters. The Torah is the souls' food there, and the mitzvot are their clothes.

The hiding and the showing are not two powers. The One who covers the world is the One who opens seams in the covering so that it can last.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of your day as mostly covered, ordinary. That's allowed. It was made that way. - **The line, softly:** "He joined mercy to it." - **Think it through.** A heavy curtain over a window, with a few thin seams of light. A person who was simply and plainly good to you. A moment that fit too well to be chance. Those are seams. - **Where the picture breaks.** A curtain and the light are two things. Here the curtain and the light are the same One. - **From inside.** Watchfulness. Not hunting for miracles. Just noticing where the hidden shows through. - **The edge.** Kindness and might are above every mind, even Moshe's. Touch that, and let it be. - **Into the day.** Name one person today through whom something good showed that was more than them.

## Chapter 6 · The hiding is also kindness

The Name *Elokim* is the Name of might and holding back. In Hebrew numbers it adds up to the same as *hateva*, "nature." That is because it hides the light that makes and keeps the world, so the world looks like it stands and runs by itself, by nature. Elokim is the sheath over Havayah. It hides the light so that creatures won't dissolve.

And here is the turn. *This might and this holding back is itself also kindness,* because the world is built by it. It is might folded inside kindness. Without it you couldn't be here. So the hiding is a kind of love.

That the traits blend into each other shows that they are one with Him. Because they are all fully united with Him, they unite with each other. As Elijah said in the Zohar: "You are the One who binds them and unites them."

Now the chapter answers the question it asked back in chapter 1. "Know today and bring it back to your heart that *Havayah is Elokim*." The two Names are truly one. Even the Name that hides is kindness, like the Name that gives. His traits are His Names, and He and His Name are one.

So "in the heavens above and on the earth below, there is nothing else." Even the solid earth that everyone sees as fully real is truly nothing next to Him. Elokim hides Him only from those below, not from Him, since He and Elokim are one. So heaven and earth, before Him, are not even called by any name. Not even the word *od*, "also," which means an extra, something added on. The chapter says they aren't even that. (It gives a lovely line: "I will praise G-d with my life; I will sing to my G-d with my *od*." Life comes from Havayah; the *od*, the body that tags along, comes from Elokim.)

Why the warning, then? Because a person might think this way: heaven and earth are real separate things, and G-d fills the world like a soul fills a body. He puts the power of growth into the earth and moves the stars, the way a soul moves its body. The Tanya says this picture doesn't fit at all. A soul doesn't make its body. The body comes from the father and mother, and grows from the mother's food, and later from its own food. But heaven and earth came from nothing but His word, and His word still stands in them every moment. They are lost in Him like sunlight inside the sun. It is only His might that hides this, so they look like things by themselves. The hiding is only for those below. Before Him, "all counts as truly nothing."

The Name that hides and the Name that gives are one Name. Nature is not His absence. It is His kindness wearing a plain face.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Let the world run the way it seems to run, by rules and causes. Don't argue with it. - **The line, softly:** "Havayah is Elokim." - **Think it through.** Water boils. A door swings shut. The sun comes up. That is nature, the covering. Now say it: the covering is kindness. Without it you couldn't stand here at all. - **Where the picture breaks.** He is not in the world like a soul in a body. A soul doesn't make its body from nothing. He does, every moment. - **From inside.** Nature stops feeling cold. What looked like a machine looks like a covering laid down with care. - **The edge.** "Before Him, not even an 'also.'" You can say it. You can't stand where He stands. Stop there. - **Into the day.** When something works "by nature" today, a kettle, a sunrise, say: "Havayah is Elokim."

## Chapter 7 · As much down here as up there

This is the longest chapter. It explains a line of the Zohar: the first verse of the Shema, "Hear, Israel, G-d is One," is the *higher unity*. The line said after it, "Blessed be the Name of His glorious kingdom forever," is the *lower unity*.

Why did G-d hide at all, so that the world looks separate? Because the whole point of the world is for His *kingship* to be shown. And "there is no king without a people." The Hebrew word for people, *am*, is related to a word for things that are separate, strange, far from the king. Even if a king had many children, that wouldn't make him a king. Not even a court of ministers would. "In a multitude of people is the king's glory."

The Name for His kingship is *Adnut*, "Lord," because He is Lord of the whole earth. This trait is what makes the world a world: a real, separate thing. If it left, the world would go back into its source and vanish.

And what is a "world"? Place and time. Place is east and west, north and south, up and down. Time is past, present, and future. None of these belong to G-d's higher traits. Only His kingship: "G-d is king, G-d was king, G-d will be king," above without end and below without end. So place and time themselves, their coming into being and their lasting, come from His kingship.

But His kingship is fully one with Him. So place and time are lost in Him like sunlight in the sun. The Name Havayah is above time: "was, is, and will be" in one moment. It is above place too, since it makes all of place, top to bottom. Yet *He is also found below, in place and time*. That is the lower unity. His very Being, the Infinite, fills the whole earth, in place and in time, *equally, truly*. *For so He is on the earth below just as in the heavens above.* Everything is "place," and place is lost in the Infinite Light, which dresses itself in it through His kingship. Only His kingship is also a hiding, so that place and time won't vanish even for us.

Then the chapter opens a door the mind can't walk through. "I, G-d, have not changed." As He was alone before the world was made, so He is alone after. Nothing changed in Him, not even in His knowing. He knows every creature by knowing Himself, because everything is from Him. The Rambam says He is "the knower, the known, and the knowing, all one," and that "the mouth can't say it, the ear can't hear it, the heart can't fully know it." We learn something and the knowledge is added to us. He is simply one, with nothing added. So just as no one can grasp Him, no one can grasp His knowing. It can only be held *by faith, which is above the mind*: He knows everything, down to the smallest worm in the sea and the tiniest gnat, and it adds nothing to Him.

So the Tanya corrects some scholars who read the Arizal's teaching of *tzimtzum*, contraction, too literally, as if G-d pulled Himself out of this world and only watches from above. That is impossible. Contraction is something bodies do. And they themselves believe He knows every creature, and His knowing is Him. The Zohar says: "No place is empty of Him." "He holds everything, and nothing holds Him. He surrounds all worlds, and He fills all worlds."

"Nothing holds Him" means no mind can grasp Him. It also means that though He fills the worlds, He isn't caught inside them like a soul in a body. A soul feels its body's pain, its cold and heat. He doesn't change with summer and winter, day and night. "Even darkness is not dark to You."

"Surrounds" means this. When you think about something, your thought wraps around the *picture* of it in your mind, not the thing itself. His thought wraps around each creature *in fact*, because His knowing it *is* its life and being.

"Fills" is the life dressed inside each thing, shrunk down to its size. Everything created has limits. Even the sun, many times bigger than the earth, can only shine so far. So the life inside things had to shrink through many great contractions. It could have spread forever and made endless worlds, and then this world would never have existed. Instead He dressed it in the letters of the Ten Sayings, and in their rearrangements, and in their numbers, each a further step down. After all these steps the life could even dress itself in a stone. The chapter shows how the Hebrew word for stone, *even*, points back to one of G-d's very high Names, through many contractions, until only a very little life is left, enough to be a stone's soul. That is "fills all worlds," and it is different from "surrounds."

The lowest place is not farther from Him. He fills this floor exactly as He fills heaven, and His knowing wraps every grain of it, from inside and from all around.

**Sit with it** *(8 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Wherever you are sitting, let it be an ordinary place. A kitchen, a bus, a small room. - **The line, softly:** "As much on the earth below as in the heavens above." - **Think it through.** Place is His kingship's garment. Here, this spot, is where He is King. Then the two sides: the life inside this cup, shrunk to fit a cup. ## Chapter 8 · The ladder inside you

The Rambam said that G-d, His being and His knowing are all simply one. The Tanya says the same is true of *all* His traits and Names. When the prophets call Him gracious, merciful, kind, or wise; when the Torah says He *wants* those who fear Him, *desires* kindness, *wants* the wicked to return and not to die, is "too pure to look at evil": none of these add anything to Him. His will, His wisdom, His kindness, His strength, His mercy are all one simple unity. They are His very Being.

And, as the Rambam said, the mouth can't say this, the ear can't hear it, the heart can't fully know it. Why is it so hard? Because *a person pictures everything he wants to understand as it is inside himself.* When we try to picture "will" or "wisdom" or "kindness," we picture them as they live in us, where they are extra things added to a self. But G-d is "holy," which means set apart by endless levels, far above every kind of praise a creature could picture.

Then the chapter builds a ladder inside a person. The first and highest thing in any creature is wisdom. It is called "the beginning." From wisdom come understanding and knowledge. From those come the feelings: love, kindness, mercy. You can see this plainly: a small child without understanding is angry all the time, and can be cruel, and loves little things not worth loving, because love follows understanding.

From the feelings come the words of thought. You think about what you love, or how to do a kind act. Inside every thought in the world is some feeling that made you think it, and that feeling is the thought's life. From the letters of thought come the letters of speech. And speech leads to deeds, like a king who tells his servants to give.

Now the proportions. The power of your soul in a deed you do is *as nothing* next to the power in your speech, like a body next to a soul. Speech is like that next to thought. Thought is like that next to the feeling inside it. The feeling is like that next to the mind it grows from. This is true in every soul in every world: wisdom is the beginning and the source.

We picture Him as a very large version of us, with wisdom and kindness added on. The ladder in us shows why that picture is our own reflection, and He is simply One.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Notice any picture of G-d you carry, even a faint one. Let it be there for now. - **The line, softly:** "A person pictures it as it is in himself." - **Think it through.** One small act: you give a coin. It came from a word ("here"), the word from a thought, the thought from caring, the caring from understanding. Five rungs. Each one is as nothing next to the one above it. - **Where the picture breaks.** The ladder is in you. He is not its top rung. - **From inside.** The mind's pictures grow quiet. Not forced quiet; the mind sees they were its own reflection. - **The edge.** "The mouth can't say it, the heart can't fully know it." ## Chapter 9 · Like touching an idea with your hands

Wisdom is the very first thing in every creature. But before G-d, wisdom is *the end of the deed*. It counts, next to Him, the way a physical action counts next to the mind. "You made them all *in wisdom*."

Here is the measure. In us there are five rungs: deed, speech, thought, feeling, mind. Each is as nothing to the one above. That is how far the mind is above the deed: five rungs. But G-d is above wisdom by *myriads of rungs like these, without end*. We only know the chain from wisdom down to action, so we say: before Him, wisdom is like action.

Then the Tanya says something sharp. It doesn't even fit to say about Him, "He can't be grasped because He's too deep." Grasping or not grasping belongs to the world of ideas. You can say an idea is too deep to understand. But He is above ideas and wisdom altogether. He is not in the category of grasping at all. *Whoever says of Him that He cannot be grasped is like someone who says of a deep, lofty idea that it can't be touched with the hands because it's so deep.* Anyone who heard that would laugh. Touch belongs only to physical things. And so, before Him, the mind and all its understanding are like a physical thing held in the hands. That is true even of the minds of the highest worlds, even of the highest wisdom that gives them all life.

Why then does the Torah call Him "wise," and the sages call Him wise and merciful and kind? Because He is the *source* of wisdom, mercy and kindness. They come out from Him. How that happens is known to the wise. (A note here, in Kabbalah language, adds that even the Rambam's "He is the knower and the knowing" only fits after His light has been dressed in wisdom's vessels. Without that, He isn't in the category of knowing at all.)

And then the chapter lays down its tools. *We have no business with hidden things.* What is given to us is to believe with whole faith that He and His traits are one: His will, wisdom, understanding and knowledge are one with His very Being, which is endlessly above wisdom and grasping. So how He is one with His traits is also beyond grasping. That is why the Zohar calls the traits "the mystery of faith": the faith that is above the mind.

Understanding is not too weak to reach Him. It is the wrong kind of thing, the way hands are the wrong kind of thing for an idea. Where the mind stops, faith is not a lesser knowing. It is the only kind that fits.

**Sit with it** *(6 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Put both hands flat on the table. Feel the wood. Hands are good at this. - **The line, softly:** "Like touching an idea with your hands." - **Think it through.** Now try to hold an idea, any idea, with those same hands. You can't. Not because it's too heavy. Because hands are the wrong kind of thing. That is your mind before Him. - **Where the picture breaks.** Even this picture is a thought. He is above that too. Let the picture go once it has done its work. - **From inside.** A mind that has run to its wall and stopped straining. A hush, and under it a trust that doesn't need to know how. - **The edge.** "We have no business with hidden things." Don't reach past. Believe, and rest. - **Into the day.** Put your hand flat on a table once today and say: "This is my mind before Him."

## Chapter 10 · A picture, and "not really like this"

After shutting the door on grasping, the Tanya opens a window. "The Torah speaks in human language," the sages said, "to soothe the ear with what it can hear." So the masters of Kabbalah were allowed to speak about G-d's traits through a picture. They called them "lights," so we could understand a little of how He is one with His traits.

The picture is the sun. The body of the sun is called "the great luminary." The rays that spread from it are called "light." When the light is inside the sun, it is one with it. There is only one thing there, the sun. *The rays and light there are one substance with the body of the sun, and have no existence of their own at all.*

His traits, His will and wisdom, are one with Him like that, and more so. They came out from Him the way light comes from the sun. *But not really in this way. In a way far and wondrous beyond our grasp,* "for His ways are higher than our ways."

Still, to soothe the ear, we can learn from the sunlight. Inside its source it has no name of its own; only the source has a name. So too, His traits and will and wisdom are called by these names only from the side of the creatures. He brings them into being, keeps them alive and leads them through His will and wisdom dressed in His traits. The sages said, "The world was made with ten things: with wisdom, with understanding, with knowledge." And Elijah said in the Zohar: "You brought out ten garments, which we call the ten sefirot, to lead through them hidden worlds and revealed ones, and in them You are hidden."

Then an example from the six days. On the first day, kindness was revealed, holding all the other traits within it, and with it He made light: "Let there be light," light spreading from one end of the world to the other. Because it had some might in it too, the light wasn't as spiritual as the light above, and it was dressed in a world with limits. On the second day might was revealed, and He made the firmament to divide the upper waters from the lower. That is holding back: the spiritual upper waters were hidden, and so the lower waters became physical. But kindness was inside that might too, because "the world is built with kindness." It was all so that dry land would appear, and a person on it to serve G-d. And so on with all the days.

Elijah finishes: all these names show how the world is led, with justice and with mercy. "But not that You have a known justice, or a known mercy, or any of these traits at all."

The traits have names only from our side. On His side there is no "kindness" and "strength" as two things, only Him. The picture is allowed, as long as you say where it ends.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Let yourself use whatever picture of G-d helps you today: light, a father, a king. It is allowed. - **The line, softly:** "But not really in this way." - **Think it through.** The sun and its rays, one thing inside the sun. His traits and Him, one, and more than that. Then your own day: the giving, like the first day's light. Far and wondrous beyond us. - **From inside.** A light hold on pictures. Using them, and not leaning on them too hard. - **The edge.** "No known justice, no known mercy." Let the names fall silent there. - **Into the day.** When you use a picture of G-d today, add under your breath: "Not really like this."

## Chapter 11 · What was hidden in the heart, spoken

The Ten Sayings are called "sayings" only from the creatures' side. Here is why.

Think of the traits in a person. Say you have kindness and mercy in your heart. They can't come out into action by themselves. First you think about the kind act you want to do. You can't act without thinking. And if you want someone else to act, like a king telling others to give, then the kindness and the thought get dressed in words. (Or when you simply speak kind words to a friend.)

So too, in a picture, with G-d's traits. When they come out to act in the world below, that coming out is called a "saying" and a "combination of letters." No action can come from His traits without it. When He made light out of kindness, the power that came from kindness to make light is what is called the saying "Let there be light."

His "letters" are not like our thoughts, heaven forbid. Still they stand for something real: *this* power makes light, and not water, even though water also comes from kindness. Water came through other combinations.

So all the life and power that come from His traits to create and keep the world are called "the holy letters." And there are two kinds of worlds. Hidden worlds come from hidden powers, like the letters of thought in a person. Revealed worlds come from powers that were revealed. Those are called "sayings," "the word of G-d," "the breath of His mouth," *like the letters of speech in a person, which show the listeners what was hidden and closed in his heart.*

But really, the Tanya says, the speech above is far above any created wisdom. With the saying "Let us make man," man was created with wisdom and mind. Or even just with the breath above: "He blew into his nostrils the breath of life." So His speech and breath are the source of the wisdom in the first man's soul, which held all the souls of the righteous, who are greater than angels. His letters come from traits that are one with Him, endlessly above created wisdom. They are called "letters" not because they are small compared to creatures, but only compared to His traits themselves.

There are twenty-two kinds of flow, different from each other, and with them all worlds were made. That is how He wanted it: exactly twenty-two, no fewer and no more. These are the twenty-two letters fixed in the mouth and tongue. Even their written shapes show the shape of each flow. And in a person, too, the letters of thought and speech flow out of the mind and heart.

The world in front of you is what was hidden in Him, spoken out. To listen to the world is to hear Someone, not noise.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Let the sounds and sights around you be whatever they are. - **The line, softly:** "What was hidden in the heart, spoken." - **Think it through.** A kind thing you once said to someone. Before you said it, it lived closed inside you. Then it came out in words and they heard your heart. Now look at the world: every thing in it is His heart, spoken out. - **Where the picture breaks.** Our words are smaller than our minds. His speech is higher than all created wisdom. It is where wisdom came from. - **From inside.** Listening changes. The world sounds like Someone speaking. - **The edge.** Letters joined with traits that are one with Him. Say it and leave it closed. - **Into the day.** When you speak kindly to someone today, notice: what was closed in me is now open. That is what the world is.

## Chapter 12 · Moonlight, down to the stone

The last chapter of the Gate goes down, not up.

Creatures are split into kinds, big families and small details, by changing the combinations of letters. Each single letter is its own particular kind of life and power. When many letters join into a word, you get more than the powers of each letter added up. You get something above them all: a larger, whole life that weighs as much as all of them together and more. It joins them and binds them, and gives life to the thing made by that word, the whole of it and each part. (A note adds that each letter's written shape is different because each carries its own kind of flow.)

Take the words "Let there be a firmament." With them the seven heavens were made, and everything in them. The sages describe them: one heaven where millstones grind manna for the righteous; one with a heavenly Jerusalem, a Temple, an altar; one with storehouses of snow and hail. The heavens as a whole live from those words. Each detail in them lives from some combination of the letters of those words, or from their swaps and substitutions.

Each change of order weaves the powers differently. The first letter in a combination leads. It is the main one in that creation. The others are secondary and are drawn into its light. That's how a new creature comes to be. And when letters are swapped or substituted, the creatures made are lower than the ones made by the letters themselves.

The picture: moonlight on the ground at night. The moon's light comes from the sun. So the light on the ground is the light of the light of the sun.

In just that way, the letters of the Ten Sayings are the great flow of life and light from His traits that makes the worlds out of nothing and keeps them going, as long as He wants. From that great light He drew out offshoots, its children and branches: the swaps and substitutions of the letters. With them He made the particular creatures in each world. Then He drew down more, a glow of a glow of a glow of the letters. And more, down and down through the chain, *until the lifeless things themselves were made, like stones and dust.* Their names, "stone" and "dust," are swaps of swaps and substitutions of substitutions.

Dust is not outside the word. It is where the word finally arrives, the last echo of the first saying, still being said.

## Chapter 1 · Return is not the fast

The letter opens with law. The Talmud teaches that there are three kinds of atonement, and return (*teshuvah*) goes with each. If you failed to do a positive mitzvah and returned, you're forgiven right away. If you broke a "don't," return holds the judgment off, and Yom Kippur finishes the atonement. If you did one of the gravest sins, return and Yom Kippur hold it off, and suffering finishes the cleaning.

In a long aside, the Tanya explains why. A mitzvah you do draws light from the Infinite into the worlds and into your soul. If you missed one, the punishment can be forgiven, but the light that would have come is still missing. The sages said "the crooked can't be made straight" about someone who once skipped the evening Shema. And a sin against a "don't" sticks evil to the soul and leaves a flaw above, at its root, so the cleaning waits for Yom Kippur, "before G-d you will be pure." But don't take this as license to be lax about doing mitzvot, especially learning Torah. The sages said G-d overlooked even idolatry, but not neglect of Torah study.

Then the letter's first real point. The Torah's commandment of return is *only leaving the sin*. You decide in your heart, with a whole heart, never to go back to that foolishness, never again to rebel against the King. That is the plain meaning of the word *teshuvah*: to return to G-d with all your heart and all your soul, to serve Him and keep His commandments. "Let the wicked leave his way, and return to G-d." "Return, Israel, to G-d your G-d."

*Not as the crowd thinks, that return is the fast.* Even for the gravest sins, where the atonement is finished by suffering, it is G-d who brings the suffering, and He does it out of love. When a person returns with his whole heart from love, love answers from above, "as water shows a face back to a face," and G-d in His kindness cleans the sin in this world. "Whom G-d loves, He corrects." That is why the great codes of law, the Rambam and the Smag, mention no fasting at all in the laws of return. Only confession and asking forgiveness.

When the prophet Joel says "return to Me with fasting and weeping," that was to cancel a decree of locusts. The same goes for public fast days. And the books of moral instruction that prescribe many fasts for grave sins, including sexual sins such as wasting seed, do so for other reasons: to be spared suffering above, to hurry the cleaning, or because the person may be returning only from fear and not yet from love.

Return is not paying a price. It is turning your face. The One you turn to was never down some far road; He is there the moment the heart decides.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Let one thing you regret come to mind. Don't dig for more. One is enough. - **The line, softly:** "To return to G-d with all my heart." - **Think it through.** The question is not how much you suffer for it. It is which way your heart faces now. Decide, quietly and whole: not again. That decision is the mitzvah. - **Where the picture breaks.** "Return" sounds like a long walk back. He was never far down that road. - **From inside.** Relief. The weight moves from punishment to direction. - **The edge.** A missed mitzvah leaves a light missing. Let that make you careful, not heavy. - **Into the day.** Name one thing you want to stop, and say only: "I am turning toward You." Anyone can say this in their own words.

## Chapter 2 · Wanted again

Everything in the last chapter was about being *forgiven*. When a person returns fully, his sin is completely wiped away. On the day of judgment it isn't mentioned, not a word, not half a word.

But there is something past forgiveness. It is to be *wanted* by G-d again, pleasing and dear to Him as before the sin, so that his service gives G-d joy. For that, in Temple times, a person brought an offering, an *olah*, even for a small positive mitzvah he had missed. The Talmud calls it "a gift after the pardon."

The picture: a man wronged the king. He sent advocates, and the king forgave him. Still, he sends the king a gift, so the king will be glad to see his face again. The word "atonement" used for this offering doesn't mean cleaning the soul. It means making things right before G-d, so that He is pleased again.

Now there are no offerings. So the fast takes their place. The Talmud has a prayer for a fast day: "May the fat and blood I have lost be as if I offered them before You."

That is why some of the great sages of the Talmud fasted a great deal over small things. Rabbi Elazar ben Azariah fasted until his teeth turned black, because his neighbor's cow went out on Shabbat in a way the other sages forbade, and he didn't stop her. Rabbi Yehoshua fasted the same way after a sharp word against the school of Shammai. Rav Huna fasted forty fasts because his tefillin strap once turned over. And the Arizal taught his students numbers of fasts for many sins, even ones that carry no heavy penalty: for anger, for drinking forbidden wine, for missing prayers. The letter gives the counts.

In general, it says, fasting is *a wonderful way to reveal the will above*, like an offering, "a pleasing smell to G-d." Isaiah calls a fast "a day of will for G-d."

Past the clean slate there is a face that opens. Return doesn't stop at being cleared; it goes on until you are near again.

That wish, to be wanted and not only cleared, is holy. It is what the old offering was for. - **Where the picture breaks.** He needs no gift. The gift changes the giver, so the face can open. - **From inside.** A longing to be near, not only to be clean. - **The edge.** "A day of will." What that will is, the chapter doesn't open. Rest in the wish. - **Into the day.** After you make something right with someone today, do one small kind thing more.

## Chapter 3 · Don't break the body; give instead

This chapter is practical law, and it is gentle.

Teachers of later generations disagreed. If someone did the same sin many times, does he need the full number of fasts for each time, like a sin-offering brought for each sin? Or is one set enough, like one burnt-offering that covers many missed mitzvot? The accepted way is in the middle: three times the count. The letter gives the example of wasting seed, which the Arizal's list counts at eighty-four fasts, so two hundred fifty-two. The reason comes from the Zohar: a sin done once leaves a mark; done three times, the stain spreads.

Then the turn. *All this is only for a strong, healthy person*, whom many fasts won't hurt at all, as in earlier generations. But someone whom many fasts could harm, who might get sick or weak, as in our generations, *is forbidden to fast much*. Even for the gravest sins. He should fast only as much as he is sure won't harm him at all. Even in the days of the Talmud, only the healthy fasted like this. The Talmud calls someone who can't bear it and fasts anyway a "sinner," even when he fasts for his sins. And a Torah scholar who weakens himself sins twice, because then he can't learn properly.

So what should he do? "Redeem your sin with charity." For each day of fasting, give a set sum. The letter names the coin of its day. A wealthy person gives more, as he can.

Still, the letter adds, anyone *whose soul wants closeness to G-d*, who wants to bring his soul back in the finest way, should, once in his life, complete the count of fasts for each grave sin. And even this is spread out gently: on the short days of winter, maybe ten fasts in a winter, or fewer, over nine years or more, "as his strength allows." He can eat a little before dawn if he says so beforehand. Half-days can count.

All the rest of the fasts, and the fasts for lighter sins and for missed mitzvot and Torah study, he can redeem with charity if he can't bear them. And he shouldn't worry about the rule "don't give away more than a fifth." This isn't waste. He's redeeming himself from fasts, and it's no less than paying for the body's healing. That's why, the letter says, the G-d-fearing people of its day give a great deal of charity: the generation is weak and can't bear so much.

Caring for the body is part of return, not its enemy. The same soul that wants to come close is asked not to break the house it lives in; and the hand that gives is doing the work of the fast.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Notice if part of you wants to punish yourself for something. Don't argue with it. Just see it. - **The line, softly:** "Whoever wants closeness to G-d." - **Think it through.** The Tanya forbids a weak person to fast. It says: give instead. The coin does the work the hunger would have done, and the body is left strong enough to learn and pray. - **Where the picture breaks.** "Redeem" sounds like buying your way out. It isn't cheap. It is what the soul asks for when it wants to come home without harming the body. - **From inside.** Kindness toward yourself inside the work of return, without making return small. - **The edge.** This is law, and it doesn't reach the mind's edge. Let it be plain. - **Into the day.** Give a coin to charity today in place of a harshness you might have used on yourself.

## Chapter 4 · Blown from the inside

Everything so far was about finishing atonement *after* return. Now the letter turns to the beginning and heart of return: coming back to G-d truly, with a whole heart. It needs a full explanation.

It starts with two sayings of the Zohar. The Hebrew word *teshuvah* can be read as *tashuv hei*, "the letter hei returns." There are two heis in G-d's four-letter Name. The lower hei is "lower return," and the upper hei is "higher return." And a hard saying: in some places the Zohar says return doesn't help for wasting seed. That is very strange, since "nothing stands in the way of return," not even idolatry. A later book explains: lower return isn't enough there; it needs higher return.

To understand this, the letter asks one more question. The Torah says certain sins bring *karet*, being "cut off," and some bring death from heaven. Such a person was supposed to die young, before fifty or sixty. Yet in every generation there are people guilty of these sins who live long, pleasant lives. How?

The answer begins with a verse: "For G-d's portion is His people." The soul is *a part of the Name Havayah*. "He blew into his nostrils the breath of life." And "one who blows, blows from within himself."

G-d has no body, of course. "The Torah speaks in human language." So here is the picture. When a person speaks, a little breath leaves his mouth, carrying a little of his life, just its outside. When he *blows* hard, the breath comes from deep inside and carries his inner life.

In the same way, though the difference above is endless: all the hosts of heaven, even the angels, were made from nothing and live from the *outside* of G-d's life. That is called "the breath of His mouth," the letters of the Ten Sayings. But the human soul came first from the *inside* of that life: "He blew." Only afterward did it come down through the letters of "Let us make man" to dress itself in a body. That's why angels are called *elokim*, the outer Name. *But the human soul, which comes from the inside of the life, is part of the Name Havayah,* which is above the letters.

Then the letter maps the four letters of the Name. The *yud*, a single point, is hidden wisdom, before it opens up. The tiny thorn on top of the yud points to G-d's will, far above wisdom. The first *hei* is wide and long: understanding spreading out, and flowing down. The *vav* is a line going down: the flow through His traits of kindness and the rest. The last *hei* is just breath, the sound of the letter hei, "a light letter with no weight," the breath that rises from the heart and splits into the five places of the mouth to make speech. That is His kingship, called "the word of G-d."

And the same four letters are in the soul. The *yud* is your hidden mind, which can understand His truth and greatness, each person by his own measure. The first *hei* is understanding, as you deepen and widen it, giving birth to love and awe, first hidden in the mind and heart and then open in the heart. The *vav* and the last *hei* are the true service that comes out of that: Torah and mitzvot, in voice, speech and deed. And the thinking itself comes from Torah, because "Torah comes from wisdom," the yud.

The chapter doesn't answer its question yet. The next two chapters do.

*The Tanya speaks here of the Jewish soul and its particular root; this adaptation applies it to every person.*

Whatever you have done, what you are came from His inside. The soul isn't a word He spoke from outside. It is a breath He blew from within.

**Sit with it** *(6 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Let yourself be whoever you've been today, good or not. Don't sort it. - **The line, softly:** "Blown from within." - **Think it through.** Think of the difference between saying a word and blowing out a candle hard, from deep in the chest. That's the difference between an angel and a soul. Then the four letters in you: a mind that can understand Him, understanding that grows love and awe, and the hands and voice that carry it out. - **Where the picture breaks.** He has no mouth and no chest. "The Torah speaks in human language." And a breath, once out, leaves. This one is still tied. - **From inside.** Dignity at the root. A self that is older than anything you did. - **The edge.** The thorn on the yud, the will above wisdom. Name it and don't enter. - **Into the day.** Before a hard word today, remember what you are made of: a breath blown from His inside.

## Chapter 5 · The rope

The soul came down into the body from the last hei, the breath above. "He blew into his nostrils the breath of life, and man became a living soul."

That is the verse "G-d's portion is His people; Jacob is the *rope* of His inheritance." A rope, one end tied above, the other end down here.

Now think about blowing. If you blow toward a spot and something stands in the way, the breath doesn't get there. So you might think: maybe something stands between a person's body and the breath above.

*But in truth nothing, physical or spiritual, stands between before Him.* "Do I not fill heaven and earth?" "The whole earth is full of His glory." "No place is empty of Him." "In the heavens above and on the earth below, there is nothing else."

Only one thing separates. As Isaiah says: "Your sins have separated between you and your G-d." Why sins? Because they go against the will above, which gives life to everything, "all that G-d wished, He did." That will is hinted in the thorn on top of the yud.

That is what *karet* means: the rope that draws life down from the Name is *cut*. "That soul will be cut off from before Me." Other sins, without karet, still damage the soul. The Hebrew word for damage, *pegam*, is used for a nick in a knife blade.

Here the picture gets finer. A thick rope is twisted from many thin cords. So is this rope: it is made of the 613 mitzvot. When a person breaks one, a thin cord snaps.

And even for someone who deserved karet, some *mark* of the Godly soul still remains in him. Through it he can live, up to fifty or sixty years. (The letter adds a brief note about a teaching of the Arizal and says it doesn't refer to the body's life.) That is the beginning of the answer to the last chapter's question. The next chapter finishes it.

*The Tanya speaks here of the Jewish soul and its particular root; this adaptation applies it to every person.*

There is no wall between you and Him. Not the body, not the world, not distance. Only going against His will frays the rope, and a rope can be retied.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** If you feel far from G-d right now, let the feeling be there. It is allowed to be felt. - **The line, softly:** "Nothing stands between." - **Think it through.** A rope, one end tied high, one end here in your hands. Between the ends: no stone, no wall, no distance. He fills heaven and earth. Then the thin cords: each good thing done adds strength; each wrong cuts a strand. - **Where the picture breaks.** A rope has two ends far apart. Here the top end fills the whole space the rope hangs in. - **From inside.** The feeling of distance loses its authority. It is still felt, but it isn't believed. - **The edge.** The will above, the thorn on the yud, that gives life to all. The mind reaches it and stops. - **Into the day.** When you feel far today, say: "Nothing stands between. Only what I do against Him, and that can be retied."

## Chapter 6 · Pulling the rope, and bringing it home

What the last chapter said, the letter explains, was true when the people were on a high level, when the Shechinah, G-d's presence, rested in the Temple. Then their bodies got life only from the Godly soul, from the inside flow of the Name.

But after they fell from that level, their deeds caused what the Kabbalah calls "the exile of the Shechinah." "Because of your sins your mother was sent away." The flow of the last hei came down, level after level, until it was dressed in a mixed realm of good and bad, called *Nogah*. From there life flows through the stars and their angels to every living body and every plant in this world. The sages said, "There is no blade of grass below without a star above it." So even someone who sins can get life for his body and his animal soul, the way animals do. That answers the question from chapter 4.

It gets harder. The Zohar teaches that whatever life a person gets *in the very moment* he does evil, in deed or speech or even thought, comes to him from "the chambers of the other side," the realms opposed to holiness. And a person is free to choose. He can take his life from the chambers of the other side, or from the chambers of holiness, where all good and holy thoughts come from. "G-d made the one opposite the other."

Now back to the rope. If you pull one end of a rope, the other end moves too, as far as it can. So it is with the root of a person's soul, the last hei above. By bad deeds and thoughts, he *pulls its flow down*, as it were, right into the chambers of the other side, and from there he gets his thoughts and deeds. Since he is the one drawing the flow to them, he takes a share first. "Let the one who understands, understand." This is what the sages meant by "the tranquility of the wicked is not in our hands," in the time of exile. This is the exile of the Shechinah, as it were: feeding the very places G-d's soul hates.

*And when a person truly returns, he takes back the flow he drew down. By his return he brings the flow of the Shechinah back to its place.* That is *tashuv hei*: the last hei returns from exile. "G-d will return with your captivity." The sages note it doesn't say "bring back." It says He *returns with* you.

*The Tanya speaks here of the Jewish people and their particular root; this adaptation applies it to every person.*

What you do is not small. It moves something far above you, and the same hand that pulled the rope down can bring it home. Even His exile, as it were, is inside His oneness, and returns.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Let one thing you did wrong come up. Look at it without flinching and without drowning. - **The line, softly:** "By my return, back to its place." - **Think it through.** A rope tied high. You pull the low end and the high end moves. What you pulled down into a dark place was holy life. Now pull the other way: undo it, regret it, decide not again. It goes home. - **Where the picture breaks.** Nothing can truly bind Him. The Tanya says "as it were." And He doesn't wait above; He returns *with* you. - **From inside.** Responsibility without despair. What I pulled down, I can bring back. - **The edge.** "Let the one who understands, understand." The chapter hints and stops. So do you. - **Into the day.** Undo one small thing today, a word or a habit, and say: "Back to its place."

## Chapter 7 · The heart's cry, and an hour of honest counting

The true, straight way of lower return comes down to two things.

The first: *to wake up mercy from above, from the Source of mercy, for your own Godly soul, which fell "from a high roof into a deep pit."* It fell from the Life of all life into the chambers of impurity. And to feel mercy for its source too, the Name itself. "Let him return to G-d, and He will have mercy on him." It means: have mercy on the flow of the Name that was dragged down into the dark to keep it alive, by a person's deeds and bad thoughts. "The King is bound in the tresses," bound, as it were, in the racing thoughts of the mind. That is the exile of the Shechinah.

The best time for this is *Tikkun Chatzot*, the midnight lament. It says there: "The crown of our head has fallen; woe to us, for we have sinned." That is why an old book calls G-d "the humiliated King." There is no greater humiliation. And the more a person thinks about the greatness of the Infinite, who fills all worlds and surrounds all worlds, each by his own understanding, the more bitter this will be to him.

The second: *to crush and humble the kelipah*, the husk, the other side. Its whole life is coarseness and lifting itself up: "Though you rise high like an eagle." Crushing it down to the dust is its death. How? With "a broken and crushed heart," being "small in your own eyes." The Zohar reads "The offerings of G-d are a broken spirit" to mean breaking the spirit of the other side.

And how is the heart broken? A little by fasting. But in our generations we can't fast much, like King David. The main way is to be one of the *"masters of accounting."* Think deeply, for one hour every day or night, before the midnight lament. Think about what your sins did: they sent the Shechinah into exile, as it were. They pulled your Godly soul away from the Life of life and down into the place of impurity and death, so that it became a vehicle for that place, drawing its life from there. The sages said, "The wicked, even in their lives, are called dead": their life comes from the place of death. (The letter adds that the verse "the dead do not praise G-d" means them: people confused by stray thoughts who don't want to return.)

Even someone who never did a grave sin, only "light" ones, still damages his soul. Remember the thin cords. Many light sins can make as much damage as one heavy one. So can one light sin repeated many times.

The prophet compares sins to clouds that block the sun. "I have wiped away your transgressions like a thick cloud, and your sins like a mist." Heavy sins are a thick dark cloud between the soul and the inner light of the Name. Light sins, "the ones a person tramples with his heels," are a thin mist. But put many thin sheets over a window, and they darken it like one thick board, or more. And the sages count some "small" sins as grave as the gravest: turning your eyes away from someone who needs charity, speaking badly of another, anger, arrogance. And Torah study outweighs them all.

The letter ends with a teaching from the bedtime Shema and from Kabbalah, linking the four letters of the Name to the four court punishments and to missed mitzvot: Shema, tefillin, tzitzit, prayer. From this, it says, a thoughtful person can learn about all sins, and about neglecting Torah, which weighs against them all.

The sorrow here is not for your own image. It is mercy for the part of G-d in you that was dragged into the dark, and even that sorrow is a way of saying He is everything, and belongs everywhere.

**Sit with it** *(8 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Set a clear time for this. A short hour, or less. When it ends, it ends. - **The line, softly:** "Mercy on the soul that fell from a high roof." - **Think it through.** A high roof, a deep pit. The Godly part of you was up there. Look honestly at one thing you did, and at where it pulled that part of you. Then the window: one thin sheet lets light through. Many sheets make it dark. Count the thin ones too. - **Where the picture breaks.** A King can't be bound. The Tanya says "as it were." The cry is real anyway. - **From inside.** Real sorrow, heavy and clean. Sorrow for Him, not for how you look. If words come, let them be your own words to G-d. - **The edge.** The humiliated King. Stay with it only as long as you set. The next chapter opens the door out. - **Into the day.** Keep a short, fixed time to look honestly at one thing, and when the time is up, stop. Jews may join it to the midnight lament or to the bedtime Shema.

## Chapter 8 · Home, as before the breath left

After thinking deeply about all of this, a person can ask truly, from the depth of the heart: "In Your great mercy, wipe away my sins." Because now it will be fixed in his heart how much mercy is needed for the Godliness in his soul, and above.

With that, he wakes up mercy from above, from the *Thirteen Attributes of Mercy*. These flow from G-d's will, the thorn on the yud, far above the flow that comes through the letters of the Name. That's why the thirteen attributes clean every flaw: "He carries sin and transgression, and cleans." Then the other side can no longer feed on the flow of the last hei. The last hei returns to its place and joins the other letters again.

And the same happens below, in the person's soul. "Your sins separate" is no longer true. "He cleans" means He washes those who return, rinses their soul of the dirty clothes, the husks. The Talmud says a sin wraps itself around a person like a garment. This washing takes it off.

And since "a wind passed and cleaned them," *their soul can return all the way to G-d, and rise higher and higher to its source, and cleave to Him in a wondrous union, just as it was united with Him completely before He breathed it out* to come down and dress itself in a body.

The picture: a person's breath, before it leaves his mouth, is still one with him. So was your soul before it was sent. That is where return goes. *This is complete return.*

This union is "higher return," which comes after "lower return." The Zohar says higher return is learning Torah with awe and love of G-d. And it says something tender: people who return have something even the fully righteous don't. They pull themselves toward the King "with more of the heart's longing, and with great force."

Return doesn't end at being forgiven. It ends where the soul was before it ever left: one with Him, like breath still inside the one who breathes.

**Sit with it** *(6 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Whatever you regretted in the last sitting, let it be finished for now. You looked. That was the work. - **The line, softly:** "One with Him, as before He breathed it out." - **Think it through.** Breath not yet blown, still inside the chest, still one with the person. That was your soul before it came down. Ask for mercy, in the Psalm's words or your own: "In Your great mercy, wipe it away." Then let it go home. - **Where the picture breaks.** A breath that leaves is gone. His never truly left Him. - **From inside.** A homecoming more than a pardon. Simple closeness, without words. - **The edge.** The will above the letters, where the thirteen mercies come from. And the heart's longing that pulls harder than the righteous. Name it and rest there. - **Into the day.** Say "My G-d, the soul You gave me is pure" slowly in the morning, as a return to before. Or say in your own words: "Take me back to where I began."

## Chapter 9 · Stronger at the knot

The Zohar says *binah*, understanding, is "higher return," and calls it "the mother bird sitting on her chicks."

What does that mean? When a person thinks about G-d's greatness, deeply, and from his understanding gives birth to love and awe that make sense to him, "to love G-d, for He is your life," not content with the natural love hidden in every soul alone, then understanding is like the mother sitting on her chicks.

The heart of love is "spirit cleaving to spirit," as in "Let Him kiss me with the kisses of His mouth." That is what "with all your soul" means: all of the soul's parts, the mind and the feelings, and their clothes, thought, speech, and deed, should all cleave to Him.

The feelings cleave to His ways: "As He is merciful, so be merciful." The mind cleaves to His wisdom, which is learning Torah, since "Torah comes from wisdom." Thought cleaves to His thought. Speech cleaves to His word, which is the law in the Torah: "I have put My words in your mouth." And deed cleaves to His deeds, which is charity, "reviving the spirit of the lowly." That is spirit cleaving to spirit, completely, when it comes from love.

Now the Zohar's hard saying from chapter 4. Sexual sins, wasting seed and forbidden relations, damage the *mind*. So their fix is Torah study, which comes from wisdom. A teaching of Elijah says: "A person sinned and deserved death before G-d. What should he do to live? If he used to learn one page, let him learn two. If one chapter, two chapters."

The picture: *a rope that snapped and is tied again. At the place of the knot it is doubled, and doubled again.* So it is with "the rope of His inheritance."

That is what the verse means: "With kindness and truth, sin is atoned," and "truth" means Torah. And the sages said the sin of Eli's house could not be atoned by offerings, but it could be atoned by Torah and by acts of kindness.

The place where you broke can become the strongest part of the rope. Return doesn't just patch the cut; it ties you to Him more tightly than before.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of your weak place, the one you'd rather hide. - **The line, softly:** "At the knot it is doubled." - **Think it through.** A rope snapped and tied again. Run your fingers over the knot: thicker than anywhere else. Now the five ways to cleave: kindness like His, a mind in His Torah, thoughts toward Him, His words in your mouth, charity in your hand. Where you fell short, add a little more there. - **Where the picture breaks.** A knotted rope is a little uglier. This knot is not a scar to hide. - **From inside.** Hope with a shape. The broken place becomes the place you hold on hardest. - **The edge.** "Spirit cleaving to spirit." The chapter names it and doesn't describe it. Don't either. - **Into the day.** Add one small extra piece of learning where you once fell short. If you don't learn Torah, read one extra Psalm, or do one extra act of kindness.

## Chapter 10 · Low, then high, every day

Higher return through Torah and kindness comes *down* from above. G-d's word is really in your mouth: "I have put My words in your mouth." And His right hand holds you through kindness: "His right hand embraces me."

But a person below has to go *up*, step by step. That is higher return too, spirit cleaving to spirit, through the heart's intention in prayer, most of all in the Shema and its blessings. There you say "You shall love G-d with all your heart and all your soul," and mean it. And "these words shall be on your heart, and you shall speak of them," so that G-d's word is truly in your mouth.

And the same in keeping every mitzvah. We bless "who made us holy with His commandments," like the words of marriage, "you are made holy to me." "Holy" means set apart. His holiness can't dress itself inside the worlds, because everything is nothing before Him. It only *surrounds* them. It is G-d's will above.

After prayer we say, "*To You, G-d, I lift my soul*": that is, to keep cleaving spirit to spirit all day long. All this comes through thinking deeply about the greatness of the Infinite in the blessings before the Shema and in the morning Psalms.

Since prayer is higher return, lower return has to come first. The sages said: "Stand to pray only with heaviness of head," which Rashi explains as humility. That is lower return, waking up mercy. The Talmud learns it from Chana, who prayed "bitter of soul."

But another teaching says: "Stand to pray only from *joy*." How do both fit? In this "orphaned generation," not everyone can turn his heart from one extreme to the other in a moment. So the advice is to do lower return earlier, at the midnight lament. If you can't do it every night, then at least once a week, before Shabbat. Shabbat is higher return. The letters of *Shabbat* spell *tashev enosh*, "return, man." On Shabbat the worlds rise to their source, especially in the Shabbat prayers.

The chapter closes with a verse: "Return to Me, for I have redeemed you." First "I wiped away your sins like a cloud": the other side is removed. "I have redeemed you" from it through mercy, woken by lower return from below. *Then* "Return to Me": higher return.

The heart isn't asked to hold one mood forever. It goes low, then high, and both are the same turning toward Him. Even the ordinary day can be lifted, "to You I lift my soul," all day.

**Sit with it** *(6 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Notice which way your heart leans today, heavy or light. Either is a place to start. - **The line, softly:** "To You, G-d, I lift my soul." - **Think it through.** Low first: humility, honesty, a little bitterness if it's there. Then high: love, His words in your mouth. Then the whole day: lifted, not only at prayer. And the week: low before Shabbat, high on Shabbat. - **Where the picture breaks.** "Up" and "down" sound like two places. His holiness surrounds everything; it is the same One on every rung. - **From inside.** Rhythm. The heart can go low and then high, and it isn't failing either way. - **The edge.** Holiness that can't dress itself in worlds, that only surrounds. Point there and stop. - **Into the day.** After morning prayer, say once: "To You I lift my soul." Say it again at one moment in the afternoon. Anyone can say it, in any language.

## Chapter 11 · A thousand times is like once

To have humility in your heart, lower return, and also joy in G-d, both at once: the Tanya already explained this in its first book. The Zohar says, "Weeping is lodged in one side of my heart, and joy in the other." Add to them faith and trust: a steady heart, sure in G-d, that He "desires kindness," is gracious, merciful, and quick to forgive, *right away, the moment you ask*, with no doubt at all.

The proof: three times a day in the Amidah prayer, right after we ask "forgive us," we say "Blessed are You, G-d, who is gracious and forgives abundantly." If there were any doubt, it would be a blessing in vain, which is forbidden. So there is no doubt at all. We asked; He forgave.

Even between people it's so. A person must forgive right away when asked, and not be cruel, even someone who hurt him badly. (The letter adds some laws about forgiveness between people.) With G-d, how much more, without end.

We praise Him as the One who "forgives *abundantly*." Among people, if someone wrongs you and asks forgiveness, and you forgive, and he does it again, it is very hard to forgive a second time, let alone a third or fourth. *But with G-d there is no difference between once and a thousand times.* Forgiving comes from His mercy, and His mercy has no limit. *Before the Infinite there is no difference at all between a small number and a large one.* Everything counts as nothing before Him.

That's why every Yom Kippur we confess the same sins as last year, even though we did them again. And not only every year: three times every day, "who forgives abundantly." Prayer takes the place of the daily offerings, which atoned day after day.

Isn't this "I'll sin and then return"? No. That is only when, in the moment of sin, a person could have held back, but leaned on return to let himself do it. Then "he isn't given the help." But even he, if he pushes hard and returns, is accepted. We, though, ask every day "bring us back in complete return" before we ask to be forgiven; on Yom Kippur we ask "may I not sin again." So we are helped, and helped again. "Whoever comes to be pure is helped": the moment he comes. So forgiveness, too, comes at once.

And "my sin is always before me"? It doesn't mean to be always sad and low. Right after, the same psalm says, "Let me hear joy and gladness." A person should be in higher return all his days, which is in great joy. "*Before* me" means at a distance, like looking at something across the way. It is only to keep you from being proud, to stay humble before everyone, with a reminder in front of your eyes that you sinned against G-d.

In fact, remembering the sin *helps* joy. It helps you take whatever happens gladly, whether it comes from heaven or from people, in words or in deeds. It is good advice against anger and irritation. The sages praised those "who are insulted and don't insult back, who hear themselves shamed and don't answer." And "whoever lets things pass, all his sins are let pass."

Forgiveness doesn't run out because He doesn't run out. Before the Infinite, counting stops; and so a humble heart and a glad heart can live together.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of the thing you keep failing at. The same one, again. - **The line, softly:** "Before the Infinite, once and a thousand times are the same." - **Think it through.** A friend forgives you once, maybe twice. Then it gets hard. Now picture mercy with no end: no counter, no running total. Ask again. It is received again, at once. If you pray, say "who forgives abundantly" and mean it. If not, say it in your own words. - **Where the picture breaks.** "Before me" isn't a weight on your chest. It's a small sign across the room, just enough to keep you humble. - **From inside.** A joy that doesn't need you to be perfect. Lightness under humility. - **The edge.** Where counting means nothing. The mind can't count there. Rest. - **Into the day.** When someone insults you today, or something goes wrong, remember: I'm not so high. And let it pass.

## Chapter 12 · The shadow and the sun

The last chapter explains why a person can be glad even for bodily suffering. This is the hardest teaching in the letter, and it should be read carefully and gently.

The letter says that for a soul that sinned, suffering in this world is a great good. It cleans the soul here, and saves it from a far harder cleaning after death, in Gehinnom. This is especially so, it says, in its own generation, when people can't do all the fasts the Arizal listed. The Ramban wrote that even all of Job's suffering, over many years, is nothing compared to one hour of the soul's suffering there. But this world "is built with kindness." Light suffering here saves from heavy judgment there.

The letter speaks to someone who has sinned and is already suffering, and tells him his pain is not wasted. It does not tell anyone to go looking for pain; chapter 3 already forbade harming the body.

Then comes its picture, and it opens far past suffering. A shadow on the ground moves a handbreadth while the sun moves thousands of miles across the sky. That is the proportion. Something small down here matches something huge up there. And in the real thing it is *endlessly* more, all the way from the highest worlds down to this physical one. The Zohar says whole upper worlds rose when one small bird, a dove, or a handful of flour was offered on the altar. So it is with every mitzvah done with the hands.

That is what the sages said: *"A person makes himself holy a little, below; they make him very holy, from above."* It's the same "holy" from chapter 10, "who made us holy with His commandments": the holiness that surrounds all worlds.

And that is how reward and punishment work too. "The reward of a mitzvah is the mitzvah." The letter ends: "Knowledge is easy for the one who understands, and the one who thinks will find good."

Small here and vast there are not two worlds. They are one chain, and your hand is at the bottom of it. A little holiness below is met by much holiness from above.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of one small good thing you could do today. It may feel too small to matter. - **The line, softly:** "A little below; much from above." - **Think it through.** A shadow on the ground moves one handbreadth. Up in the sky, the sun has crossed thousands of miles. The shadow is small only because the sun is so far. One coin, one blessing, one kind word, one handful of flour on an old altar: small here, vast there. - **Where the picture breaks.** The shadow only follows the sun. Here your small act below *moves* what is above. - **From inside.** Your small deeds stop feeling small. - **The edge.** "The reward of a mitzvah is the mitzvah." Nothing to add. Let it be enough. - **Into the day.** Do one small mitzvah or one small kindness today, and picture the shadow moving: small here, vast there.
