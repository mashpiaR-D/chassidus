These are the last pages of the Tanya. The Holy Letters were written by the Alter Rebbe to his Chassidim over many years and gathered by his sons after his passing. Most are short. They ask people to give charity, to pray with care, to stop talking in shul, to love one another, and they comfort people who have lost a teacher. Tucked inside each request is one of the book's deepest ideas: charity as the one thing He Himself does all the time; the wick the future light will need; the soul in exile, and the heart that can wake up for it; the sick Shechinah whose blood stops moving when people split apart. The Kuntres Acharon ("the last notes") is different. It is a set of short, dense essays that go back over chapters of the first part of the book and over the Arizal's writings. They ask why the mouth matters, why the deed matters most, and why holding an etrog can reach further than the finest thought. The book ends there, on Shabbat and on the quiet of speech that stops. Where a letter is full of Kabbalah, this adaptation says so and gives the point in a plain sentence or two. Where it speaks hard words about the nations or the "other side," it says what the text says, gently, and leaves it there.

## Iggeret HaKodesh 17 · The one thing He does all the time

When you let mercy wake in your heart for someone who needs it, something wakes above too. Great mercy comes down on you, from the Source of all mercy. The rabbis say you eat the "fruits" of it in this world, and the "principal" stays for the World to Come.

The fruits are easy to picture. Life flows down from the Life of all life, world after world, until it becomes the plain things we pray for: children, long life, food on the table.

The principal is harder. The letter reads a verse: "Your commandment is very wide." Why one commandment, not "commandments"? And why "wide"? Because it means one commandment in particular: charity. Charity is G-d's own commandment. It is what the Holy One, in His glory and Himself, does all the time: He gives life to the worlds. Another verse calls charity "the way of G-d," like a road between towns. Charity is the road the Infinite Light will travel, the light that surrounds all worlds, all the way down into this world.

Now the letter compares two kinds of reward. In Gan Eden (the Garden of Eden, where souls go after this life) the light is measured. Each soul understands the secrets of the Torah it learned here, "each by its own measure." That is light that fills, coming down step by step, sized to the one who gets it. At the resurrection of the dead, the light will be the kind that surrounds: no measure, no limit. The rabbis say the righteous will sit "with crowns on their heads." A crown sits around the head, not inside it. That crown is the highest level, the one the Zohar calls "nothing" because no mind can hold it. And it will shine openly exactly when the soul is back in a body, a pure and clear one. "Their beginning is lodged in their end." The highest shows up at the lowest.

So "Your commandment is very wide" means: charity is a very wide vessel, a broad surface for the Infinite Light to wear ("He put on charity like a coat"). "Very" means without end. The letter adds that Gan Eden has many levels, one above the other, and the souls of scholars keep climbing and have no rest there. Only after the resurrection will they rest.

When you give, you are not doing something next to Him. You are doing the very thing He is doing right now to keep the world alive. And what you give becomes the place where His light, beyond every measure, will one day live in this world.

**Sit with it** *(6 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of one person you gave to lately, or meant to and didn't. Let it be there as it is. - **The line, softly:** "Giving is what He does, always." - **Think it through.** Right now He is giving life to every world, without a break. The coin you gave did the same thing, on a small scale. A road runs from His giving to yours. On that road, one day, the light that has no measure will come down into bodies, into this world. - **Where the picture breaks.** A road stays where it is, and you walk it. Here the traveler is the Infinite, coming toward you. - **From inside.** Standing next to Him, at the same work. The gift stops feeling like a loss and starts feeling like company. - **The edge.** The crown called "nothing" can't be grasped by any mind, even in Gan Eden. The text puts it in the body, at the end of days. Don't try to picture it. Let it be wider than you. - **Into the day.** When you give today, say quietly: "This is what He does, always." (Anyone can do this with any gift of money, food, or time.)

## Iggeret HaKodesh 18 · Two loves

"How beautiful, how sweet you are, love in delights." There are two kinds of love for G-d, the letter says.

The first is "love in delights." It is joy in G-d so great that the soul almost melts. You taste that He is good, a sweetness beyond words, a real taste of the World to Come. Not everyone gets this. It can't be earned by effort, the way awe can. Awe you have to work for, the way a person digs for hidden treasure. But this love falls on a person from above, by itself, without his planning it. It comes only after he has worked at awe (the awe of how great G-d is) as far as his soul can go. Then the love comes down "to live together with the awe."

The second love is longing. The soul wants to cling to G-d, to be "bound in the bundle of life." Being near Him is very good to it, and being far from Him is very bad, like an iron wall between them.

This love is hidden in the heart of every one of the people, even the wicked. It is where regret comes from. But because it is hidden, in exile in the body, the "husk" (the coarse, self-centered pull, called the klipah) can rule over it. The Tanya calls that the "spirit of folly" that makes a person sin.

So the work is this. First, push the husk out of your body: out of your thought, your speech, your deeds. Then "bring the prisoner out of the dungeon" with a strong hand. Be brave. Let the hidden love come out into every power of the soul. The main place to start is the mind. Think, over and over, as far as your mind goes, how G-d is the Life of all life, and the life of your own soul in particular.

Then you will long to be near Him with a natural longing, "like a son who longs to be with his father always, and like fire that always rises by its nature toward its source." The more you keep thinking this, the more the longing grows and spreads into your mouth and your limbs, into Torah and mitzvot. Through them you cling to G-d Himself, "for the Torah and the Holy One are all one."

This longing, when it is out in the open, is what the verse means: "My soul thirsts for You." Like a thirsty man who has no water yet. There is no delight in it yet at all. Only thirst.

And we pray for help with this. Let the heart be full of this love alone. Its rival, the desire for this world, should not move into the house. The love should be the lady of the house. Even if the rival can't be sent away completely, let her stay in the back, a servant, used only for what you truly need, like eating and drinking. "Know Him in all your ways."

*The Tanya speaks here of the Jewish soul and its particular root; this adaptation applies it to every person.*

The pull toward G-d isn't something you make up. It's already in you, like fire's pull upward, because you come from Him. The distance you feel is real, and so is the thing that hates the distance.

**Sit with it** *(6 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Whatever you feel toward G-d right now, even nothing, let it be there. - **The line, softly:** "I want to be near You." - **Think it through.** He is the Life of all life. And He is the life of my own soul, this one, now. A son who wants to be home with his father. A flame that leans up no matter which way you hold the candle. - **Where the picture breaks.** A son can walk home; a flame reaches the air. This longing doesn't get there by moving. It grows as the mind keeps thinking, until it spills into your mouth and your hands. - **From inside.** Thirst. Not the sweetness yet, just the wanting. The text says that is enough to start. - **The edge.** The love of delight can't be reached by trying. It is a gift, and it comes when the awe is ready. Leave room for it. Don't chase it. - **Into the day.** Once today, in your own words, say: "I want to be near You." Let the wanting be the prayer.

## Iggeret HaKodesh 19 · The back of wisdom

The Arizal wrote that Moshe, the greatest prophet, did not grasp the inside of the highest wisdom. He saw only its "back," the part of it that comes down, clothed in lower levels. That is what G-d told him: "You will see My back, but My face will not be seen." (The letter traces this through the sefirot, the levels of G-d's light. The point is simple: Moshe's prophecy reached the lower levels openly, and the higher ones only from behind.)

But this raises a hard question. The Torah says no prophet ever rose like Moshe. So how could the Arizal teach about levels even higher than those?

The answer, the letter says, is plain. Prophecy is called "seeing." Something hidden shows itself to the prophet, openly. The sages of the hidden wisdom, like Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai and the Arizal, grasp by wisdom and knowing. Nothing appears to them. They understand hidden things that stay hidden. That is why the rabbis said, "A sage is better than a prophet." Wisdom can reach higher than what is able to come down and be seen. Only the lowest levels come down to be seen, because they are the channels through which every higher level gives life to the one below.

Inside those channels is understanding. Inside understanding is the back of wisdom, which is above understanding. So the Torah "comes from wisdom," and that is why the reasons for the mitzvot were never revealed. Even where the Torah does give a reason, the reason we understand is not the whole reason. Inside it is a wisdom above the mind. The same is true of every word G-d spoke to the prophets, "whether words of rebuke or stories." You can see it in the words that are written one way and read another. The reading is what we understand. The writing is above understanding, and has no clothing.

What is the "face" of a level, and what is its "back"? The face is the level itself, one with the Infinite. The back is what spreads down into limited creatures. The letter gives a picture from us. A person has five layers: mind, feelings, thought, speech, deed. The life in the deed is nothing next to the life in speech, and that is nothing next to the life in thought and feeling and mind. In the same way, all of His wisdom that comes down into creatures is as nothing next to its face.

So Moshe, who reached the back of wisdom, was the one through whom the Torah was given, "the fallen fruit of the wisdom above." And the Torah that fell down to us is for doing. Every soul, the Arizal says, comes back again and again until it has done all 613 mitzvot in action.

A short note follows. The letters we have come in three forms: written (the shapes in a Torah scroll), spoken (the twenty-two sounds, the same in every language, only combined differently), and thought. Spoken letters get their life from the letters of thought. Even when you speak while thinking of something else, you can only say words that were in your thought many times before; a trace of that thinking stays in them. That trace is what the "back" of a higher level is: what enters the lower to give it life.

Every word of the Torah, even a plain story, is full of a wisdom that is one with Him. What we see is the back. The face is right there inside it, too close to be seen.

**Sit with it** *(6 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of a verse or a story in the Torah you never quite understood. Let the not-understanding be there. - **The line, softly:** "There is more in this than I can see." - **Think it through.** The story is a garment. Inside it is understanding. Inside that is wisdom that no mind can hold. Moshe himself saw the back. And the back came down to us so that we could do something with it. - **Where the picture breaks.** A back is usually the less important side of a person. Here the back is how the face reaches you at all. - **From inside.** Respect for the verse you don't understand. It isn't empty. It is full above you. - **The edge.** "My face will not be seen." Stop where Moshe stopped. The wisdom is there; seeing it is not the point. - **Into the day.** Read one Torah story today (or one line of Psalms, for anyone) and say: "There is more in this than I can see." Then do one thing it asks.

## Iggeret HaKodesh 20 · Only He makes something from nothing

This letter is long and full of Kabbalah. Its core is one of the strongest things the Tanya says about G-d's own being.

The Zohar says that in the highest world, "He and His life are one; He and His vessels are one." How can the Infinite be one with vessels, which have limits? The letter answers: it means those vessels are G-dliness, able to create something from nothing, just as the Infinite can.

Most things in the world come from other things, the way an effect comes from a cause. An effect stays wrapped in its cause and melts into it, like sunlight in the sun. But no matter how many steps you go down from spirit, you never get dust. Thin spirit only makes thinner spirit. Matter, a real "something," needs a different kind of act: something from nothing. In Hebrew that is called *beriah*, creation.

A created thing is two things at once, the letter says. Seen from above, from His knowing, it is nothing at all, melted into the light that makes it. Seen from below, from our knowing, it is completely separate. It doesn't know the power that makes it, and it is nothing like that power. It isn't even like an effect and its cause, which at least resemble each other. That is why it is called "something from nothing."

Who can do this? Only He. His being is from Himself. Nothing came before Him to cause Him. So only He, by His own power, can create something out of absolute nothing, with no cause before it. His light is "like its source," and so it can do the same. (The letter maps all this onto the sefirot, the worlds, and the "line" of light, at length. The point: the power to make something from nothing is His very essence, clothed in vessels so that it can make limited things.)

Why did He make things that feel separate? So that He could be King over them. "There is no king without a people." A king needs subjects who are not himself and who choose to do his word. That is why the mitzvot, which are bodily, are the root of the Torah, which is wisdom. The letter's picture: the placenta forms first from the seed, and for forty days it is the main thing, before the child has a shape. So the deed is the root, and the wisdom grows from it. "Whoever learns without meaning to do, it would be better had the placenta been turned over his face."

Then the most beautiful part. Where does His power to make something from nothing show most? In the plain earth. The earth brings up grass and trees out of nothing, every year, forever. The stars can't do that. The rabbis say every blade of grass has a star that tells it "grow." But that is only after it has sprouted: from small to big. Before it sprouts, who would the star even talk to? The earth's power to grow is itself "nothing," spiritual, and what comes up is solid. Why the lowest element? Because at the very bottom of all the worlds, right under the "feet" of the first spiritual form, the Infinite that surrounds all shines with almost nothing in between.

A seed you plant becomes a whole tree, fruit and all. A single grain becomes hundreds. That too is like something from nothing. And the fruit a person plants is better than what grows wild, because a person's work below draws the highest lights down, which is why people were created. We who speak live on animals, animals live on plants, plants on earth. A baby can't say "Abba" and "Imma" until it tastes grain. The lower feeds the higher.

So, the letter ends, understand how great the mitzvot done in deed are. They are the whole reason the soul came down into this physical world. "One hour of return and good deeds in this world is better than all the life of the World to Come."

The plainest thing, a sprout in the dirt, points most directly to Him. Only the One who simply is, with no cause, can make a real "something." Every solid thing in front of you is being made that way, now.

**Sit with it** *(7 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Look at something ordinary: a plant, a slice of bread, the ground outside. Don't try to feel anything special about it. - **The line, softly:** "Something from nothing. Only He." - **Think it through.** A chain of causes can only make more of what it already is. Spirit makes thinner spirit, never a stone. So this bread, this leaf, has to come from the One whose being is His own. From above, it is nothing. From here, it is solid. Both are true. - **Where the picture breaks.** A farmer plants and the earth does the rest. But the earth's power to sprout is itself His word, "Let the earth sprout," still speaking. No one is doing the rest but Him. - **From inside.** Wonder at plain things. The more ordinary, the straighter it points. - **The edge.** "His being is from Himself." The mind can say those words and can't go inside them. The letter puts it in the soil instead. Leave it there. - **Into the day.** Water a plant or look at grass today and say: "Something from nothing. Only He." Then do one good deed with your hands.

## Iggeret HaKodesh 21 · Little by little

This letter goes to the people who give a set amount each year to support the poor of the Holy Land. It asks them to give it every week, or at least every month, rather than once a year.

First, being quick with any mitzvah is always good. The letter says something surprising about Abraham. The binding of Isaac was not such a great test for someone of his level. Many holy people gave their lives for G-d without G-d ever speaking to them, and G-d had spoken to Abraham directly. What made it great was his speed. He got up early and went with wonderful quickness, to show how glad he was to do his Maker's will. From him we learn to be quick with every mitzvah, and above all with charity, which shields a person from harm. "Charity saves from death," and surely from smaller troubles. A person is judged every day, so it is good to give as early as you can.

But charity has a special quality of its own. It is better done many times, even if the total is the same. The Rambam says: a thousand gifts of one coin are better than one gift of a thousand coins, because many acts refine the soul.

The letter adds a deeper reason. "The work of charity is for life." Giving draws higher life down, from the Life of all life, to the "land of life," which is the Shechinah (G-d's presence in the world, the power that gives life to everything). The Shechinah is "the fallen booth of David," fallen to the dust. When the people went into exile, she went with them. When you give life to a poor person, "to revive the spirit of the lowly," who has nothing of his own, that wakes the same above, and the Shechinah is given life too. Each gift makes a union above. So many gifts make many unions.

And that is also what the Rambam meant by "refining the soul." The Zohar calls the Shechinah "soul," because she is our life. "Our soul is bowed down to the dust." That is why the rabbis said, "Great is charity, for it brings the redemption near." Each gift raises her from the dust, little by little, "until Shiloh comes."

G-d's presence in the world is not far off and untouchable. She can fall, and she can be lifted, and your small, often-repeated kindness is what lifts her.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Notice how you usually give: in a big once-a-year gift, or a little at a time, or not much at all. No judging. Just notice. - **The line, softly:** "To raise her from the dust, little by little." - **Think it through.** The Shechinah is fallen, in exile with us. Each coin is a hand under her arm. One lift is good. Many lifts are many unions. - **Where the picture breaks.** You lift a fallen person with your arms. Here you lift her by giving to someone who has nothing, which seems to have nothing to do with her. It has everything to do with her. - **From inside.** Patience. Redemption comes like lifting someone slowly from the ground. - **The edge.** The letter does not go past this. It stays with the deed and the Shechinah in exile. Stay there too. - **Into the day.** This week, give a small amount every day instead of once.

## Iggeret HaKodesh 22 · Face to face; one body

These are two letters that begin the same way: "My beloved ones, my brothers and friends."

The first is a rebuke, and it says so: "From hidden love comes open rebuke." Some people had begun traveling from town to town to ask the Rebbe for advice about worldly things: business, money, what to do. Where, the letter asks, did you find this custom in any book? Even the greatest sages of the Talmud were never asked such things. Only the prophets were, like Shmuel, whom Saul asked about his father's lost donkeys. Everything in a person's life, except Torah and the fear of Heaven, can only be known by prophecy. "Everything is in the hands of Heaven, except the fear of Heaven."

The Rebbe says why people do it. They love the body's life, for the sake of heaven, so they can serve G-d with it. And that love blinds them. When the body suffers, they can't bear it. So they run far away for advice, instead of turning to G-d in humility to come back to Him and accept His rebuke with love. "For the one G-d loves, He rebukes."

The letter's picture: a father who is kind, wise, and good strikes his son. A wise son does not turn his back and run away to find help, or even someone to plead for him. He turns his face to his father, face to face, and bears it with love, knowing it is for his good.

Above, "face" means will and desire. Our Father in heaven gives His children every good thing, life for soul and body, with love and desire, through the Torah, which is His will. (Those who serve idols, the letter says, also get their bodily life from Him, but without that desire; that is why their gods are called "other gods," from the Hebrew word for "back.") It is the same with a person: to accept with love and desire is to turn your face. Not to accept is like turning your back.

How do you accept with love? The rabbis' counsel: look into your deeds. You will find things that need cleansing. Then you will see how great His love for you is. It is like a great and awesome king who washes the dirt off his only son with his own hands. When you see that love, your own love wakes, "as water reflects a face." And then His face shines back openly, with the love that was hidden inside the rebuke. The hard things are sweetened at their root.

The second letter is short; the Rebbe says he is too busy to write all he feels. He asks them to hold fast to prayer, out loud, against everything that gets in the way, with a simple will that is above reason. "For it is a stiff-necked people, and You will forgive." Forgiveness too is above reason. He asks them to walk simply and not search out "many calculations." Be humble before every person, because each one has something the other lacks. "All Israel are like one man." One body is made of many limbs, and when they are torn apart the heart feels it. So work with all your heart to plant love for your friend in your heart. Never let a bad thought about another person come up. If it does, push it away like smoke, the way you would push away a thought of idolatry. Speaking badly of others is as grave as the worst sins, and thought is even stronger than speech, for good and for bad.

*The Tanya speaks here of the Jewish people and of those who serve idols; this adaptation applies its teaching to every person.*

Pain is not a sign that He has turned away. It is the one place you can choose to turn toward Him, and find that His face was toward you all along. And the others around you are not separate from you; you are limbs of one body, with one heart.

**Sit with it** *(7 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Let one thing that hurts right now be there. Don't explain it. Don't argue with it. - **The line, softly:** "Face to face with my Father." - **Think it through.** A kind and wise father. A son who doesn't run, but turns toward him. A king washing his only child with his own hands, because he loves him. - **Where the picture breaks.** With a human father you can often see the lesson. Here you often can't. The letter doesn't ask you to explain the pain. It asks only that your face stay turned toward Him. - **From inside.** Turning toward instead of away. Something unclenches. What looked like a rebuke starts to show the love inside it. - **The edge.** Forgiveness is above reason, and so is the simple will it asks you to bring. Don't calculate. Just turn. - **Into the day.** When something hurts today, talk to G-d about it in your own words before you go to anyone else. And when a bad thought about someone comes, let it go like smoke.

## Iggeret HaKodesh 23 · Surrounded, head to foot

"Ten who sit and learn Torah, the Shechinah rests among them." The letter says this is the whole of what a person is for. The soul came down into this world for this rising, and there is no rising higher.

Think of it. The Shechinah, whose awe "the heavens and the heavens of heavens cannot hold," comes to live among the people, when they learn and do mitzvot in a group of ten. "Nothing holy happens with fewer than ten."

The rabbis also found a verse for one person learning alone. But look, the letter says: for one person, all they found was a reward. For the resting of G-d's holiness, one person is no match at all for ten.

What is the difference between a reward and a resting? The reward is G-d's light shining into the soul of the one who seeks Him, through the Torah, which is His garment. ("He wraps Himself in light like a coat.") A soul has limits in all its powers, so the light that shines into it is limited too. It fits inside. That is why people who seek G-d are moved in prayer. Their hearts are glad, they feel sweetness in G-d's light, it flashes out like lightning. That is the reward of the Torah, set into the soul that works at it.

But the resting on ten is a huge light, without any limit. It can't fit inside a limited soul. It can only surround the soul from above, from its head to its feet. "Let the pleasantness of G-d be upon us, and establish the work of our hands upon us." Upon us, not inside. That is why we don't feel it. We can't grasp the sweetness of the Shechinah that rests on us when many of us learn and do mitzvot together. "There is no reward for a mitzvah in this world," because it can't be grasped here at all, only when the soul leaves the body.

The Rebbe says he heard from his teachers: if an angel stood among ten Jews together, even if they weren't learning, such awe would fall on it that it would cease to exist.

And so the letter grieves. Before and after prayer, people sit together and joke. Where ten are gathered and the Shechinah rests on them, there is no greater insult to her. Yet she stays, like "a king bound in the tresses." The letter asks them to learn together, in tens, between the afternoon and evening prayers: the stories and teachings of the Talmud (the book Ein Yaakov), a little of the practical law every person needs, and on Shabbat afternoon the laws of Shabbat. No one should separate himself from the group.

*The Tanya speaks here of Jews gathered in tens; this adaptation applies the teaching to every person.*

The greatest thing present may be the thing you can't feel. What you feel is sized to you. What surrounds you, head to foot, has no size.

**Sit with it** *(6 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of the last time you prayed or learned with others and felt nothing much. Let that be true. - **The line, softly:** "Surrounded, head to foot, whether I feel it or not." - **Think it through.** A small light fits inside me, and I feel it: warmth, joy, a flash. A light without limit can't fit inside. It stands around me. An angel would vanish in it. I sit in it and notice nothing. - **Where the picture breaks.** Being surrounded usually means pressure you can feel. This surrounding is too great to press on anything. - **From inside.** Not measuring by feeling. A quiet trust that more is here than you can register. - **The edge.** The surrounding light can't be grasped in this world. Stop trying to feel it. That is the respect it asks. - **Into the day.** Join others for prayer or learning this week (a minyan, a class, a group reading Psalms), and as you sit, think: "Surrounded, head to foot."

## Iggeret HaKodesh 24 · The King's hour

"My beloved brothers, do no harm." The letter is about talking during prayer.

Prayer is the hour G-d chose out of the whole day, the time He wants to be revealed. People gather in the "small sanctuary," the shul, to visit His presence, which "dwells with them even in their impurity," and He lets Himself be found by those who seek Him.

Someone who talks about his own affairs then is showing that he doesn't want to look. He doesn't want to see the glory of the King, which is revealed above at that hour, and below too, to anyone who wants to look. That glory is "wrapped and clothed inside the words of the prayer, set in everyone's mouth." The same printed words for everyone. And the glory shows itself to each person according to his understanding and the root of his soul. "A man is praised by his understanding."

The picture: the kingdom of heaven is like a kingdom on earth. A king usually hides in inner rooms, with guards at every door. People wait days and years to see him. Then one day he decides to show himself to everyone. He sends word through his kingdom: come, stand before me, see my glory. And someone comes and stands there, not bothering to look, busy with his own business. How foolish. And worse, it is an insult to the king, in front of everyone. It says: looking at you gives me less pleasure than my own affairs.

That is why the rabbis said to pray "as if standing before the King." At the very least, the letter says, look like it on the outside, even if your mind isn't there. The Zohar says the one who talks in prayer "shows separation," as if something could be apart from the One.

So the Rebbe makes a rule for everyone: no idle talk from when the prayer leader begins until the last Kaddish. Someone who breaks it on purpose should sit on the ground and ask three people to release him, and he will be healed. The rule was never meant for someone who forgot, or let a few words slip.

The ordinary prayer book, the same words everyone says, is where He wraps Himself. Nothing is apart from the One, and talking through it acts as if something were.

Plain words, printed for everyone. - **The line, softly:** "He is wrapped in these words." - **Think it through.** The King hides all day in inner rooms. For this one hour He calls everyone in. He is inside the words themselves, and He shows Himself to each person by what that person can understand. - **Where the picture breaks.** A human king is in one room, and you have to travel to him. This King comes to wherever the words are said. - **From inside.** The siddur feels inhabited. The same words you've said a thousand times have someone in them. - **The edge.** The letter stays in the King's hall and goes no further. So do you: look, and don't look away. - **Into the day.** At one prayer today, stop at one word and think: "He is wrapped here." And keep your mouth for the words until the end. (Anyone can do this with a psalm or their own prayer.)

## Iggeret HaKodesh 25 · His breath is in him right now

This letter explains a saying of the Baal Shem Tov, from a book called "the Will of the Rivash." The Rebbe says first that it isn't really a will. It is a collection of his sayings, gathered by people who didn't always get the wording right. But the meaning is true.

It starts from a hard saying of the rabbis: "Whoever gets angry is like one who serves idols." Why? Because at the moment of anger, faith has left. If he believed that this came to him from G-d, he wouldn't be angry at all.

The person who curses you, hits you, or damages your things has free choice. He is guilty, before people and before Heaven, for choosing wrong. Even so, what happened to you was already decided above. "G-d has many messengers."

And more than that. At the very moment someone hurts you, G-d's power, the breath of His mouth, is inside him, keeping him alive. When Shimi cursed King David, David said, "G-d told him, curse." Where did G-d tell him? The thought that fell into Shimi's heart came from G-d, and the breath of His mouth was giving Shimi life as he spoke. If that breath left Shimi for one moment, he could not have said a thing.

This is how the Baal Shem Tov read the verse "Forever, G-d, Your word stands in the heavens." The words "Let there be a firmament" still stand in the sky, giving it life right now. Not like the philosophers, who think the world is like a pot a smith made: once it's made, it stands without him. That is making something from something. Making something from nothing is more like the splitting of the sea. G-d blew a strong wind all night, and the water stood like a wall. If the wind stopped for a second, the water would fall back. So too, if the Creator's power left a created thing, it would go back to nothing. Even the dust and stones have this word inside them, a kind of soul and life.

That word is called the Shechinah, because it "dwells" in creatures to give them life. (The letter explains how it comes down through the four worlds, changing only in how much it is hidden, never in itself. It also says, in a long aside, that outside the Holy Land the life of the nations comes through angelic "princes," with only a spark of G-d's word shining on them from above; that the life in them is in exile, which is why the rabbis call their gods "other gods"; that this is the exile of the Shechinah, which will end when "I will turn to the peoples a clear language, so all call on the name of G-d." It says the souls of the nations are drawn from the "other side," and that they draw life only from the "back" of holiness. The letter also notes that a person's power of speech mirrors G-d's speech, which is why words of Torah must be said aloud.)

Now back to anger. All of this is about worldly things. When someone is doing something forbidden and you can stop him, that is different; Moshe got angry for that.

But what if you can't stop it? A stranger stands near you talking loudly while you pray. He is confusing you. Why did G-d do this? Only so that you will push harder in your prayer, from deeper in your heart, until you don't hear him at all. That takes a great effort. And the way to wake it is this very thought: the Shechinah came down, as it were, so far that a spark of her light is clothed, in exile, in this man's words that disturb your prayer. "Man rules over man, to his harm": to wake the one praying to pray from deeper.

The Rebbe says why some people object to all this. It isn't about the wording. They don't believe the Shechinah clothes itself even in the husks. But there is nothing more physical than the dust of the earth, and G-d's word is clothed there. This isn't hidden Kabbalah, he says. It is the plain faith of the verse "Do I not fill heaven and earth?" The simple faith passed down from our ancestors, who walked with G-d without trying to work out with human reason how He fills the whole world. That is above reason, without end. He can't explain it well in a letter. If they want, let them send one person and he will explain it mouth to ear.

*The Tanya speaks here of the Jewish people, the nations, and a non-Jew who disturbs prayer; this adaptation applies its teaching to every person.*

Nothing that happens to you comes from outside Him. The one who hurts you is being kept alive by His breath at that very moment. When the other person stops being a separate power, anger has nothing left to hold.

**Sit with it** *(7 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Bring to mind someone who angered you lately. Let the heat be there. Don't argue with it. - **The line, softly:** "His breath is in him right now." - **Think it through.** "Let there be a firmament," still standing in the sky. The sea held up by the wind, all night. This person, right now, held in being by the same word. If it left him for a moment, he couldn't say a thing. - **Where the picture breaks.** The wind that split the sea was outside the water. The word that keeps this person alive is inside him, closer than his own breath. - **From inside.** The grip loosens. Where the heat was, often a strange quiet. - **The edge.** How He fills heaven and earth, even the coarsest place, the letter says not to work out with human reason. It is simple faith, above the mind. Stop there. - **Into the day.** When someone angers you today, say inside: "His breath is in him right now." If someone's noise disturbs your prayer, pray one line from deeper.

## Iggeret HaKodesh 26 · The law and the husk

A passage in the Zohar seems to say something shocking. It says that learning "forbidden and permitted, pure and impure" belongs to "the tree of good and evil," while the Zohar itself is "the tree of life." At first glance, it sounds as if studying the practical law were from the side of evil.

The letter proves that can't be what it means. The whole revealed Torah is called "a tree of life." The Zohar was hidden in the rabbis' days. Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai himself, who wrote the Zohar, studied the Mishnah in his cave and could answer every question twenty-four ways. The rabbis say that since the Temple fell, G-d has in His world only "the four cubits of the law." And even in the days of Moshiach people will still need the laws: how to slaughter, what makes things impure. (The letter spends several paragraphs on these proofs.)

So what does it mean? Read closely, the Rebbe says. It doesn't say "the study of forbidden and permitted." It means the forbidden thing and the permitted thing themselves. The objects belong to the mixed realm of good and evil, the in-between husk called *nogah*, which is this world.

The Hebrew words tell it. *Assur*, "forbidden," means tied: the husk holds it and it can't rise. *Mutar*, "permitted," means untied: it can rise. When a person eats permitted food for G-d's sake, or simply eats and then learns and prays with the strength it gave him, the food rises. It becomes letters of Torah and prayer going up to G-d. That is on weekdays. On Shabbat even the mixed realm rises, so it is a mitzvah to enjoy meat and wine, which on a weekday would be gluttony. A forbidden thing can't rise, on Shabbat or weekday, unless it was eaten to save a life, which makes it fully permitted.

But the learning of those laws is something else entirely. It is the Oral Torah, which is the word of G-d, one with Him. And when the Shechinah goes down into the mixed realm to gather the sparks that fell there, she is, as it were, in exile. So in exile the main work of Torah and mitzvot is to sort: to clear what is permitted and pure from what is forbidden and impure. When a person works hard to understand a law, he lifts it out of the husk that hid it. The hard question itself, the Zohar says, comes "from the side of evil," because the husk was hiding the wisdom.

Only people in bodies can do this. The angels can't. We are dressed in physical bodies from that same mixed realm, and when we break our cravings, the other side is weakened. That is why the angels come to hear new Torah from people below. And every person can uncover new wisdom, in law or story, open or hidden, each by the root of his soul. He is obligated to, to lift the sparks that fell to his share. "Whoever studies Torah, G-d says, it is as if he redeemed Me and My children from among the nations."

After the sorting is done, the Torah will be about its inner side: higher unions, the deeper reasons for the mitzvot, "for the root of the mitzvot is very high, in the Infinite." (The letter also says that then the laws will be known to all without study, except to those who have not yet tasted the tree of life, who will still need them.)

*The Tanya speaks here of Jewish souls, the "mixed multitude," and the nations; this adaptation applies its teaching to every person.*

The coarsest part of life, food and the fine print of the law, is where the hidden sparks are waiting. Nothing permitted is too low to rise. Eating, then praying with that strength, turns the world back into His word.

**Sit with it** *(6 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of your last meal. What you ate, how fast, what you did after. - **The line, softly:** "May this become words of prayer." - **Think it through.** Forbidden means tied. Permitted means free to rise. The bread I eat gives me strength. When I use that strength to pray, learn, or do good, the bread becomes those words. A hard question in the law is a spark hiding in a husk. Working it out sets it free. - **Where the picture breaks.** Normally food turns into body and that's the end of it. Here food can end up above the body, in words that go to G-d. - **From inside.** Your meal matters. Your small piece of learning matters, even to the angels. - **The edge.** When the sorting is finished, the Torah will be about its inner side, "the root of the mitzvot, very high, in the Infinite." We aren't there. Do the sorting in front of you. - **Into the day.** Before eating today, say: "May the strength from this become words of Torah and prayer." Then use it for one good thing.

## Iggeret HaKodesh 27 · What a teacher leaves behind

This letter went to the Chassidim in the Holy Land, to comfort them on the passing of Rabbi Menachem Mendel of Vitebsk, the Rebbe's own teacher and friend. "I came to speak to the heart of the broken ones who sigh and groan," he writes, "and to comfort them double."

The rabbis say of a tzaddik (a truly righteous person) that when he died "he left life to all the living." What life? "The righteous live by their faith." "The fear of G-d is for life." "One who pursues kindness will find life," and kindness is love. So the life of a tzaddik isn't a life of flesh. It is spiritual life: faith, awe, and love.

While the tzaddik was alive, those three lived inside a vessel, a body in one place. His students got only the glow that shone outside that vessel, through his words and his holy thoughts. That is why "no one grasps his teacher's mind" fully.

After his passing, it's different. His spirit, which is his faith, awe and love, is no longer in a vessel or a place. So everyone close to him can receive a part of the thing itself, not just its glow. The letter adds: the air of Gan Eden spreads around every person, and every good thought and word is recorded in it. And nothing holy is ever fully uprooted from where it was, even after it rises. The part of him that stays below is what spreads into his students, each one according to how bound to him he was, in his life and after, with great love. Spiritual things are drawn only by great love, and by real preparation and effort to take on these three traits the way the teacher taught. "If you toiled and found, believe it."

There is a second light too. It does not come inside the students' minds. It shines on them from above. As the tzaddik's soul rises to its source, all the Torah and work of his whole life rise with it, and very high lights are "sown" in response. That light shines down on everyone who became a servant of G-d through him. It brings thoughts of return and good deeds into their hearts. The good deeds born of it are "growths of growths."

This light is very hidden. It is like the sun lighting the stars from under the earth. The Tikkunim says this about Moshe: after his passing, his light spreads in every generation to six hundred thousand souls, like the sun shining from below the earth on the stars.

So "a tzaddik who has passed is found in all the worlds more than in his life," even in this world. His work keeps growing. "And we, all of us here today, are alive in his ways." In worldly things too, the Zohar says, the righteous protect the world more after their passing than in their lives.

What was truly alive in someone we loved (the faith, the awe, the love) was never made of flesh, so it didn't end with the flesh. It is spread out now, and it can come into us as much as we are bound to it.

**Sit with it** *(6 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of someone you learned from who is gone. Let the missing be there. - **The line, softly:** "Their life was faith, awe, and love, and it is not gone." - **Think it through.** While they lived, you got the glow that came out through their words. Now the thing itself isn't held in one body in one place. The more you loved them and walk their way, the more of it can come into you. And a hidden light shines on you from above, like the sun lighting the stars from under the earth. - **Where the picture breaks.** You can't see the sun at night. You can see the stars. Here you often can't see either one. You only notice, later, a good thought that came from somewhere. - **From inside.** Grief with a thread in it. What you loved is not gone, but spread out. - **The edge.** The light from above "does not come into the mind." The letter points to it and does not open it. Leave it closed. - **Into the day.** Do one thing today the way a teacher you loved did it.

## Iggeret HaKodesh 28 · Hidden work comes to light

The Rebbe wrote this to Rabbi Levi Yitzchak of Berditchev, his relative by marriage, to comfort him when his son, Rabbi Meir, died.

The rabbis ask why the Torah puts Miriam's death right after the law of the red heifer. They answer: just as the red heifer atones, so the death of the righteous atones. But why the red heifer, which was burned outside the camp? Why not the sin offering, brought inside, on the altar, which is a real atonement?

The letter explains with Kabbalah. The offerings on the altar lift up the animal soul, the natural life in a person, to its root in the heavenly chariot. That is why they atone only for mistakes, sins that come from the animal soul getting too strong.

The red heifer is different. Its ashes are mixed with cedar, hyssop and living water, and the water is then called "sanctified." That holiness comes from the highest, most hidden wisdom, the place where "darkness is turned into light," where the broken world is repaired. So it purifies even the impurity of death, the strongest impurity of all, lower even than the mixed realm.

Now the tzaddikim. Every morning and every night, in the Shema, they gave their souls to G-d with love. And in their learning too. All that effort went up, hidden. It drew down a kindness from the highest will. And "all the toil a person toiled with his soul in his life, above, in hiding, is revealed and shines openly, from above to below, at the time of his passing."

When that hidden kindness shines at their passing, it brings kindness and salvation into the world. It atones for the sins of the generation, even deliberate ones, the ones from below the mixed realm. Because it comes from the source of all repair, where the darkness of the broken world turns into the light of the mended one. That is why Miriam's death sits next to the red heifer.

Nothing done for G-d in hiding is lost. The deepest, most hidden work of a life comes out at the end as a light that reaches even the darkest place, where darkness itself turns into light.

**Sit with it** *(6 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of some good you did that no one saw. Or someone whose quiet effort no one noticed. - **The line, softly:** "Hidden toil is revealed." - **Think it through.** The red heifer, burned outside the camp, purifies the worst impurity. Its power comes from the most hidden wisdom, where darkness turns to light. A righteous person's daily giving of their soul, unseen, is stored above, and at the end it shines down and heals. - **Where the picture breaks.** We can't see the showing. The heifer's ashes could be seen; this light can't. The text says it happens. We trust it. - **From inside.** A quiet trust that unseen effort counts. - **The edge.** It reaches "the hidden mind," the highest level, above the usual order. The letter names it and says no more. - **Into the day.** Do one good thing today in secret, and tell no one.

## Iggeret HaKodesh 29 · Hollow pillars

"A woman of valor is the crown of her husband." The Talmud calls the laws of the Torah its "crown." It says whoever learns laws every day is promised the World to Come. And one chapter in the morning and one at night is enough to keep the command to learn. Why the laws, and not any Torah?

The Arizal taught that each person has to come back, life after life, until he has done all 613 mitzvot, in thought, speech and deed. The mitzvot make the garments of the soul, and none of the 613 powers of the soul may be left without its garment.

Why does the soul need garments? Because the soul is created. And no created thing can grasp the Creator. Even after G-d's light comes down through many, many levels, the soul still can't bear it. The light is too good, too sweet. The verse calls it "the pleasantness of G-d." A soul that took it in directly would go out of its sheath and stop existing, "like a candle before a torch."

So from that very light, a little comes down, level after level, until a garment is made from it: a created garment, but of the same kind as the light. Wearing it, the soul can enjoy the light and not vanish. Like looking at the sun through clear, bright glass. Like Moshe, who "went into the cloud": he was clothed in the cloud, and saw through it.

That garment comes from the outer side of the light. What is the outer side? The letter's picture comes from us. Your soul has a power of delight: you delight in a new idea, for example. And the outer side of delight is will. You want what you want because it gives you delight, not pain. It is the same, in a way, above. G-d's will is the outer side of His delight, which is "the pleasantness of G-d." (Only in a way: He and His will are truly one, and nothing like our will. The Torah speaks in human language.) You can see that delight is higher than the mind: when you understand something new and wonderful, delight is born in that moment. The delight was above the idea, wearing it.

G-d's will is called the "Supernal Crown." A crown sits around the head, over the mind. And, the Kabbalists say, it has 620 pillars of light. The picture: pillars in a great house, standing on the floor with their tops joined to the ceiling. That will is clothed in the 613 mitzvot and the seven rabbinic ones, and almost all of them are done with the body. Even the mitzvot of speech count as deeds ("moving the lips is a deed"). Even the mitzvot of the heart were given only to a person in a body, who can choose.

So the mitzvot are like pillars reaching from the height of all levels, His will, down to this physical earth. And they are hollow. When you do a mitzvah, the pillar surrounds you, and through it your soul rises all the way up, "bound in the bundle of life," clothed in the light of His will. And wearing that, it can look at the "pleasantness of G-d," which is above the crown.

Now: the will in the Written Torah is hidden. It is revealed only in the Oral Torah. Take tefillin. The Written Torah says, "Bind them as a sign on your hand, and as *totafot* between your eyes." What to bind? How? Where exactly? Only the Oral Torah says: one box on the arm, four on the head, square, of leather, with black straps, on the upper arm, above the hairline. Or Shabbat: "Do no work." What is work? Only the Oral Torah says: the thirty-nine kinds, not carrying heavy beams. Every mitzvah is sealed until the Oral Torah opens it.

That is why the Oral Torah is called "your mother's teaching." Like a baby, whose every limb is hidden in the father's seed, and the mother brings it out, whole. So the Oral Torah brings the will out into the open. She is "the woman of valor" who gives birth to countless laws. She is "the crown of her husband," the Written Torah, because the will she reveals is higher than wisdom, like a crown over the head. That is why the laws are called the crown, and why whoever learns them is promised the World to Come: his soul is dressed in the supernal will.

*The Tanya speaks here of the Jewish soul and its 613 mitzvot; this adaptation applies its teaching to every person.*

You can't take in G-d's light directly; you would vanish like a candle before a torch. So He made the mitzvah a pillar from His highest will down to your floor, hollow, so you could stand inside it and rise.

**Sit with it** *(7 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of one mitzvah you do with your body: tefillin, Shabbat candles, a blessing on food, giving a coin. - **The line, softly:** "A pillar from His will down to my floor." - **Think it through.** No creature can grasp Him. His light is too sweet to survive. So a little of that very light became a garment, like clear glass in front of the sun. The mitzvah is a pillar in a great house, standing on the floor with its top joined to the ceiling. It is hollow. I step inside and the soul rises. - **Where the picture breaks.** A real pillar holds up a roof. This one holds you up, and the "roof" is His will, which is one with Him. - **From inside.** The mitzvah feels like a place you can stand inside. - **The edge.** Above the crown is His delight, "the pleasantness of G-d." The garment is what lets the soul look at it without vanishing. Don't try to look without it. - **Into the day.** While doing one mitzvah today, picture standing inside a pillar that reaches from your floor to His highest will. (For anyone: any kindness you do as His will, not your own idea.)

## Iggeret HaKodesh 30 · The joint between endless and counted

The rabbis say: if someone always comes to shul, and one day he doesn't, G-d asks after him. "Who among you fears G-d?"

It's the same with every mitzvah, the letter says, and most of all with charity, which weighs as much as all the others. Maybe you never took a vow to give. Still, if the fear of G-d has touched your heart, your Godly soul won't let you give less to holiness than you have been giving every year. Your giving revives "the spirit of the lowly and broken, who have nothing of their own." It lifts "the fallen booth of David." It raises her, so that she can "be one with the One."

"Everything goes by the number of deeds," the rabbis say, and "each penny joins into a great sum." What is this great sum? The rabbis also say: when is "G-d great"? When He is "in the city of our G-d." The "city" is the place of counting, where things are numbered.

Here is what it means. When you give with good will and a warm face, you draw life, grace and kindness down. That wakes the same above. "May G-d make His face shine." Grace and kindness come down from the Life of all life, the Infinite, whose greatness can't be searched or grasped at all, into the realm that gives life to every created thing. And that realm is the world of number and counting: "a thousand thousands serve Him."

So the many acts of charity make "peace." Peace means the thing that joins two opposite ends and stands between them. One end is the top of the heavens: "His greatness has no search." The other end is the bottom of the heavens, clothed in the created worlds: limit and number. Your coin is the place where the two meet.

The endless and the counted aren't two separate worlds. In one small, steady gift they meet, and that meeting is peace.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of the regular amount you give, or the regular good thing you do. Maybe it has gotten smaller lately. Just notice. - **The line, softly:** "My coin is where the endless meets the counted." - **Think it through.** His greatness has no end. The world is made of numbers: this many coins, this many days. Each penny joins a great sum. In that sum, the greatness without limit rests in the place of counting. - **Where the picture breaks.** A joint between two boards holds two things of the same kind. Here the two ends have nothing in common, and still they meet. - **From inside.** Steadiness. Keeping a habit becomes a way of holding two ends together. - **The edge.** "His greatness cannot be searched." Don't search it. Let it rest on the small number in your hand. - **Into the day.** Give your usual amount today, not less, with a warm face. Hold it as the joint between endless and counted.

## Iggeret HaKodesh 31 · The body's blood stops moving

The Tikkunim says, "The Shechinah is sick in exile." What does that mean?

The letter explains by a picture of a sick body. What makes a body healthy or sick? Life spreads from the heart to every limb, carried in the blood. The spirit of life and the blood go round and round, through every limb and every vein inside them, and come back to the heart. When that circling goes as it should, the person is fully healthy, because every limb is tied to the heart and gets the life it needs. But if something anywhere blocks the flow, or slows it, the tie between the limbs and the heart is cut or weakened. Then the person falls sick.

It is exactly like that above. The souls are called "the limbs of the Shechinah," and the Shechinah is called "the heart." ("Shechinah" means that G-d's light dwells in the worlds to give them life.) And that life comes to the worlds first through the souls. All created things are nothing next to the Creator, and on their own they couldn't take in His light and exist. They get it through the souls, which "rose in His thought" before the world was made by His word. "With whom did G-d consult? With the souls of the righteous."

*The Tanya speaks here of the souls of Israel as the Shechinah's limbs; this adaptation applies its teaching to every person.*

## Iggeret HaKodesh 32 · A wick for the light to come

The letter blesses the givers. May G-d bless their strength and the work of their hands, and may generous people keep rising, so that "the one who gets others to give" is great in every town and every group.

"His charity stands forever." The Hebrew word for "stands" is feminine. Feminine means receiving: charity receives the stirring of the giver's pure heart. And still, it "stands forever."

What does that mean? All the charity and kindness people do in this world, from the goodness of their hearts, stays alive in this physical world until the resurrection. Then will come the time when the Infinite Light, the kind that surrounds all worlds, is revealed here, in this world. (The Rebbe explained this at length the year before; that is Letter 17.)

But for the light to shine here, it needs a vessel to be clothed in. Like a body for a soul. "Is not My word like fire?" "Fire does not shine in this world unless it takes hold of a wick and wraps itself in it." The body, the vessel, the wick for His light is kindness: the heart's generosity, the will to give life to someone who has nothing. The Tikkunim calls kindness "the right arm," and the whole body is included in the right.

That is why the rabbis said, "Charity is paid only by the kindness in it," reading the verse "sow for yourselves in charity, reap by kindness." The harvest is the showing of what was sown and hidden in the ground. So the charity and kindness done in exile are hidden, sown, until the resurrection. Then the Infinite Light will wear them and shine in this physical world.

There are two words for charity. *Tzedakah*, feminine, is the charity that receives: it will take in the surrounding light at the resurrection. *Tzedek*, masculine, "walks before Him." It is the kindness that wakes in a person's heart by itself, from love of G-d in the Shema, from wanting to cling to Him and give his soul in "One," and "with all your might," which means, plainly, with your money. And "as water reflects a face," this stirring below draws the Infinite Light down into this physical world, openly, at the resurrection. That is "walks before Him": it leads the Face above all the way down to this world.

*The Tanya speaks here of the kindness that Jews do in exile; this adaptation applies its teaching to every person.*

The coming light will shine in this world, in bodies and things, and it will shine through what you give now. Your kindness isn't used up. It is laid down and kept, the wick His own light will take hold of.

## Kuntres Acharon 1 · Say it with your mouth

These notes go back over some chapters of the first part of the book. The first asks: how is someone who reads the Torah's stories bound to the highest wisdom?

The Arizal wrote that as a person does things below, so the "image of the upper man" does them above. That fits thought, the Rebbe says: thinking about the written letters. But speech does more. Spoken words break through and rise. With love and awe that come from understanding, they rise to the highest world. With natural love and awe, to a lower one. And even plain reading of Scripture rises from this world into the lowest spiritual world, because the voice "breaks through the air." Thought alone doesn't do this. It stays with the "image" above, the root of the soul.

The Zohar says, "Thought does nothing," even for good. What does that mean? Thought does not wake anything above to come down. It stays up there and adds great light up there. The main union is above. But the "fruits" in this world come from drawing a tiny bit of light down, and that happens through speech and deed. Thought alone draws nothing down. That is why a person who only thinks the Shema, without saying it, hasn't done the mitzvah. The whole reason his soul came down into this world was to draw the higher lights down.

Going up is a different matter. To lift things from below to above, you do need good thought. "Without love and awe, it doesn't fly up." Words said without love and awe still "break through the firmaments," the Zohar says; even idle talk does. But that means only the outer halls, not into the "body" of the upper man. Without love and awe, the Tikkunim says, a word can't rise and stand before G-d. (The note works this out through the worlds and the sefirot; that is its point.)

The soul came down so that light would come down with it. The mind can go up alone; only the mouth and the hand bring anything down into the world, where He wants it.

The mouth brings the light down. - **Where the picture breaks.** We think of speech as smaller than thought. Here, for drawing down, it is greater. - **From inside.** The mouth gets respect. Moving lips are part of the prayer. - **The edge.** The note speaks of "the image of the upper man" as the soul's root, and goes no further. ## Kuntres Acharon 2 · Deeds turn the face

The Arizal wrote that the upper union can return to "face to face" only through mitzvot done in deed. Why deeds, specifically?

Because a union above needs something to rise from below first. The Kabbalists call it "feminine waters," the rising that asks the flow to come down. And that rising belongs to the realm of action.

Good deeds are called "cutting away the thorns" that cling to the "back." The thorns are the husks that feed on the outer side of holiness. When a person does a mitzvah with a physical thing, he lifts the good hidden in it back to its source, and the thorns fall away.

The Arizal also says Adam repaired things through prayer. That fits: prayer is words, and "moving the lips is a deed." Spoken words come from the life-soul in the body and its blood, which is rooted in the mixed realm. So speech is also an act.

That is why "thought does nothing." Without a rising from below, from the physical, nothing can be drawn down for the union. (The note explains this with the Kabbalah of the worlds: the higher levels, left alone, only want to receive from above, not to give below.) And those who know the deep intentions of prayer, what lifts from below is their own souls, given over in their Torah and when they bow their faces in prayer.

The highest union depends on the lowest thing. A hand doing a mitzvah turns the face above toward the world.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Look at your hands. They have done many things today. - **The line, softly:** "The deed turns the face." - **Think it through.** Above, a union waits for something to rise from below. The deed is that rising. It lifts the good in the physical thing and cuts away the thorns that clung to the back. - **Where the picture breaks.** Turning a face is gentle. ## Kuntres Acharon 3 · The children's breath

The Arizal wrote that Torah learned without intention creates angels, in a lower world. The Zohar says no voice is lost except the voice of Torah and prayer, which rises and breaks through. Prayer said with intention makes angels in a higher world. But prayer without intention is pushed down.

So what is the difference between Torah and prayer without intention? The note says it is obvious. When you learn, you understand what you're learning; otherwise it isn't learning at all. What's missing is only the intention of doing it for G-d's sake, from love in the open. It is plain learning, from the love hidden in the heart by nature.

That is different from learning truly not for G-d's sake, in order to be made great. That learning doesn't rise "above the sun." The thought of self wraps itself around the words and won't let them go up.

Prayer with stray thoughts is also weak. But since its aim was heaven, it can be fixed easily. When a person prays one prayer with intention, it can gather up all his prayers of the year and lift them.

So: prayer that fell short is better than learning for self-greatness, which stays under the sun. But plain learning, not for self, just from the hidden natural love, is "no worse than the breath of schoolchildren." The children's breath rises because it is breath without sin. It rises even when they learn only out of fear of the teacher's strap. And the Zohar says the angels carry the children's breath all the way up to the highest world.

(The note spends some lines sorting out which "firmament" each kind of prayer reaches in the Zohar's map, and says even sins "rise" to some halls, but the risings have nothing in common except the name.)

What rises is not what you feel; it is what isn't wrapped in self. A tired, unmoved hour of learning, if it isn't for show, goes up like a child's breath.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of a day when you learned or prayed and felt nothing. Let it be as dry as it was. - **The line, softly:** "Breath without sin rises." - **Think it through.** Children learn because the teacher is watching. Their breath rises anyway, carried by angels. What holds words down is not dryness. It is learning to be made great. - **Where the picture breaks.** A child's breath is clean because the child hasn't sinned. Ours isn't clean in that way. But learning that isn't for show is clean in the way that counts here. - **From inside.** Relief for the dry days. Learning counts even when you feel nothing. - **The edge.** The angels carry it up to the highest world. The text leaves it there. - **Into the day.** Learn a few lines today when you're tired, simply, not for anyone to see, and let it rise.

## Kuntres Acharon 4 · Holding the etrog

This is the longest note, and much of it is technical Kabbalah. Its heart is one of the boldest turns in the whole Tanya.

It starts with a question. The Arizal says that in our time, the main work of refining the world is done through prayer, even though Torah study is higher than prayer. Why?

Torah and mitzvot add light inside the highest world. Torah adds it on the inside, mitzvot on the outside. But prayer does something else. It draws the Infinite Light itself down into the lower worlds, to change creatures from what they are: the sick person is healed, rain comes down. A mitzvah doesn't change the parchment of the tefillin when you put them on. And when a mitzvah does change something, a person does it, with his hands. Prayer asks for a change that only Heaven can make, from the One who alone can do everything. To draw that down, something must rise from below first: a fire in the mind and heart without limit, "with all your might."

Then the note turns. A mitzvah done in deed is, in truth, far higher than the love and awe that come from understanding. Why?

"To cling to Him," the Torah says, by clinging to His traits. But even with the traits, you cling only to the fact that they exist, not to what they are. "I am dust and ashes." All the more with the Infinite, whom no thought can grasp. Even the angels grasp only that He is, that He gives life to all, and not what He is. They say "Holy, holy, holy": set apart. A creature can't grasp anything of the Creator's essence. And without grasping, there is no true holding.

But a mitzvah is "the work of G-d." Take the etrog. Its life comes down, step by step, from the very essence of the highest vessels, which are one with the Infinite. ("He and His vessels are one.") So when you hold the etrog and wave it as the law says, you are truly holding the life clothed in it, which is one with the Infinite Light.

Your intention can't do that, even if you know the secret. It reaches only that the thing exists, not what it is. Learning the laws of the etrog does hold it, in speech and thought. Knowing how the worlds unfold is also a great mitzvah, even "above them all": "Know today and set it on your heart." It leads to a whole heart, which is the main thing, and it helps strip away a coarse picture of G-d. But it is one mitzvah of 613. A person has to keep all 613, in thought, speech and deed.

The note's picture for why. A spoken word of wisdom can't give birth. A drop of seed from the brain can, because the essence of the person is in it, not just a ray. Thought and speech, even understanding an idea, are only a glow and a garment of the mind. So love and awe from the mind only rise. They are like the angels. Only mitzvot in deed draw down. "The outside of the upper comes down, and the inside of the lower goes up." Both are needed.

And here is the purpose of everything. The whole chain of worlds is for the light above to be revealed below, not for the lower to rise up. That is only for a while. That is why Moshe begged, with 515 prayers, to enter the Land: so he could do the mitzvot that can be done only there.

But how can a plain etrog, or tefillin parchment, reach so high? Like sowing. A seed wakes the earth's power to grow, which is G-d's word, "Let the earth sprout." So the etrog and the parchment wake a power all the way up, higher even than the places they are drawn from. And when a person learns the laws, he draws the Infinite Light into this world, "to be included and nullified in His light. For this is the whole of man."

This, the note ends, was the work of Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai and all the sages in the revealed Torah: to draw His light down and to refine the world through all the long exile. "For this is the purpose of the whole chain: that the highest come down and have a home in the lowest, in order to raise them, to be one in One."

The mind can reach only that He is, never what He is. The hand holding a mitzvah holds what the mind can't: His life, one with Him, here in the lowest world, which is where He wanted to be.

**Sit with it** *(8 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of the best thought you ever had about G-d, and how far it really reached. - **The line, softly:** "Holding this, I hold what my mind can't." - **Think it through.** My understanding of Him, even full of love, touches only that He exists. "I am dust and ashes." The etrog's life comes down from the essence of the highest vessels, one with Him. Held as the law says, it is held. A thought is a glow. A deed carries the essence, like a seed. - **Where the picture breaks.** A seed makes something like its parent. The mitzvah doesn't make anything like Him. It lets the thing that is one with Him be held here. - **From inside.** The mind bows to the hand. Many find it restful: you don't have to understand in order to hold. - **The edge.** Here the mind's limit is stated plainly: existence, not essence. The deed goes past it. Don't try to follow with thought where only the hand goes. - **Into the day.** The next time you hold something for a mitzvah (tefillin, a candle, an etrog, a coin for charity), hold it a moment longer and say: "I'm holding it."

## Kuntres Acharon 5 · A hair of the King

Some laws are about cases that almost never happen, or maybe never happened, and surely won't in the future. For example, the fine details of an offering spoiled by a wrong thought. Why learn them?

Every forbidden thing in the world has a root of life in the husks. Otherwise it couldn't exist at all, because nothing exists without a flow from above. Even a man curling his hair out of vanity is getting his life, that moment, from the halls of the husks. So even forbidden things that never happened in this world have their roots, really existing, in those halls.

And the cases that could never happen, like an honest mistake no one ever made? Those may not have a root in the husks. (A later note by the Tzemach Tzedek says maybe they do.) But either way, they exist in the highest wisdom, which spread into every detail for Moshe at Sinai. Even what every faithful student will ever discover in the future.

Because the spreading of the highest wisdom is the Infinite, clothed in it in actual fact. And every detail of the law is a "hair" drawn from the highest wisdom, clothed in it, and from there coming down through all the worlds.

The husks feed on the outer garments of holiness. When you learn the laws, in speech and thought, the husk is separated from the holy and falls away, because you draw down the Infinite Light that is in that wisdom. And each soul must complete all 613, in thought, speech and deed, which are the details of the laws. That is why souls come back, again and again, to finish all the refining that belongs to them.

In the future, when the refining is finished, Torah will be only "do good": lifting the soul higher and higher without end. Even the prohibitions will be sweetened at their root, joined with the positive commandments. So the whole Torah is eternal, every detail of it.

No detail is too small to hold Him. The Infinite is clothed in the highest wisdom in fact, and every fine point of law is one hair of it, drawn from His head.

Every fine detail of law, even one that never happens, is drawn from the highest wisdom, and the Infinite is clothed in that wisdom. - **Where the picture breaks.** A cut hair is dead. This one never leaves its root. - **From inside.** Care with details stops feeling like fussiness. ## Kuntres Acharon 6 · David's songs

"Your statutes were songs to me," King David said. The rabbis say G-d punished him for it: "You call them songs?" The Zohar too speaks of "the praise of the Torah and its song." So what is the praise? What is praiseworthy to G-d in "this is forbidden, that is permitted"?

The note answers. It is like the verse "How great are Your works, G-d; Your thoughts are very deep."

All the worlds, high and low, hang on the fine point of one mitzvah. If an offering is brought properly, a union happens above, and all the worlds rise and receive their life. If the priest changed one thing, say he caught the blood in his left hand, or used the wrong vessel, the worlds' rising and their life are canceled. The same with tefillin: kosher, and the highest light comes down, the source of life for all worlds; one flaw, and it goes. The same with the fine points of the prohibitions.

So think about it. How great are His works: the many worlds and all their hosts. And they are all as nothing next to one fine point of the Torah, which is the depth of His thought and His wisdom. With one small detail all the worlds rise and get their life, or the reverse. From this you see the greatness of the depth of His thought, which has no limit, beyond the life of all the worlds, since all their life flows from one detail of it, like a hair drawn from a person's mind.

This was King David's joy. He sang to make his heart glad with Torah in his troubles.

Then why was he punished? Because this greatness, that all worlds are nothing before one detail, is only the "back" of the depth of His thought. "The Torah is the fallen fruit of the wisdom above." But the inside of the depth, the inner Torah, is completely one with the Infinite Light that is clothed in it. And before the Infinite, all the worlds are truly nothing. "You were the same before the world was created." So the inner Torah can't be praised at all by saying that the worlds depend on it. They don't even count.

The inner Torah isn't the joy of a human heart. It is, so to speak, the King's own joy and delight. "G-d understands its way and knows its place," in knowing Himself. It is "hidden from the eyes of all living." "My face will not be seen." "I was with Him, His delight, playing before Him": with Him, before Him, that is its inside. Its back is "playing in His inhabited world, my delight with the children of men." The Torah was given face and back.

Because David grasped the back, he was punished with forgetting, which comes from the back. He forgot for a moment that the holy ark must be "carried on the shoulder." The shoulders are the back, and carrying the ark on them joins the back to the face, to the holy work, the highest wisdom, where the tablets came from, written on both sides, with no front and back.

Next to one detail of His will, all the worlds are nothing. And next to Him, even that praise is too small, because the worlds aren't even something to compare with. Song first, then silence.

The comparison itself falls away. - **From inside.** Song, then a hush. The joy of how much a detail holds, then quiet before what is above even that. - **The edge.** The inner Torah is "hidden from the eyes of all living," His own delight, known in His knowing Himself. ## Kuntres Acharon 7 · The spark in exile

"Let charity flow like a mighty stream," the prophet Amos says. The Hebrew for "mighty" is *eitan*. The note reads it two ways.

First, a "mighty stream" is what flows from the source called "eitan," a hidden point in the highest wisdom. And the letters of *eitan* can be read as "I will be revealed." In the future, it says, the Infinite Light and His unity will be revealed inside the inner point of the heart. The stream will shine into the inside of the heart, and the heart will be nullified in His unity from its depths, once the coarse cravings that cover it are removed.

Now, in this exile, there is also counsel, a way to let a little of that light shine into the inner point of the heart, a taste of the future. It is this: wake great mercy, from above, for the Godly spark in your own soul.

Because in truth, as long as a person hasn't reached the point where His light shines into the inner point of his heart, where he is nullified in His unity from the depth of his heart until the soul almost expires, there is great reason for pity on the spark in his soul. The spark comes from the highest wisdom itself. If it can't shine into the inside of the heart, where it is meant to show, it is truly in exile.

Through great mercy from above, it comes out of exile and captivity, and the great love shines into the inner point of the heart. (The note points back to chapter 45 of the first part: Jacob's mercy that redeems Abraham's love.)

And it is known that the waking above depends on waking below. It depends on mercy waking in the hearts of merciful people who give physical things: gold and silver. So the act of charity is the "mighty stream" itself.

"All that a man has, he will give for his soul." For his Godly soul, to light it with the light of life, the Infinite.

There is a part of G-d in you that hasn't yet been able to shine into your own heart, and it is in exile. Pity for it, turned into a real gift to someone who needs it, is what lets its light in.

## Kuntres Acharon 8 · Prayer is the work now

"The news is not good," the Rebbe writes. "I heard, and I trembled." In one shul, people had removed a prayer leader because he prayed slowly. But the rabbis say three things lengthen a person's life, and one of them is lengthening your prayer. The prayer leader wants life and long days for everyone there.

Even someone in a real hurry, who can't wait until the leader's repetition reaches the Kedushah, should rather skip hearing it than cut into the lives of those who want to live. He is excused, and the leader fulfills the obligation for him, as if he had heard.

The Rebbe says he checked: even in the early generations of the Mishnah and Talmud, when Torah was people's steady work, prayer mattered. How much more now, in the "heels of Moshiach," when our Torah isn't steady, the times are hard, and the main service is prayer, as Rabbi Chaim Vital wrote.

So it is right to give our very souls for it. And for those who understand, it is a real Torah duty to think and contemplate a little, each by his measure, while saying the praises of G-d in the Verses of Song and the two blessings before the Shema. The aim is to wake the love hidden in the heart of all the people, so that it comes out into the open, felt in the heart, at the time of the Shema itself.

That is the mitzvah of love in the verse "And you shall love G-d with all your heart," the first of the 613 in the Rambam's count, the root of all the positive commands. It can't mean the hidden love, which is already in every one of us by nature, from birth. You can't command what is already there. It means bringing it out.

While the love is hidden, it is only in the Godly soul. When it comes out into the life-soul, it is felt in the heart, on the left side, where the life-soul lives. And that is what the Arizal means by "refining the sparks" in prayer. The life-soul is turned around, or at least bent. "The blood is the soul." And the blood is made new every day from food and drink, and affected by clothes and home.

In earlier generations, when souls were greater, this refining happened in a moment: the Shema and its blessings and a few short verses of song were enough. Now it takes longer.

*The Tanya speaks here of the love hidden in the heart of every Jew; this adaptation applies it to every person.*

The love for G-d is already in you, hidden. Prayer is the time it can come into the part of you that lives and wants and eats, until even that part turns toward Him.

**Sit with it** *(7 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Notice how fast you usually pray, or how rarely. Just notice. - **The line, softly:** "The hidden love can come out now." - **Think it through.** No one has to command me to have this love. It's in me by birth. What's asked is that it come out, into the heart that beats and gets hungry and tired. One thought held through the blessings before the Shema (for anyone: one psalm, said slowly) is how it comes out. - **Where the picture breaks.** "Taking longer" usually means less efficient. Here the longer prayer is the one that gives life to the others around it. - **From inside.** The Shema feels like the place where the hidden becomes open. - **The edge.** This note stays close to the heart and the prayer. The edge here is the heart opening, not what is beyond it. - **Into the day.** Take a few more minutes today before the Shema (or before your own prayer), and think one thought all the way through.

## Kuntres Acharon 9 · The quiet of Shabbat

"Rebuke your fellow, even a hundred times." So the Rebbe can't keep quiet. He cries out "in a weak voice": please, with great mercy, have pity on your souls. Take great care with Torah and with prayer, "the service of the heart." Start together, as one, word by word. Not one this way and one that way, one silent and one chatting.

The main cause, he says, is who leads the prayers. Anyone who wants to can grab the spot, or no one wants to. So here is a fixed rule, never to be broken: choose steady prayer leaders, suited for it, by lot or by the agreement of most of the group. They should pray word by word, at a middle pace, out loud, not too long and not rushed. Each one must lead on his day, and gather everyone around him praying at least a little aloud. (In the manuscript, the Rebbe wrote "Gevald, gevald": how long will this trip us up? Haven't we had enough troubles?)

Also: finish the whole Talmud every year, in every town, dividing the tractates by lot or by choice. Each learner should also finish, every week, the "eight faces" of Psalm 119.

And because this generation is weak, and people can't fast as they should for their sins, there is counsel from the rabbis: "Whoever keeps Shabbat according to its law is forgiven all his sins." According to its law, exactly. So every person must know the many laws of Shabbat well. And be very careful not to speak idle talk.

Then the last lines of the book. Every mitzvah has an inside and an outside. The outside of Shabbat is resting from physical work, as G-d rested from making the physical heaven and earth. The inside of Shabbat is the intention in Shabbat prayer and Torah study, to cling to G-d who is One. "A Shabbat for G-d your L-rd." That is "Remember."

And the inside of "Keep" is resting from mundane speech, as G-d rested from the ten sayings by which the physical heaven and earth were made.

The book that began its account of the world with the word that makes it, ends with the word that rests.

The world is made by His speech. On Shabbat He rests from speaking, and we rest from ordinary talk, so that what speech was covering can come near. Clinging to the One is Shabbat's inside.

**Sit with it** *(6 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of how much talking fills your days, and your Shabbat, or your day of rest. - **The line, softly:** "He rested from the sayings. I rest from mine." - **Think it through.** Ten sayings made heaven and earth. On Shabbat He rested from them. "Remember": prayer and learning to cling to Him, One. "Keep": ordinary words stop. The quiet is not empty; it's the inside of the day. - **Where the picture breaks.** When a person stops talking, the room is just quiet. When He "rests," the world doesn't stop, since His word still stands in everything. The quiet is of a different kind. - **From inside.** A quiet that is not empty. Speech stops, and what speech was covering comes near. - **The edge.** The last line doesn't say how G-d "rested" from sayings that, elsewhere in the book, still stand in every created thing. It leaves the question open. So does this. - **Into the day.** This Shabbat, keep one hour free of ordinary talk. (Anyone: on your day of rest, one hour with no chatter, only prayer, learning, or your own words to G-d.)
