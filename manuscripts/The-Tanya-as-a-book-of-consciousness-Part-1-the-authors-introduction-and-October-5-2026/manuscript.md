This part reads the doorway of the Tanya and its first eighteen chapters as a map of the inner life. The title page promises that the whole of serving G-d is "very near to you, in your mouth and in your heart, to do it," and these chapters show the ground under that promise. They begin where a person really is: pulled two ways, judging himself by a score, swinging between sadness and carelessness. Then they name what is under the pulls, a breath blown from the inside of G-d; show how a mind that holds a truth births awe and love; show how a deed, a word and an act of understanding can hold the One Himself; and describe the state in which a thing feels like it stands on its own. From there they lay out the whole range of inner rule: the one whose bad is turned, the one whose bad is only bent, the one whose good has gone quiet above him, and in the middle the beinoni, the person who is awake inside the struggle and never lets the pull reach his thought, speech or hand. The part ends below the mind altogether, in a faith older than reasons that cannot say no to the One. Every chapter was read in the Hebrew first; every source line below was checked against the Hebrew text file of the Tanya and found at the line given. Where a chapter runs into long lists or technical Kabbalah, the reading compresses it into a plain sentence and says so.

## Author's introduction · Reading alone in the fog, and being answered

**The state.** Two states: the seeker alone with a book, whose own mind is muddled, who cannot find his own place in the teachings; and the relief of being answered, what the author calls *margoa lenafsho*, rest for the soul, with *eitzah nechonah*, sound counsel for the very thing that is hard for him.

**The chapter as consciousness.** Before any teaching, the author writes a letter to the people who used to come to him. He starts with something everyone in his circle already said: hearing words of guidance from a living teacher is not like reading them in a book. The reader reads "according to his own way and his own mind." If his mind is confused and he walks in darkness in his service of G-d, he can barely see the good light hidden in the books, even though that light is sweet to the eyes and healing to the soul. This is a real state. You open a holy book and the words stay flat. The light is there. Your eyes are closed from the inside.

He goes further. Books built on human reasoning cannot fit every soul, because no two minds are moved by the same thing. What stirs one person leaves his friend cold. Even the teachings of the Sages, where every soul down to the smallest spark is tied into the Torah and the Torah ties it to G-d, speak to everyone in general. Not every person gets to know his own particular place in the Torah. Even in plain law the Sages argue from one end to the other, and "both are the words of the living G-d," because souls come down along different lines: kindness, strength and the middle. A soul from the line of kindness leans toward kindness. How much more so in the hidden things of the heart: the awe and love in each person's mind and heart, which each one has "according to what he measures in his heart." The inner life is personal. A general answer often misses you.

So he speaks to the ones he knows, the people who opened to him "all the secrets of their hearts and minds" in the service that lives in the heart. These pages are his answers to their questions. He can no longer answer each person one by one, and people forget. So he wrote the answers down, so each one could keep them between his eyes, no longer push to see him in private, and find in them rest for his soul and good counsel for whatever is hard. If someone cannot understand a counsel here, let him lay his heart out before the great ones in his town. And he asks those great ones not to cover their mouths out of a false humility. The letter ends with a hope for the day when no one will need to teach his neighbor, because "all will know Me," and with a short note about the printing: many handwritten copies had spread full of errors, and friends brought a clean text to press.

The picture here is light in a book. It breaks in one place. More light does not help a closed eye. What opens it is being met where you stand: a counsel for your question, from someone who has heard hearts like yours. The book is written as that meeting.

**The movement.** From being alone with a question in the fog, to naming the question, to carrying its answer yourself. What carries a person across: a counsel that fits his own road, and when he cannot find it, speaking his heart to someone wiser.

**Entering it.** Hold one line: "The thing is very near to you, in your mouth and in your heart, to do it." Think it through: what is the one thing in serving G-d that is hard for me right now? Say it in one plain sentence. Bring it down: that sentence is the question these pages were written to answer. Where the picture breaks: "near" still sounds like two places close together. The book will say it is closer than that. Stop there. Words to Him: "Show me my own place in Your Torah." One act: this week, bring that one sentence to someone wiser than you and ask it plainly.

**Signs and the fake.** You have touched it when a page answers the thing you actually carry and your heart goes quiet and settled. Its fake is collecting teachings that moved someone else and wearing their feelings, or hiding your real question behind a false humility.

*Source: Tanya, Author's introduction (line 60): כי בהן ימצא מרגוע לנפשו ועצה נכונה לכל דבר הקשה עליו בעבודת ה׳*

*The Tanya speaks here of the Jewish soul and its particular root; this reading applies it to every person.*

## Chapter 1 · Two pulls, one person

**The state.** The ordinary divided state of a human being: two souls, *shtei neshamos*, that is, two pulls living in one person. One of them is the *nefesh habehamis*, the animal soul, the life of the body and the pull toward self, which draws from *kelipas nogah*, a "glowing husk" that holds some good.

**The chapter as consciousness.** The book opens with an oath the Sages say every soul takes before birth: "Be righteous and do not be wicked. Even if the whole world tells you that you are righteous, be in your own eyes like one who is wicked." At once the Tanya names the trap in it. If a person sees himself as wicked, his heart sinks, he becomes sad, and he cannot serve G-d with joy. If it does not bother him at all, he drifts into lightness. These are two states most people swing between: the heavy one that condemns itself and the light one that shrugs. Neither can serve. The chapter is looking for a third way to see yourself.

It lays out five kinds of people from the Talmud: the righteous who has it good, the righteous who has it hard, the wicked who has it good, the wicked who has it hard, and the *beinoni*, the one in between. Then it overturns the usual idea. The beinoni is not someone with half good deeds and half bad. The sage Rabbah called himself a beinoni, and his mouth never stopped learning. A person is called wicked at the moment he sins, even over a small rabbinic rule, even for failing to protest when he could, even for sitting idle when he could learn. So the beinoni has none of that. "Mostly merits" is only a borrowed name used for judgment. The true name *tzaddik* belongs to the one whose heart has been emptied of the pull toward evil: "my heart is hollow within me." Here is the key to the whole book: tzaddik, rasha and beinoni are not scores. They name who rules inside a person. (The chapter supports this with several proofs from the Talmud and Zohar; we give only the conclusion.)

Then the root. Every person, the good and the bad alike, has two souls. One comes from the husk and the "other side." It dresses itself in the blood and keeps the body alive. From it come the bad traits, each from one of four elements. Fire rises: anger and pride, the swell of the self when it is slighted. Water makes things grow: the craving for pleasures. Air: silliness, mockery, boasting and empty talk. Earth: laziness and sadness, the heavy state that will not move. And the same soul is also the source of the good traits a person is born with, like mercy and kindness. It comes from the glowing husk, which has good mixed in it, like the tree of knowing good and bad.

That last point cuts deep. The pull toward self is not pure darkness. Some of your natural warmth grows from the same ground. Feeling kind is not yet proof that the higher soul is speaking.

The chapter then says the souls of the nations come from other husks with no good in them, and that their kindness is done for their own glory. It says this plainly, and we pass it on as it stands.

The picture of four elements breaks in two places. These are not physical fire and water; they are directions a self moves in. And "two souls" can sound like two people. It is one person, pulled.

**The movement.** From judging yourself by a score, and swinging between sadness and lightness, to seeing the two pulls clearly for what they are. What carries a person across is understanding in detail: telling apart which pull is moving at this moment.

**Entering it.** Hold one line: "There are two souls in me, and neither one is the whole of me." Think it through: this week's anger that rose like fire; the want that spread like water; the chatter; the heaviness that would not get up. Bring it down to one: take the last time you were angry. It rose, it wanted to be seen. That is a direction, not a verdict. Where the picture breaks: the kindness you felt yesterday may be your nature too. Do not crown it yet. Stop at the edge: then who am I? Leave the question open. The next chapter answers it. Words to Him: "You made both. Let me know which one is speaking." One act: once today, when a pull rises, name its element in your heart and do the act the other soul wants.

**Signs and the fake.** You have touched it when you can see a pull as a pull without despair and without a shrug, and the sadness lifts because your whole self is no longer on trial. Its fake is "that's just my animal soul" used as an excuse, or a crushing guilt dressed up as humility.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 1 (line 82): דלכל איש ישראל, אחד צדיק ואחד רשע, יש שתי נשמות*

## Chapter 2 · Breathed out from the inside

**The state.** The second soul, *chelek Elokah mimaal mamash*, "truly a part of G-d above." In plain words: the deepest point in you is not a thing that was made. It is breath from His innermost, and it stays tied to its root even at the lowest level.

**The chapter as consciousness.** The verse says G-d "blew into his nostrils a soul of life." The Zohar adds: one who blows, blows from within himself. A breath pushed out hard comes from the inside of a living being, from its inmost life. So the core of you is not something set apart from G-d and fashioned. It is what came out of His inside.

A second picture: souls "rose in His thought," and Israel is called His firstborn son. As a child is drawn from the father's mind, so each soul is drawn from His thought and wisdom. And His wisdom is not a separate thing He has. He and His wisdom are one: He is the knower, the knowing and the known. This, the chapter says, no person can grasp. (A note brings the Kabbalists and the Arizal into agreement and adds that the Infinite is far above even wisdom; we compress it into that sentence.)

Now the part that speaks to how a person feels about himself. Souls differ without end. The souls of the Patriarchs and Moses stand to the souls of our late generation the way a head stands to the heels. In every generation there are leaders whose souls are head and brain to the souls of simple people. Yet the root of every soul, from the highest to the one dressed in the body of the lightest person, is drawn from the supernal wisdom.

The picture: the drop that comes from the father's brain becomes, after nine months in the mother, even the child's toenails. It went down level by level until it became nail. And still it is bound to what it was at first, and even now the nails get their life from the brain in the head. So every soul, going down through the worlds of Atzilus, Beriah, Yetzirah and Asiyah, became even the soul of the least learned, and it stays bound to its first essence. The life of the simple soul flows to it through the souls of the wise and righteous of its generation. That is why the Sages said that one who clings to the wise is as if he clings to the Presence of G-d itself: through them his soul is tied to its root. Those who rebel against the wise draw their life from the "back" of the wise men's souls. The chapter ends with a word on why parents should be holy at conception, since every soul wears a garment from its parents' essence, and adds that a very lofty soul sometimes comes to a lowly father.

From inside, most people live in the toenail. Far, hard, dry, sure they are small. The chapter does not deny the distance. It says the life in the nail still comes from the head right now. The picture breaks here: a nail does not feel the brain, but you can come to know your root. And "part" can sound like a piece cut off. The One has no parts. The word *mamash*, "truly," insists that the tie is real and essential, not a figure of speech.

**The movement.** From "I am just a heel, far from anything holy" to knowing that the life in you right now comes from the Head. What carries a person across: understanding the tie, and clinging to people who are the brain of his generation.

**Entering it.** Hold one line: "Blown out from within, from His inmost." Think it through: one who blows hard gives what is inside him. So what is inside me came from inside Him. Bring it down to one real thing: the part of you that still wants the good, even on your worst night. That is the breath. Where the picture breaks: "part of G-d" does not mean a piece. Stop there; it is more one than a part. Words to Him: "I came from You. Keep me tied." One act: this week, go sit with or learn from someone wiser, and let his head nourish your heel.

**Signs and the fake.** You have touched it when a quiet dignity stays with you that does not depend on your level. Its fake is spiritual pride, "I am a piece of G-d" used to swell the self, or contempt for simple people whose root is the same as yours.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 2 (line 86): היא חלק אלוה ממעל ממש*

## Chapter 3 · Holding it until the heart answers

**The state.** The soul's ten powers, three of mind and seven of heart. *Chochmah*, the first flash of a truth; *binah*, working it out in length and depth; *daas*, holding it fast, "binding the mind" to it. And what that holding births: *yiras haromemus*, awe before His greatness, and *ahavah*, love that longs to cleave.

**The chapter as consciousness.** Each level of the soul has ten powers, like the ten divine attributes it came from. They split into two: mind and feelings. The mind holds chochmah, binah and daas. The feelings are love of G-d, fear and awe of Him, glorifying Him, and the rest. The mind is the mother of the feelings. They are born from it.

Chochmah is the mind that grasps anything at its start. The chapter reads the word as *koach mah*, "the power of what is": a point not yet worked out. Binah is when a person brings that point into action, turning it over to understand it fully and deeply. These two are "father and mother." They give birth to love and awe.

How: when the mind reflects deeply on G-d's greatness, how He fills all worlds, surrounds all worlds, and how everything before Him counts as nothing, a state is born in the mind: awe of His greatness, to fear and to feel shame before a greatness with no end. And dread of Him in the heart. Then the heart catches fire with a strong love, like flames, with longing and desire and a soul that thirsts for the greatness of the Infinite. This is *klos hanefesh*, the soul pining away, as in "my soul yearns and even expires." The thirst is from the fire in the heart. The water, what feeds and cools, comes from the brain, from chochmah. All the other feelings are branches of awe and love.

Then daas. The word comes from "Adam knew Eve." It means joining, bonding. A person ties his mind with a strong, firm knot and fixes his thought on the greatness of the Infinite, and does not let it slip away. And here the chapter names the failure that sits under most spiritual life: even someone wise and understanding in G-d's greatness, if he does not bind his mind and fix his thought on it firmly and with persistence, will not give birth to true awe and love, "only empty imaginings." So daas is what keeps the feelings alive. Without it they are either never born or born false.

From inside, the three are easy to tell apart. The flash: "yes, G-d fills everything." The working out: "this room, this hand, the ground under the house." The holding: staying with it, coming back each time the mind wanders, until something in the chest answers. Most people stop at the flash. Some understand and move on. Only the holding births the child.

The picture of father and mother breaks in one place: a child, once born, lives on its own. Love and awe do not. They live only as long as daas keeps holding.

**The movement.** From knowing about G-d to knowing in the sense of joining, and from there into awe and love. What carries a person across: understanding in detail, and above all persistence, holding the thought and not letting it drift.

**Entering it.** Hold one line: "Everything before Him counts as nothing." Think it through, detail by detail: the table in front of you, the city outside, the whole earth; each held in being by Him right now. Bring it down to one real thing: the cup in your hand. Stay with it until it feels held, not solid on its own. Where the picture breaks: "nothing" does not mean destroyed. It means it has no weight next to Him. Stop at that edge and do not push past it. Words to Him: "You are so great, and You are here." One act: before your next meal, hold that one line for the length of the blessing and say the blessing slowly.

**Signs and the fake.** You have touched it when a feeling comes after the holding, stays after it, and changes one choice. Its fake is what the chapter calls "empty imaginings": a glow you stirred up without holding anything, which leaves no trace.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 3 (line 113): לא יוליד בנפשו יראה ואהבה אמיתית, כי אם דמיונות שוא*

## Chapter 4 · Embracing the King through His garments

**The state.** The soul's three garments, *machshavah, dibur, maaseh*: thought, speech and action. When these are clothed in the commandments, a person is wrapped in G-d's own will. Behind this stands His humility, the contraction of the Infinite into something a mind and a hand can hold.

**The chapter as consciousness.** Besides its ten powers, the soul has three garments: thought, speech and action. When a person does the commandments with his body, speaks words of Torah, and understands the Torah with his mind as far as he can, the whole of his soul is dressed in the commandments. In detail: his mind is dressed in understanding Torah, and his feelings, awe and love, are dressed in doing the commandments and speaking Torah. Love is the root of the positive commandments. One who truly loves wants to cleave, and there is no way to cleave except by doing what He wants, which the chapter calls the "limbs of the King." Awe is the root of the prohibitions: fear of rebelling against the King of kings, or a deeper awe, shame before His greatness at doing what He hates.

Then a turn that changes how the ordinary looks. These garments are higher than the soul itself. The Torah and the Holy One are "all one," because Torah is His wisdom and will, and He, His knowing and what He knows are one. And yet He is called the Infinite, beyond all grasp, "no thought can grasp Him." So the chapter says: where you find His greatness, there you find His humility. He contracted His will and wisdom into the commandments, their laws, and the letters of Scripture, so that a soul could grasp them in thought and do them in speech and deed. That is why Torah is compared to water, which runs from a high place to a low one. Torah came down from its place, level by level through the worlds, until it dressed itself in physical things, in ink on parchment, so that even speech and action, lower than thought, could hold it.

So a person wrapped in Torah and commandments is "bound up in the bundle of life with G-d Himself." His light surrounds that person from head to foot. That is why the Sages said one hour of return and good deeds in this world is better than all the life of the world to come. There, souls enjoy a glow of His presence; that is all a created being can grasp. Here, in the commandment, a person grasps what is clothed in it, and that is G-d Himself.

The picture: one who embraces a king. It makes no difference to how close he is whether the king wears one garment or many, since the king's body is inside them. And when the king embraces him with his arm, "His right hand embraces me," even though the arm is inside the sleeve.

From inside, this is the state of a person doing something plain, giving a coin, lighting a candle, saying a verse, and in it holding the King, though it feels ordinary. The blocks: the sense that a physical act is far from G-d, and the belief that a lofty feeling is closer than a deed. The picture breaks in an important place. A king's clothes are not the king. Here the garment is His own will, which is one with Him. The picture understates the closeness.

**The movement.** From "G-d is beyond reach" to "I am holding Him," carried by the act, the word and the understanding, each given to G-d.

**Entering it.** Hold one line: "Where you find His greatness, there you find His humility." Think it through: the Infinite, whom no thought grasps, put His will into a small act a hand can do. Bring it down: pick the next commandment you will do today, one real thing, and see it as the King's arm. Where the picture breaks: His garment is not outside Him. Stop there. Words to Him: "You came down this far so I could hold You." One act: do that one commandment slowly, knowing whom you are holding.

**Signs and the fake.** You have touched it when a plain deed feels close, not small, and the craving for special experiences quiets. Its fake is doing the act as a habit with no one inside it, or chasing a feeling as though it were closer than the deed.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 4 (line 127): הרי זה כמחבק את המלך דרך משל*

## Chapter 5 · Holding and being held

**The state.** The moment of truly understanding a teaching of Torah, read as a *yichud nifla*, a wondrous union: the mind holds G-d's will and is held inside it, one from every side. The chapter calls Torah known this way *mazon hanefesh*, food of the soul, an inner light, where the commandments are garments, a light that surrounds.

**The chapter as consciousness.** Elijah said to G-d, "No thought can grasp You." The chapter stops on the word "grasp." When any mind understands something, it takes hold of it and surrounds it, and the thing understood is held and surrounded inside the mind. And at the same time the mind is dressed in the thing it understands, for as long as it holds it. Anyone who has worked through a hard idea until it clicked knows both halves: you hold it, and for that moment you are inside it.

Now take a ruling in the Mishnah or Talmud: if Reuven claims this and Shimon claims that, the ruling is such. That ruling is G-d's wisdom and will. It rose in His will that this is how it should be, even if such a case never happens in the world. So when a person understands that ruling properly, his mind grasps and surrounds G-d's will and wisdom, which no thought can grasp except as they are dressed in these laws. And his mind is dressed in them too. The chapter calls this "a wondrous union with nothing like it in the physical world, to become one from every side."

That is why knowing Torah is greater than all the commandments of action, and even greater than Torah that is only spoken. Through the deeds and the words, G-d dresses the soul and His light surrounds it from head to foot. In knowing Torah, the mind is dressed in His wisdom and His wisdom is also inside the mind, as much as each person can hold by his own mind and power of understanding, from the plain meaning to the secret.

So Torah is called bread, food for the soul. Physical bread feeds the body when you take it all the way inside, where it turns into your own blood and flesh. So Torah learned well, with the mind's effort, until it is grasped and becomes one with the mind, turns into food for the soul and life inside it, from the Life of all life dressed in His wisdom. "Your Torah is within my inward parts." The garments of souls in the Garden of Eden are the commandments, and the Torah is their food. And "for its own sake," *lishmah*, means learning in order to bind your soul to G-d through understanding, each by his own mind. Food is inner light and the garments are surrounding light. Torah is both: food, and also a garment for the mind that is dressed in it, and all the more when it is spoken aloud, since the breath of speech becomes a surrounding light. (The chapter cites Etz Chaim, Pri Etz Chaim and the Zohar for these points; we give the points.)

From inside, this is the difference between skimming and understanding. You can pass your eyes over a teaching and be untouched, or labor with it until it becomes yours, and in that moment, if you know what it is, you are one with His will. The picture of bread breaks in a telling way. Bread becomes you. Here you are also taken into it. And physical union always leaves two sides; this one is "from every side."

**The movement.** From being wrapped from outside, by deeds, to being fed from inside, by understanding. What carries a person across: learning "well, with the mind's effort," until the idea is grasped and becomes one with him, and learning it in order to bind himself to G-d.

**Entering it.** Hold one line: "This ruling is His will, and I am holding it." Think it through: take one small law you know well. Who decided it? It rose in His will, even for a case that may never happen. Bring it down: go over that one law until you can say it in your own words and see why it is so. Where the picture breaks: bread becomes you; here you also become it. Stop at the edge of that. Words to Him: "Let what I understand be You inside me." One act: learn one short piece of Torah today until you understand it, and say it out loud once.

**Signs and the fake.** You have touched it when understanding feels like meeting, not owning, and it stays with you as nourishment. Its fake is piling up information to feel clever, learning that feeds the self and not the bond.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 5 (line 134): והוא יחוד נפלא שאין יחוד כמוהו*

## Chapter 6 · The feeling of standing on your own

**The state.** The *sitra achra*, "the other side," read as a state: the sense that a thing, or a self, is *davar nifrad bifnei atzmo*, "a separate thing standing on its own." Its opposite is *kedushah*, holiness, which rests only on what is *batel*, the self gone quiet before Him.

**The chapter as consciousness.** "G-d made this opposite that." The animal soul also has ten powers. It has seven bad traits from the four elements, and a mind that births them. The feelings always match the mind: a small child loves little things and rages over little things because his mind is small. When a person thinks, speaks or acts with these powers, his thought, speech and hands become their "garments of impurity." All the deeds under the sun that are not for G-d, and all the words and thoughts that are not for Him, are "vanity and a breaking of the spirit." That is what *sitra achra* means: another side, not the side of holiness.

Then the heart of it. The side of holiness is only what G-d's holiness rests on and flows into. And He rests only on what is *batel* to Him, what does not stand before Him as a self. That can be actual, like the high angels, or potential, like every Jew, who has it in him to give his life for G-d's holiness. That is why the Sages said that even one person sitting and learning Torah has the Presence resting with him, and wherever ten gather, the Presence rests always.

But whatever is not batel to Him, whatever is "a separate thing standing on its own," does not get its life from the inside of holiness. It gets its life from the "back," from a light that went down through countless levels, cause after effect, contraction after contraction, until it grew so small that it could go into exile inside that separate thing, to keep it in being and stop it from falling back into nothing. So this world and everything in it is called the world of the husks and the other side. That is why the affairs of this world are hard and bad and the wicked prevail in it. A note adds at once that inside this world are the ten holy attributes of Asiyah, inside them those of Yetzirah, inside them those of Beriah, inside them those of Atzilus, and inside those the light of the Infinite. The Infinite fills even this lowest earth.

The husks come in two ranks. The lower one is three husks wholly impure, with no good in them. From them, the chapter says, come the souls of the nations and the bodies that carry them, the souls of impure animals, forbidden plants, and the life of every forbidden act, word and thought. (The next chapter takes up the upper rank.)

Read as consciousness, this is the most ordinary state there is. "I am here. My life is mine. The world stands there on its own." That is not wickedness. It is how things appear when the light is thin: a self standing apart, a world that seems to run by itself. That is how the state tzimtzum produces looks from inside. Holiness is the reverse: the self going quiet, not destroyed, simply no longer standing as a second thing before the One.

The picture of "another side" breaks if you take it as a second power. The chapter is clear: the separate thing lives only from the back of His light. Its standing on its own is the light grown small, not a rival to it.

**The movement.** From standing as a separate self to *bittul*, being quiet before Him. What carries a person across: the power every soul has to give itself for Him, and learning Torah and joining with others, where the Presence rests.

**Entering it.** Hold one line: "He rests only on what is quiet before Him." Think it through: the chair, the street, my own will all seem to stand by themselves. Each is kept in being by a light hidden inside it. Bring it down to one real thing: one choice today where you feel "this is mine, my way." See the separate self standing there. Where the picture breaks: the "other side" is not a second god. It lives from Him. Stop there. Words to Him: "Let me be less in the way." One act: do one thing today purely because He wants it, not because you do.

**Signs and the fake.** You have touched it when the self grows quiet and there is more room, not less; you feel lighter, not erased. Its fake is erasing yourself out of low worth, or a show of humility that is the self standing even taller.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 6 (line 148): אלא הוא דבר נפרד בפני עצמו*

## Chapter 7 · The mixed middle, and what lifts it

**The state.** *Kelipas nogah*, the glowing husk: the whole neutral, permitted life of the body, done for its own sake. It is a middle state that can sink or rise. What lifts it is *birur*, sorting out the good in it by doing it for G-d. Beyond that, the chapter names *teshuvah meahavah*, return out of great love, which turns even deliberate sins into merits.

**The chapter as consciousness.** The animal soul, the souls of kosher animals, the food a person may eat, and every permitted act, word and thought in the affairs of this world, when they are done not for heaven but for the body's want, even for its real needs, all draw from the upper rank of the husks, called *nogah*, "glow." It is mostly bad with a little good mixed in, and from that good come the good natural traits of chapter 1. It stands in the middle between the three wholly impure husks and holiness. So sometimes it is swept into the impure ones, and sometimes it rises into holiness, when the good in it is sorted from the bad and goes up.

The chapter's example: a person eats fat meat and drinks fragrant wine to open his mind to G-d and His Torah, as Rava said, or to keep the joy of Shabbos and festivals. Then the life in the meat and wine, which came from the glowing husk, is sorted and rises to G-d like an offering. So too one who tells a funny thing to open his mind and gladden his heart for G-d, since service needs joy, as Rava used to start his lessons with a joke. But one who gorges on meat and wine to fill the body's lust brings the life in them down, for the time being, into the wholly bad, and his body becomes their vehicle for a while, until he returns to serve G-d. Because the meat and wine were *heter*, permitted, which literally means untied, they can rise with him when he returns. Still, a mark remains in the body, and the body must be cleansed after death. (The chapter says the same of the life in seed spent in lust with one's own wife without sanctifying the act.)

Forbidden foods and forbidden relations come from the wholly impure husks. They are *assur*, which means bound. They are tied in the hands of the outside forces and do not rise until the end of days, unless the person returns so deeply that his deliberate sins become merits. That is a return from the depth of the heart, with great love and longing to cleave to Him, thirsting "like a dry and weary land." Because until now his soul lived in a dry land far from G-d's face, his thirst is greater than the thirst of the righteous. Return without this love is still real, and G-d forgives, but the sins do not become merits. (The chapter then works through several cases of seed and forbidden relations, which differ in how the life in them can rise through return and the bedtime Shema; we compress them into this sentence. It ends with the one case the Sages call beyond repair, a child born of a forbidden union, since that life has already entered a body.)

From inside, this chapter is about the same act having two states. The same meal can be the body feeding itself or an offering. The same joke can be escape or a door to joy. Nothing changes on the plate. What changes is for whom. The permitted life is untied: waiting to be lifted. And the chapter holds out the strongest hope for the one who went furthest: his distance becomes the size of his thirst.

The picture of the offering breaks here: it is not a rite done over the food but the direction of the one who eats it. And "untied" and "bound" are not about the food alone but about whether its life can go up with you.

**The movement.** From living for yourself, to sorting the good out of ordinary life by doing it for Him; and from the lowest place, through the thirst of distance, into return from love. What carries a person across: intention that is real, and for the one who has fallen far, love born from the dryness itself.

**Entering it.** Hold one line: "This is untied. It can rise with me." Think it through: my food, my rest, my talk today. Each is permitted and waiting. Bring it down to one real thing: your next meal. For what are you eating it? Where the picture breaks: nothing is burned on an altar; the lifting is in you. Stop there. Words to Him: "Take this up with me." One act: before one meal today, say in your own words, "I am eating this to have strength to serve You," and mean it.

**Signs and the fake.** You have touched it when ordinary things begin to feel like they belong somewhere and a quiet gladness comes with them. Its fake is "it's for the sake of heaven" said over plain indulgence, the label without the direction.

## Chapter 8 · What you take in stays in you

**The state.** The *reshimu*, the imprint, that everything taken in leaves in a person: food becomes flesh, pleasure leaves a residue, words and ideas color the mind. Against it stands a clear mind, careful about what it lets in, and a way of using the world "as an axe to cut with."

**The chapter as consciousness.** Forbidden food has a further weight. Even if someone eats it without knowing, for the sake of heaven, and serves G-d with the strength it gave him, its life does not rise into his words of Torah and prayer the way permitted food does. It is bound. This holds even for a rabbinic prohibition. So the craving for forbidden things is called a "foreign demon," the evil pull of the nations, whose souls come from the wholly impure husks. The craving for permitted things, just to fill one's want, is a "Jewish demon": it can come back to holiness. But until it does, it is still husk. And even after, its mark stays stuck in the body, "because every food and drink becomes blood and flesh of his flesh at once."

So the body needs cleansing after death, the "beating of the grave," to be purified of what it took on from this world's pleasures, unless a person, like Rabbi Yehudah the Prince, never took pleasure from this world his whole life. Empty talk that is permitted, like that of a simple man who cannot learn, leaves a stain the soul must be cleansed of by being whirled in "the hollow of the sling." Forbidden speech, mockery and gossip, needs deeper cleansing. One who could learn and spends his time in empty talk needs more than that as well. (The chapter lists these purifications in order; we give them as one: what the soul took on, it must be washed of.)

Then the mind. Studying the wisdoms of the nations counts as empty talk when it takes the place of Torah. And it leaves a deeper stain. Empty talk soils only the feelings, since anyone can babble. But those wisdoms dress the mind itself, the divine soul's chochmah, binah and daas, in the glowing husk, because they fell from the "back" of holy wisdom when the vessels broke. Unless a person makes them "an axe to cut with": uses them to earn a living so he can serve G-d freely, or knows how to use them for G-d's service and His Torah, as the Rambam and Ramban did.

As consciousness, this chapter describes the state of a person filled with what he let in. Nothing passes through without leaving something. A meal eaten for pleasure alone leaves a heaviness toward pleasure. A day of chatter leaves a mind that chatters. Ideas absorbed without purpose shape the very instrument you would use to know G-d. The hard words here, demons, the grave, the sling, are the Tanya's honest way of saying that what we take on becomes ours to clear. They are not threats to crush a person. They tell him that the self can be washed, and that it is better to choose now what goes in.

The picture of food becoming flesh breaks gently. Flesh stays flesh. The imprint in the soul can be cleansed, and a mind soiled by what it took in can be made clean.

**The movement.** From taking things in without thought to choosing what enters the body and the mind, and using the world as a tool for Him. What carries a person across: intention before taking something in, and the test of the axe: does this cut a path to Him?

**Entering it.** Hold one line: "What I take in becomes me." Think it through: today's food, today's talk, what I read and watched. Bring it down to one real thing: one thing you took in yesterday only for the taking. What did it leave? Where the picture breaks: flesh stays flesh, but the soul can be washed. Stop there. Words to Him: "Clean what I took on, and guard what I let in." One act: choose one thing you will not take in today, and one thing you will take in only as an axe, for Him.

**Signs and the fake.** You have touched it when the mind feels clearer and lighter after a day of choosing what came in. Its fake is a sour disgust with the body, or pride in being careful while the mind still feeds on the same things in private.

## Chapter 9 · Two kings, one small city

**The state.** The state of being contested ground: the body is an *ir ketanah*, a small city, and two souls war to rule it. The divine soul lives in the brain and the right side of the heart; the animal soul lives in the left side of the heart. The goal is a love so full that it overflows into the left side and turns the same longing toward G-d, up to *ahavah betaanugim*, love of delight.

**The chapter as consciousness.** The animal soul lives in the heart, in the left chamber, which is full of blood. So lusts, boasting, anger and the like are felt in the heart. From there they spread through the body and also rise to the brain, "to think about them, dwell on them and get clever with them," the way blood starts in the heart and reaches the head. Anyone who has watched a want put on a reasonable argument knows this: the heart wants first, and then the mind is sent to find the reasons.

The divine soul lives in the brain and spreads from there to the body. In the heart it lives in the right chamber, which has no blood. There it is love of G-d, flaring like flames in the hearts of those who understand and reflect on what awakens love, and the joy that comes when the eyes of the mind look at the King's glory without end. All holy feelings in the heart come from the mind.

"One nation shall prevail over the other." Like two kings fighting over one city, each wanting to rule it and have its people obey him, the two souls fight over the body and every limb. The divine soul wants to rule alone. It wants the whole body to be its garment and vehicle, with no stranger passing through. The brain would be full of G-d's wisdom, reflecting on His greatness, and through daas it would birth awe in the mind and dread in the heart, and love burning like fire, the soul yearning to cleave to Him with all the heart, soul and might, from the depth of the right chamber. Its inside would be "paved with love," full and overflowing, until it spills into the left chamber too, to subdue the animal soul's water, which is craving, and turn it from this world's pleasures to love of G-d. That is why the verse says "with all your heart": with both your pulls.

Higher still is *ahavah rabbah*, great love, called "love of delights": taking pleasure in G-d, a taste of the world to come. That delight lives in the mind, in chochmah, which delights in knowing Him. It is the holy water in the divine soul, and it turns around the animal soul's water, the very source of the craving for this world. Then the bad becomes wholly good, like a good inclination, once its "soiled garments," the pleasures of this world, are taken off. All the heart's feelings would be for G-d alone. Speech and thought would be filled with Torah; the hands with commandments.

The animal soul wants the exact reverse. And yet, the chapter ends, it wants that for the person's good, so that he will overpower it and win, like the harlot in the Zohar's parable, sent by the king to test his son.

The two kings picture breaks at the end. In a real war, the enemy wants to win. Here the enemy is the King's servant and the test was set so the son would pass it. And the love the chapter wants does not wipe out the craving. It reaches the same water and turns it.

**The movement.** From a city ruled by the heart's wants, to a heart so full of love that it spills over and turns those wants toward G-d. What carries a person across: the mind's reflection on His greatness, held through daas, until love is born and overflows.

**Entering it.** Hold one line: "With all your heart: with both your pulls." Think it through: the want rises in the left side and climbs to the head wearing reasons. The love starts in the head and fills the right side. Bring it down to one real thing: the strongest craving you carry this week. What does it really want? Warmth, sweetness, rest. Where the picture breaks: the enemy is the King's servant; the craving is water waiting to be turned. Stop there. Words to Him: "Let my love for You reach even this." One act: the next time that craving rises, give its strength to one act of love for Him, a prayer said with heart, a kindness done warmly.

**Signs and the fake.** You have touched it when the same energy that used to pull you now warms you toward Him, and the fight feels like it has a point. Its fake is grim suppression with no love inside it, or a rush of excitement that leaves the craving exactly where it was.

## Chapter 10 · The bad turned, or only bent

**The state.** The two states of the *tzaddik*, the righteous one. The *tzaddik gamur*, complete, whose bad has been turned into good; and the *tzaddik she'eino gamur*, not complete, whose bad is still there but small, bent and nullified. The measure between them: how complete the love of G-d is, which shows in how complete the loathing of evil is.

**The chapter as consciousness.** When a person strengthens his divine soul and fights the animal soul so hard that he drives the bad out of the left chamber, but the bad has not been turned into good, he is a righteous one who is not complete. A tiny bit of bad is still in him, bent under the good because it is so small. So it seems to him that it has gone away entirely. But the chapter says plainly: if all of it had truly gone, it would have been turned into good.

The complete tzaddik has turned his bad into good by taking off its soiled garments entirely. That means loathing the pleasures of this world when they are only for the body and not for G-d, because they flow from the other side. He hates the other side with a perfect hatred, because of how great his love for G-d is, his love of delights. The two are measured together: "I hated them with perfect hatred." As great as the love is, so great is the loathing, since loathing is love's exact reverse.

The one who is not complete does not hate the other side perfectly, so he does not loathe the bad perfectly either. Wherever hatred and loathing are not complete, some trace of love and pleasure for that thing remains. The soiled garments were not all taken off, so the bad did not become good. It is just nullified by its smallness. And so his love for G-d is also not complete. This level has countless degrees, depending on which of the four elements the remaining bad comes from and how small it is, like one part in sixty, or a thousand, or ten thousand. These are the many righteous people in every generation.

The complete tzaddikim are few. They are called "sons of ascent" because they turn the bad and raise it up to holiness, those who "turn darkness into light and bitter into sweet." They are also called that because their good deeds are not only to quench their own thirst for G-d, "Ho, all who thirst, come to the water," but for a need on High: to unite the Holy One and His Presence in the lower worlds, like a son who loves his father and mother more than his own body and soul and would give his life to redeem them. (A bracketed passage explains in Kabbalistic terms how sorting the good out of the glowing husk and drawing kindness down through each commandment are one act; we give it as that sentence.)

As consciousness, the chapter gives a way to know where you stand that does not rest on how quiet you feel. The quiet of a small, bent pull can feel like freedom. The test is whether there is still a trace of pleasure in the thing when you picture it. And it opens a higher state: service not for your own thirst but for His.

The picture of taking off soiled garments breaks in a helpful place. The one who wore them is not thrown away. The same bad, once undressed, turns out to be good.

**The movement.** From bad that is bent to bad that is turned, and from serving to quench your own thirst to serving for His sake. What carries a person across: a love so great that the loathing of what opposes it becomes complete.

**Entering it.** Hold one line: "As great as the love, so great the loathing." Think it through: one pleasure I have mostly left. Is there still a trace of sweetness when I picture it? Bring it down to one real thing: name that trace honestly. It is not failure; it is the measure of my love so far. Where the picture breaks: the garment comes off and the one inside is good. Stop there. Words to Him: "Grow my love until nothing else is sweet." One act: do one commandment today for His sake alone, not for how it will make you feel.

**Signs and the fake.** You have touched it when a pull you left no longer has any sweetness, and your love feels larger than your wanting. Its fake is thinking the pull is gone because it is quiet, or calling your own spiritual thirst "serving Him."

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 10 (line 192): נשאר איזה שמץ אהבה ותענוג לשם*

## Chapter 11 · The good bowed down, and still above him

**The state.** The two states of the *rasha*, the one ruled by the pull toward self. *Rasha vetov lo*: the good in him is bowed under the bad, comes and goes, and speaks as remorse. *Rasha vera lo*: the good has withdrawn from inside and stands above him as a *makif*, a surrounding light.

**The chapter as consciousness.** "This opposite that": opposite the righteous one whose bad is bent is the one in whom the good is bent. The good of the divine soul in his mind and in the right side of his heart is bowed under the bad in the left side.

This too has countless degrees. For some, the bowing is small and not constant. Only now and then does the bad win, and it takes only part of the small city, dressing itself in one of the three garments. In deed alone, light sins. Or in speech alone, a trace of gossip or mockery. Or in thought alone, thoughts of sin, which the Sages call harder than sin, even thoughts of intimacy by day, or turning the heart to idleness at a time he could be learning. At that moment he is called a rasha, because the bad in his soul overcomes him, dresses in his body, makes him sin and makes him impure. Afterwards the good in his divine soul rises, he regrets, asks G-d's forgiveness, and G-d forgives him if he returns properly, as the Sages taught.

For others, the bad wins more. It dresses in all three garments, in heavier sins and more often. But in between, he regrets. Thoughts of return come to him from the good in his soul, which rises a little. It is not strong enough to overcome the bad and leave the sin completely, "to confess and forsake." Of these the Sages said, "the wicked are full of regrets." That is most of them: there is still good in their souls.

And one who never regrets, to whom no thought of return comes at all, is called *rasha vera lo*. The bad alone remains inside him, because it overpowered the good so much that the good withdrew from within him and stands above him, surrounding him from above. And still the Sages said, "among any ten, the Presence rests."

As consciousness, the chapter gives a gentle and honest reading of the hardest states. Remorse, even remorse that does not yet change anything, is not a sign of being lost. It is the good soul speaking from inside. Being "full of regrets" means the good is still there. And even where there is no remorse at all, the soul has not gone. It stands above, around the person, like a light waiting outside a closed house.

The picture of a city partly conquered breaks here. A conquered city is lost; here the rightful king is still in it, or just above it, and the city can be taken back in one moment of return.

**The movement.** From remorse that comes and goes, to a return that confesses and forsakes; and from the good hovering above, to the good living inside again. What carries a person across: the thought of return itself, and the Presence that rests among any gathering of ten.

**Entering it.** Hold one line: "The regret itself is the good in me." Think it through: the last time I felt sorry for something I did. Who was feeling sorry? Bring it down to one real thing: one sin you keep regretting and keep doing. The regret is your soul's voice; do not despise it. Where the picture breaks: the good that seems gone is standing above you. Stop there. Words to Him: "I am sorry. Help me not only to regret but to leave it." One act: take one real step away from that one thing today, however small.

**Signs and the fake.** You have touched it when regret turns into a first step instead of a mood. Its fake is regret that replaces change, or despair that says "nothing good is left in me," when the despair is itself the good speaking.

## Chapter 12 · The beinoni: awake inside the struggle

**The state.** The *beinoni*, the one in between: the bad in him is at full strength in the heart but never reaches his thought, speech or deed, because *hamoach shalit al halev*, the mind rules the heart. In prayer his divine soul rules; after prayer he keeps the doors, pushing a bad thought away "with both hands" the moment it rises.

**The chapter as consciousness.** In the beinoni, the bad never wins enough to take the small city and dress itself in the body to make it sin. The animal soul's three garments, thought, speech and action, never overpower the divine soul in his mind, mouth or limbs. Only the divine soul's garments are dressed in his body. He has never sinned and never will. He is never called a rasha, not for a single moment.

But the essence of the divine soul, its ten powers, does not rule the city alone except at set times: during the Shema and prayer, which is a time of expanded mind above and a fitting time for every person below. Then he binds his mind to G-d, goes deep into the greatness of the Infinite, and wakes a love like flames in the right side of his heart, to cleave to Him by keeping the Torah from love. At that time the bad in the left side is bowed and nullified.

After prayer, when the expanded mind leaves, the bad wakes again in the left side and craves this world's pleasures. Only, since it does not have sole rule of the city, it cannot bring its craving into action, into the limbs, into words, or even into deep thought about how to fill it. Because the mind rules the heart. That is how a person is made from birth: anyone, by the will in his mind, can hold himself back and rule the spirit of craving in his heart, not fulfill it in deed, speech or thought, and turn his attention completely the other way, especially toward holiness. "As light is better than darkness": a little light pushes away much darkness by itself. So the wisdom of the divine soul pushes away the folly of the other side.

Still, he is not called a tzaddik. The light's advantage over the darkness is only in the garments, not essence against essence. After prayer there is no open fire of love in his heart, only a hidden love, the natural love of the divine soul. So the fool's folly can be open in the left side of his heart, wanting everything of this world, permitted and forbidden, as if he had never prayed. Doing something forbidden does not even occur to him. But thoughts of sin can rise to his mind and disturb him from Torah and prayer. The Sages said no one escapes them each day.

What helps him is the imprint of prayer left in the mind, and the hidden awe and love in the right side. Even in the mind alone, the bad has no rule to make him dwell on a bad thought willingly. The moment it rises, he pushes it away with both hands and turns his mind away as soon as he recalls it is a bad thought. He does not accept it willingly, let alone think of doing it or speak of it. One who dwells on it willingly is called a rasha at that moment, and the beinoni is never a rasha even for a moment. The same between people: the moment a grudge, hatred, jealousy, anger or resentment rises from heart to mind, he does not accept it. On the contrary, his mind rules the spirit in his heart to do the exact opposite: to treat that person with kindness and extra warmth, to bear with him to the very end, not to pay him back, but to do good to those who wronged him, as Joseph did with his brothers.

As consciousness, this is the waking state inside the struggle. Both pulls are alive. The bad thought still comes. What is different is where the person stands: at the doors, awake, able to say no in the first moment. The picture of light breaks here: physical light pushes darkness away for good, but this darkness comes back after every prayer. The light does not change it. It only keeps it from ruling.

**The movement.** From the heart ruling the mind to the mind ruling the heart, in thought, speech and deed. What carries a person across: the fact that the mind rules the heart by nature, the imprint of prayer, the hidden love, and the choice made in the first moment a thought rises.

**Entering it.** Hold one line: "The mind rules the heart." Think it through: the heart can want anything. It cannot move my hand, my mouth, or even my willing thought without my mind. Bring it down to one real thing: the one thought that keeps rising in you this week. Decide now what you will do in its first moment. Where the picture breaks: this light does not end the darkness; it keeps it from ruling. Stop there. Words to Him: "Help me push it away with both hands, and turn to You." One act: today, when that thought rises, turn your mind at once to one line of Torah or one kindness to the person who angered you.

**Signs and the fake.** You have touched it when a bad thought comes and goes without being welcomed, and you are not shaken by its coming. Its fake is gritted teeth with the thought still entertained inside, or rising above an insult only to feel better than the one who gave it.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 12 (line 215): מיד בעלייתו לשם, דוחהו בשתי ידים*

## Chapter 13 · The bad asleep, and a lip of truth

**The state.** The beinoni's honest way of seeing himself: *kerasha*, "like one who is wicked," never wicked itself. The bad in him is not gone; it is asleep, "like a man who sleeps and can wake." Yet his love in prayer is *sfas emes*, a lip of truth, true for his level.

**The chapter as consciousness.** The Sages said of the beinonim, "both judge him," the good pull and the bad. They did not say "both rule him." If the bad had any rule in the small city, even for a moment, he would be a rasha at that moment. The bad pull is like a judge who states his opinion in a case, and the ruling may still go the other way, since another judge disagrees and a third decides. So the bad pull states its opinion in the left side of the heart, and it rises to the mind as a thought. At once the second judge disagrees: the divine soul in the mind, spreading into the right side of the heart. And the ruling follows the one who decides: the Holy One, who helps the good pull. "If not for G-d's help, he could not overcome it." That help is the light of G-d shining on the divine soul, giving it the advantage of light over darkness.

Since the bad in the beinoni is at full strength, as it was born, not nullified and not moved from its place, only kept from spreading into the body because G-d stands at the right hand of the poor, he is called "like a rasha." That is the oath of chapter 1: "Even if the whole world tells you that you are righteous, be in your eyes like one who is wicked." Not truly wicked. He should hold himself a beinoni and not believe the world when it says the bad in him is gone. He should see its essence as fully strong in its place, not weakened at all, and even stronger with time, since it has been used much in eating, drinking and the rest of this world.

Even one whose delight is in G-d's Torah day and night, for its own sake, has no proof that the bad has moved. It may be at full strength, only its garments are kept out of his mind, mouth and hands. The essence of the divine soul rules the essence of the animal soul only when love is open in his heart, as in prayer, and even then it only rules: "when this one rises, that one falls." The divine soul grows strong through binah, reflecting on the Infinite's greatness and birthing a love like fire, and then the other side is subdued. It is nullified only in the tzaddik, "my heart is hollow within me." In the beinoni the bad is like a man asleep, who can wake. It sleeps during the Shema and prayer, while his heart burns with love, and afterward it can wake. That is why Rabbah held himself a beinoni, though his mouth never stopped learning with a longing like the longing in prayer: he was like a beinoni who prays all day.

Then truth. For the righteous, a love that passes after prayer is not called true service: "a lip of truth stands forever, a lying tongue only for a moment." Yet for beinonim, it is called whole service, true for them, each at his level. "I call their love in their prayer a lip of truth too," the author writes, since their divine soul has the power to wake this love again every day in prayer, with the preparation right for each soul. Truth is the trait of Jacob, the middle bar that runs from one end to the other, from the top of all levels to the bottom. At every level it runs through the middle point, which is that level's own truth. Truth has no limit upward, and every level is as nothing before the one above it.

As consciousness, this chapter answers the dilemma of chapter 1. "Like a rasha" is not sadness, because you are not wicked. And it is not lightness, because you know the sleeper is there. It is a clear, humble wakefulness. And your prayer's love, which fades by lunch, is still true, because you can wake it again tomorrow.

The two judges picture breaks in one place: the deciding judge is not a stranger outside you. His help is the light shining in your own soul.

**The movement.** From believing what others say about you, or what the quiet says, to an honest picture of yourself that is neither heavy nor light. What carries a person across: G-d's help, the decider, and daily preparation for prayer, which wakes the love again.

**Entering it.** Hold one line: "It is asleep, not gone; and my love today is true for me." Think it through: the pull that was quiet during prayer. Where did it go? It slept. Bring it down to one real thing: a praise someone gave you lately. Thank them, and do not believe it about your depths. Where the picture breaks: the one who decides is not outside you; His light is in you. Stop there. Words to Him: "Help me again today. Without You I could not." One act: prepare for tomorrow's prayer tonight: choose one thought of His greatness to bring into it.

**Signs and the fake.** You have touched it when you can hold "I am like a rasha" without sadness and "my prayer was true" without pride. Its fake is a humility that depresses, or a calm that says "I have arrived" when the thing is only asleep.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 13 (line 226): כאדם שישן, שיכול לחזור וליעור משנתו*

## Chapter 14 · Anyone can be a beinoni, at any moment

**The state.** The beinoni as a state open to every person at every moment, because it lives where choice lives: deed, speech and thought. It is entered with a sentence said to the heart: "I do not want to be separated from the One G-d in any way." Loathing evil and delighting in G-d belong to the heart, and they come as a gift.

**The chapter as consciousness.** The beinoni's measure is every person's measure; everyone should aim for it, because anyone can be a beinoni at any time. The beinoni does not loathe evil. That belongs to the heart, and not all times are the same. What he does is "turn from bad and do good" in actual deed, speech and thought. There the choice, the ability and the permission are given to every person, even against what his heart wants. Even when the heart craves something, permitted or forbidden, he can overcome it and turn his mind away completely, by saying to his heart:

"I do not want to be wicked even for one moment, because I do not want to be separated from the One G-d in any way. 'Your sins separate between you and your G-d.' I want to cling to Him with my soul, dressed in His three garments, deed, speech and thought in G-d, His Torah and His commandments, from the hidden love in my heart for Him, as in every Jewish heart. Even the lightest of the light can give his life for G-d's holiness, and surely I am not less than him. Only a spirit of folly entered him, and he imagines that with this sin he is still a Jew and his soul is not separated from the G-d of Israel, and he forgets his love for G-d hidden in his heart. But I do not want to be a fool like him and deny the truth."

The matters given to the heart are different: that the bad be truly disgusting in the heart and hated with a perfect hatred, or even a less than perfect one. That can only truly come from a great and powerful love, love of delights, taking pleasure in G-d, a taste of the world to come. Not everyone merits this. It is like receiving a reward, "a service of gift." That is why Job said, "You created righteous ones": there are many kinds of souls, the pious, the strong who overcome their pull, masters of Torah, prophets, righteous. And that is why the oath is doubled, "be righteous, and do not be wicked." Since not everyone can become a tzaddik, and a person does not have so much choice in it, he is made to swear a second time: at least, do not be wicked. That is in everyone's hands: to rule the craving in his heart and never be a rasha even for one moment, both in turning from bad and in doing good, and "there is no good but Torah."

Still, a person should set times to counsel himself to loathe the bad, using the Sages' blunt pictures of where the body's pleasures end: that all the delicacies become waste, that all this world's pleasures end in rot. (One of these pictures, about a woman, is the Sages' plain way of showing where physical desire ends; it speaks of pleasure's end, not of anyone's worth.) And the reverse: to take joy in G-d by reflecting on the Infinite's greatness as far as he can, even knowing he will not reach it in truth, only in imagination. He does his part, to keep the oath "be righteous," and G-d will do what is good in His eyes. And habit rules everything and becomes second nature. When he gets used to loathing the bad, it becomes a bit loathsome in truth. When he gets used to rejoicing in G-d, an awakening from below brings one from above, and perhaps a spirit from on high will come to him, a spark from the root of some tzaddik, to serve G-d with true joy.

As consciousness, this chapter is freedom. You do not have to wait for your heart to change before you can stand as a beinoni. The heart may want what it wants. You can still say the sentence, and turn. The picture of the "fool" breaks in mercy: the one who sins is not called evil but confused, "a spirit of folly entered him," and he forgot a love he still has.

**The movement.** From the heart's craving to a choice made in deed, speech and thought; and from effort and habit, over time, toward the gift of true loathing and true joy. What carries a person across: speaking to the heart, the hidden love, set times of reflection, and habit becoming second nature.

**Entering it.** Hold one line: "I do not want to be separated from the One, in any way." Think it through: even the simplest person would give his life rather than deny Him. I am not less. Bring it down to one real thing: the next time a craving rises today, you will say this sentence to your heart. Where the picture breaks: the one who sins is not a villain but a fool who forgot his love. Stop there. Words to Him: "I want to stay close to You. Keep me from folly." One act: set five minutes today to reflect on His greatness, even if all you reach is imagination, and do your part.

**Signs and the fake.** You have touched it when you turn from something your heart still wants and feel free, not deprived. Its fake is "I'm only a beinoni" used as permission never to reach higher, or forced disgust that turns into contempt for people.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 14 (line 232): שכל אדם יכול להיות בינוני בכל עת ובכל שעה*

*The Tanya speaks here of the Jewish soul and its particular root; this reading applies it to every person.*

## Chapter 15 · The hundred and first time

**The state.** *Oved Elokim*, "one who serves G-d," in the present tense: the state of being in the middle of the work, pushing past your own nature and habit. Against it: the beinoni "who has not served," who does everything right but has no struggle, because his temperament carries him; and above both, the tzaddik, *eved Hashem*, a servant of G-d as a title, the war already won.

**The chapter as consciousness.** The prophet says, "You will see the difference between the righteous and the wicked, between one who serves G-d and one who has not served Him." The difference between "one who serves" and "a righteous one" is this. *Oved*, serving, is in the present tense: he is in the middle of the work, which is the war with the bad pull, to overcome it and drive it out of the small city. That is truly great work and toil, to fight it always. That is the beinoni. The tzaddik is called "servant of G-d," a title, like "wise man" or "king": he already became one. He has finished the war with the bad, and his heart is hollow within him.

Among beinonim there are also two levels: "one who serves" and "one who has not served." Even the second is not wicked. He never did the lightest sin, he keeps every commandment he can, and his mouth does not stop learning. But he fights no war with his pull, because his pull does not stand against him at all. He is diligent in learning by nature, from his temperament. He has no struggle with desire because he is cool by nature. He lacks the feeling of pleasure in this world's delights. So he does not need to reflect much on G-d's greatness to birth awe and love. The hidden love in every Jewish heart is enough for him. So he is not called "one who serves," because that hidden love is not his doing. It is an inheritance from our fathers. The same is true of one who trained himself to learn diligently until the habit became second nature: the hidden love is enough for him, unless he wants to learn more than his habit.

That explains the Talmud: "one who serves G-d" is one who reviews his lesson a hundred and one times, and "one who has not served" reviews it a hundred times. In their days the habit was to review each lesson a hundred times. The Talmud's picture is the donkey drivers' market: they hire out for ten parsas for one coin, but eleven parsas cost two coins, because it is beyond their habit. So the hundred and first time, beyond the habit of his youth, weighs as much as all the rest, and more, to earn the name "one who serves." To change the nature of a habit, he must wake love for G-d by reflecting on His greatness, to rule the nature in the left side of the heart, where nature comes from. That is whole service for a beinoni. Or he can wake the hidden love in his heart to rule his nature; that too is called service. But when there is no war at all, the love is not called his service.

As consciousness, this chapter unsettles the comfortable good person. Goodness that flows from temperament is real but it is not yet service. The state the chapter names is the edge of your own habit, the one more time, the one step past what is easy for you, where you must call on love to move. For the one whose nature is hot, the war is obvious. For the one whose nature is cool, it has to be found.

The market picture breaks gently: G-d does not pay by the mile. The hundred and first time is worth more because it is the one where you meet Him instead of your habit.

**The movement.** From goodness carried by nature and habit, to service that pushes past them. What carries a person across: reflecting on G-d's greatness to wake love, or waking the hidden love, and turning it against the pull of habit.

**Entering it.** Hold one line: "The hundred and first time is the one that serves." Think it through: where am I good because it is easy for me? Where does my habit stop? Bring it down to one real thing: one practice you already do. Find the point just past your usual. Where the picture breaks: this is not about counting; it is about meeting Him past your nature. Stop there. Words to Him: "Let me serve You, not only my habits." One act: today, go one step past your habit in one thing: one more review, one more minute of prayer, one more kindness, done out of love.

**Signs and the fake.** You have touched it when you feel the resistance of your own nature and move through it for His sake. Its fake is taking your temperament for virtue, or inventing a struggle to feel like a fighter.

## Chapter 16 · Wings for a dry heart

**The state.** The "great rule for beinonim": when no open fire of love comes, the awe and love hidden in the mind's understanding, *tevunah*, still give the deed *gadpin*, wings to fly upward. It is the state of a dry but honest heart, whose clear conclusion "it is fitting" moves the mouth and hands, and G-d joins that *machshavah tovah*, good thought, to the act.

**The chapter as consciousness.** The main thing for beinonim is to rule the nature in the left side of the heart by the light of G-d shining on the divine soul in the mind. He reflects on the Infinite's greatness to birth awe in the mind, to turn from every wrong, even a light rabbinic one, and love in the right side of the heart, longing to cleave to Him through the commandments and Torah.

And more: a great rule for beinonim. Even if his mind cannot reach far enough to birth an open love, a heart burning like flames with a longing he can feel, and there is only a love hidden in his mind and in the hidden places of his heart, it still works. (A note gives the Kabbalistic reason: some souls are from a state of pregnancy and hiddenness, not of birth and openness; we give it as that sentence.) What does that hidden love look like? The heart understands, through wisdom and understanding in the mind, the greatness of the Infinite, before whom everything counts as truly nothing. So it is fitting that every living soul should pine for Him, to cleave to Him and be absorbed in His light. And his own soul, too, should by rights be pining to leave its sheath, the body, and cleave to Him. Only, against their will, the souls live in the body and are bound to it, "like living widows." And no thought can grasp Him except as He is dressed in Torah and the commandments, like embracing the King in chapter 4. So it is fitting for the soul to embrace Him with all its heart, soul and might: by keeping the commandments in deed, speech and thought, which is understanding Torah.

When he goes deep into this in the hidden places of his heart and mind, and his mouth and heart are the same, so that he does with his mouth what his understanding concluded, delights in Torah day and night with his mouth, and his hands do the commandments as his understanding concluded, then this understanding dresses itself in the deed, speech and thought, and becomes their mind, their life, and "wings to fly upward," as if he had done them with open awe and love burning in his heart. Because this understanding is what brought him to do them. Without it he would not do them at all; he would tend only to his body. (Even one diligent by nature loves his body more, by nature.)

The Sages hinted at this: "A good thought, G-d joins it to the deed." They should have said "counts it as if he did it." The explanation: open awe and love in the heart can dress themselves in the deed on their own, since the heart is also physical, like the limbs that act, only it is inner and gives them life. So it can dress itself in them and be their wings. But the awe and love in the mind's understanding and the heart's hidden places are too high to dress themselves in the deed, unless G-d joins them to it. That is why they are called "good thought," since they are not open awe and love in the heart. G-d joins them to raise the deed to the world of Beriah, where deeds done with open, thoughtful awe and love rise. Without this, deeds still rise to the world of Yetzirah through the natural awe and love hidden in every heart.

As consciousness, this chapter is for the dry season. Many people wait for a feeling before they act, and it does not come. The chapter says the understanding is enough, if it is honest and if it reaches the mouth and hands. The "living widows" picture names a real state: the soul bound in a body, longing without being able to feel its longing. The picture breaks here: a widow's husband is gone. This soul's Beloved is present, dressed in the very commandment she can do today.

**The movement.** From waiting for a feeling that does not come, to acting from a clear, quiet understanding, with mouth and heart the same. What carries a person across: going deep into "it is fitting," letting it reach speech and deed, and G-d, who joins the good thought to the act.

**Entering it.** Hold one line: "It is fitting that my soul pine for Him." Think it through: before Him everything is as nothing. So it would be right for my soul to long for Him. It is bound in me, like a living widow. Bring it down to one real thing: one commandment you have been waiting to feel like doing. Where the picture breaks: the widow's Beloved is here, inside the commandment. Stop there. Words to Him: "I do not feel it, but I know it. Join my thought to my hands." One act: do that commandment today, dry as you are, because it is fitting, and say the reason out loud.

**Signs and the fake.** You have touched it when you act from a clear conclusion without waiting for fire, and there is a quiet steadiness in it. Its fake is a conclusion that never reaches the mouth or hands, or "my love is hidden" said to excuse never reflecting at all.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 16 (line 256): רק שבעל כרחן חיות הנה בתוך הגוף וצרורות בו כאלמנות חיות*

## Chapter 17 · Your mind is yours

**The state.** *Mocho birshuso*, "his mind is in his own power": the state of a free mind that can turn toward what it chooses, and so birth the love that brings a person to act, *re'usa deliba*, the heart's will in its hidden places. Its opposite is the captured state, where a person is "in the power of his heart"; the way out of that begins with *lev nishbar*, a broken heart.

**The chapter as consciousness.** Now the book can explain its founding verse: "The thing is very near to you, in your mouth and in your heart, to do it." "In your heart" seems to go against our experience. It is not very near to turn one's heart from this world's desires to a true love of G-d. The Talmud asks, "Is awe a small thing?" How much more so love. And the Sages said only the righteous have their heart in their power.

But the verse says "to do it." It means the love that brings a person to do the commandments, the heart's will in its hidden places, even when it is not an open fire. That is very near and easy for anyone who has a brain in his head, because his mind is in his power, and he can reflect on whatever he wants. When he reflects on the Infinite's greatness, he will, as a matter of course, birth in his mind at least a love for G-d, to cleave to Him by keeping His commandments and Torah. And "this is the whole of man," since "today to do them": today is the world of doing, and tomorrow the reward. The mind rules by nature over the left side of the heart, over the mouth, and over every limb that acts.

The exception is one who is truly wicked. The Sages said "the wicked are in the power of their hearts," with no rule over them, as a consequence of the greatness of their sin. The chapter says harshly that the Torah did not speak to these, who are called dead while alive. Its point is this: for such a person, the mind cannot be the starting place. He cannot begin to serve G-d without first returning for the past, to break the husks that have become a screen and an iron wall between him and his Father in heaven. He breaks them through a broken heart and a bitter soul over his sins. "The offerings of G-d are a broken spirit, a broken heart": through a broken heart, the spirit of impurity is broken. This is the lower return, raising the lower letter *heh* from where it fell among the outside forces, which is the exile of the Presence. When a person does the deeds of Edom, he draws the divine spark that gives life to his soul down into the animal soul in the left of his heart, which rules him while he is wicked, and his soul is captive there. When his heart breaks within him, the spirit of the other side breaks, and the spark rises from its fall and stands.

As consciousness, the chapter draws a line most people never look for. For almost everyone, the mind is free. You can decide what to think about. The claim "I cannot control my thoughts" is usually not true, and the chapter sweeps it away: a person with a brain in his head can turn it to G-d's greatness. And for the one whose heart truly drags him, the way is not thinking harder but a heart that breaks. That breaking is not sadness. It is a bitter honesty that shatters a wall.

The phrase "dead while alive" is harsh, and it is meant honestly: a life ruled wholly by the heart's wants has lost the thing that makes a person alive. The picture of the iron wall breaks in hope: iron does not break from inside, but this wall does, when the heart breaks, and the captive spark stands up.

**The movement.** For most: from a mind that drifts to a mind that chooses its subject, and from there to a love that leads to action. For the one who is captured: from a heart that rules him to a broken heart, which breaks the wall and frees the spark. What carries a person across: the mind's own power to reflect, and for the captured, bitterness over the past that turns into return.

**Entering it.** Hold one line: "My mind is mine. I can turn it where I want." Think it through: I chose what to think about just now. I can choose G-d's greatness instead. Bring it down to one real thing: one minute today, turn your mind on purpose to "He fills this room." Where the picture breaks: if your heart drags you, begin not with thinking but with an honest, broken heart. Stop there. Words to Him: "Here is my mind. I give it to You now." One act: do one commandment today because that one minute of thought brought you to it.

**Signs and the fake.** You have touched it when you turn your mind to Him on purpose and a quiet love to do His will follows. Its fake is "I can't help what I think," or, for the broken heart, pity for yourself and gloom in place of a bitterness that breaks the wall and gets up.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 17 (line 265): כי מוחו ברשותו ויכול להתבונן בו ככל אשר יחפוץ*

## Chapter 18 · Beneath the mind: a faith older than reasons

**The state.** The *ahavah mesuteres*, the hidden love inherited from the Patriarchs, rooted in the soul's *chochmah*, the point above understanding where the Infinite Himself is dressed. Its face is *emunah*, faith above knowing: the simple certainty in which it is impossible to deny the One. "I am always with You."

**The chapter as consciousness.** The verse said "very near," and now the chapter explains the word "very." Even one whose knowledge of G-d is short, who has no heart to understand the Infinite's greatness and cannot birth awe and love even in his mind, still has it very near to keep all the commandments and Torah, in his mouth and heart, from the depth of the heart, in truth, with awe and love. That is the hidden love in the heart of every Jew, an inheritance from our fathers. First the chapter explains where this love comes from, what it is, how it is inherited, and how awe is included in it.

The Patriarchs were "the chariot," wholly given over to G-d, so they could draw souls for their children after them from the ten holy attributes of the four worlds, each according to his level and deeds. Even to the lightest of the light and to those who rebel, there comes at least the lowest level of holiness in the world of Asiyah. And yet, since that comes from the ten holy attributes, it holds all of them, including chochmah. Inside it is dressed the chochmah of higher and higher worlds, until the chochmah in which the light of the Infinite truly shines. (The chapter traces the nesting through the worlds; we give it as that sentence.) So the Infinite is dressed in the chochmah of every person's soul, whoever he is, and that chochmah, with the Infinite light inside it, spreads through the whole soul and gives it life, from head to foot: "wisdom gives life to those who have it." Sometimes, the chapter adds, even rebels draw very high souls from the depths.

Chochmah is the source of understanding, above binah, which is the grasping of an idea. It is *koach mah*, "the power of what," what is not yet grasped or understood. That is why the Infinite light, which no thought can grasp, dresses itself in it. And that is why every Jew, even women and the unlearned, believes in G-d. Faith is above knowing. "The simple one believes every word," and next to the Holy One, who is above all mind, everyone is simple: "I was a fool and did not know; I was like a beast with You; and I am always with You." That is: by being a fool and a beast, I am always with You. So even the lightest of the light and those who rebel, for the most part, give their lives for G-d's holiness and bear terrible suffering rather than deny the One. Even if they are ignorant and do not know His greatness, and do not reflect on the little they know, they do not give their lives because of reasons. They do it without any thought, as if it were simply impossible to deny the One, with no reason and no argument at all. Because the One G-d lights up and gives life to the whole soul, by dressing Himself in its chochmah, which is above knowing and reason.

As consciousness, this is the state beneath all the states of the earlier chapters. Mind, feeling, struggle, even the beinoni's watchfulness, all rest on a point that cannot say no to Him. From inside it does not feel like a conclusion. It feels like an impossibility: I simply cannot deny Him. It usually sleeps, and wakes in a real test. The person who thinks "I am too simple for contemplation" learns that the simple place is exactly where the One is closest.

The picture of the simpleton breaks here. A simple person believes anything because he cannot judge. This faith is not weak judgment. It is the Infinite in you, higher than judgment.

**The movement.** From "I cannot understand, so I cannot serve" to discovering the love that was always there beneath understanding, and letting it move the mouth, heart and hands. What carries a person across: knowing the hidden love is his inheritance, and remembering the point in him that could never deny the One.

**Entering it.** Hold one line: "I am a fool before You, and I am always with You." Think it through: is there anything that would make me say, and mean, that there is no G-d? Stay with how impossible that is. Bring it down to one real thing: that impossibility is the Infinite in your chochmah, alive right now. Where the picture breaks: this is not blind belief; it is higher than seeing. Stop at the edge, and do not try to explain it. Words to Him: "I cannot understand You, and I am with You always." One act: keep one commandment today with no reason in mind except that He is yours and you are His.

**Signs and the fake.** You have touched it when a quiet, unshakable "yes" to Him is there under every mood, needing no argument. Its fake is stubborn pride in an identity dressed up as faith, or a belief that is really just an argument you once won.
