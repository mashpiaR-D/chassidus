<a id="c22"></a> ## C22 · I've given up; what's the use?

*10 findings from Sha'arei Teshuvah, Imrei Binah, Toras Chaim (part 2 of 4), Toras Chaim (part 3 of 4), Toras Chaim (part 4 of 4), Sefer HaSichos (part 1 of 2), Likkutei Sichos (part 3 of 3), Biurei Tanya, Sha'arei Avodah*

> I've tried so hard for so long and nothing has changed, so why keep fighting? It's hopeless. And since I'll never be good anyway, I might as well do what I want.

**What is really going on.** The works read despair not as a clear view of the truth but as a mechanism, and often as a disguise. Biurei Tanya: sadness says 'no hope' to the soul and then gives permission: 'I'm not a tzaddik anyway, so I'll do what my heart wants'. It grows from demanding completeness of oneself. The Rebbe's letters (Likkutei Sichos) call despair one of the inclination's clearest tactics for getting a person off the hook; the sign is that it does nothing to dim the appetites. The Rebbe Rayatz: 'what can you do' is despair built on laziness and falsehood. The Mitteler Rebbe (Toras Chaim, Sha'arei Teshuvah, Imrei Binah): this is a world of change, where every rise is preceded by a matching fall; real change can't be seen while it happens; and despair can be the seed rotting before it grows, but only if it passes through humility into joy. Holy bitterness grieves the distance and pleads for mercy, while despair slides into craving and anger. Sha'arei Avodah: you don't have to finish, only to fight by your own measure.

**Where the works agree.** Despair is never the truth about the person. It is either an excuse with a motive or a phase in a longer rhythm. All the works send the person back to action by their own measure, and all insist that any grief over failure must end in joy and a deed, not in collapse.

**Where they differ.** Toras Chaim and Imrei Binah find a use for near-despair: the seed rots, then light sprouts. The Rebbe's letters and Biurei Tanya treat despair purely as the other side, to be exposed and dropped, not used. Sha'arei Teshuvah says don't search yourself at all, while Biurei Tanya and the letters ask you to diagnose your sadness by its vitality or fruit. The Rebbe Rayatz exposes it as laziness; the Mitteler Rebbe consoles with the rhythm of change.

**Order and timing.** First, test the heaviness by what it produces. If it is an excuse, do one small act now. If the person is worn out after real effort, offer the rhythm of change and invisible growth, and have them stop checking. Only for a stable person with life in them: brief chosen bitterness before prayer, ending in joy and a deed.

- Hopelessness with thoughts of death or self-harm needs immediate human help and crisis resources, not these frames. - 'Despair is the evil inclination's tactic' can land as blame on someone clinically depressed. The texts' test ('it doesn't dim your appetites') fails in depression, where appetite often does fall. - The rotting-seed teaching must never be read as encouraging someone to hit bottom. - Bitterness practices are not for the depressed or self-hating. - 'I might as well do what I want' may describe addiction, which needs treatment, not only reframing. - Consolations about losses in 'body, money, children' can wound the grieving if offered too fast.

<a id="c19"></a> ## C19 · Relentless guilt: endless self-checking and self-punishment

*7 findings from Tanya, Hayom Yom, Likkutei Sichos (part 3 of 3), Sha'arei Avodah, Chanah Ariel, Sheeris Yehudah*

> I keep examining myself and replaying guilt. I think real repentance means fasting and misery, that I must suffer before I'm clean. I can't stop thinking 'maybe it's forbidden'.

**What is really going on.** The works agree that return is not self-punishment. Tanya (Iggeres HaTeshuvah): the commandment is to leave the sin with a whole heart, and forgiveness comes at once, no less the thousandth time. Fasts that could harm health are forbidden, and charity replaces them. 'My sin is ever before me' means humility, not lifelong gloom. Sha'arei Avodah: you are out of the pit the moment you truly regret and leave the sin; cleansing is only wiping off the mud. Hayom Yom: truth is the middle path, and harsh self-strictness is as false as self-loving leniency. The Baal Shem Tov: refine the body, don't break it. The Tzemach Tzedek told a fasting daughter-in-law to eat and be strong. The Rebbe's letters: regret-thoughts that breed sadness and sloth are the inclination dressed as piety; divert attention rather than argue. Sheeris Yehudah: the anxious 'maybe it's forbidden' is the heart knocking from melancholy, and the sages are not pleased with it.

**The false conclusions, and what the works set against them**

- *"Real repentance means I have to suffer and pay before I'm clean."* → Return is leaving the sin with a whole heart, and it takes effect at once. Cleansing comes after, like wiping mud after climbing out. Fasts that harm health are forbidden. `TAN·Q18` · `SA·Q28` · `SA·Q29` · `TAN·Q19`

**Where the works agree.** All agree that return is a turning of the will, not a payment in suffering. Forgiveness is immediate, bodily self-punishment is rejected, and guilt that breeds gloom, paralysis or endless doubt is no sign of piety. The memory of wrongdoing has a limited, useful job, humility, and nothing more.

**Where they differ.** How much reckoning, and for whom: Tanya still prescribes a fixed hour of deep reckoning and, for coarse hearts, self-crushing, and Sha'arei Avodah's higher return begins with 'despising his life'. The Rebbe's letters tell some people to drop self-accounting altogether for now, and Hayom Yom asks for a periodic accounting that includes strengths. On self-denial, Hayom Yom's early Chassidim refused craved permitted things, while the Baal Shem Tov forbids mortification, and Chanah Ariel says it depends on the person.

**Order and timing.** First: the decision to leave the wrong, and trust in forgiveness, now. Then close the loop: no more checking for now, and put attention into action. A question of permission goes once to a merciful authority. Only when stable: a bounded accounting at a fixed time, strengths included, ending in joy.

<a id="c15"></a> ## C15 · Stuck in remorse and bitterness, with no room for joy

<a id="c23"></a> ## C23 · Can I really be forgiven, and trusted again?

*6 findings from Tanya, Derech Mitzvosecha, Kuntres Umaayan, Likkutei Sichos (part 3 of 3), Sheeris Yehudah*

> I fell again, the same thing so many times. The damage feels permanent. I feel bad, but is this real repentance or a formality? After a public mistake, can I ever be trusted again?

**What is really going on.** Tanya puts return at its simplest: a wholehearted decision to leave the wrong. Forgiveness comes from limitless mercy, where once and a thousand times are the same, and one should trust it at once. Harmful fasts are forbidden, and keeping the sin in view only guards against pride. Likkutei Sichos calls despair over a 'fake' return the evil inclination's counsel, measures return by direction, and reads even fear-driven return as the soul going home. Kuntres Umaayan says being bothered is already a start, and self-justification is what blocks it. Derech Mitzvosecha calls return healing: regret uproots the will behind the wrong, and spoken confession removes its body. Sheeris Yehudah, on a shamed slaughterer, separates honest error from willful sin and repairing the past from guarding the future.

**Where the works agree.** Every work denies that anyone is past return. A core remains untouched by any fall, the conclusion 'it's over' is itself the error, and there are ways back even when mind and heart have gone dark: speech, the cry, action, company. Return builds on what is still whole instead of creating something from nothing.

**Where they differ.** On bitterness: Torah Ohr prescribes it at a set time, even over sins already repented. Pelach HaRimon warns it cannot hold steady and may cause a fall. Sha'arei Teshuvah has it arise only from real extremity. Hayom Yom and Inyanah go straight to wholeness and hope with no bitterness. Torah Ohr and Sha'arei Teshuvah see value in the fall itself (descent for ascent); Hayom Yom says only that it is not final. Likkutei Sichos turns the person outward to others.

**Order and timing.** First break the verdict: the untouched core, nothing is lost, mood bends judgment. Daily: Modeh Ani on waking, and words of Torah and prayer said aloud when dull. Diagnose and keep a simple regimen, with a partner and an elder. Turn toward helping others. Set-time bitterness only later and only if steady, always ending in joy. The cry from the heart comes unbidden, in real extremity.

- 'It's over for me' can signal suicidal risk. Safety and professional help come first, before any teaching. - Never present bitterness, the 'rotting seed' or the story of a soul departing in remorse to someone depressed, grieving or at risk of self-harm. - 'Descent for ascent' must not sound as if the wrong done was good, or be used to minimise harm to others. - The 'untouched core' teaching is not a way to skip accountability or repair toward people harmed. - Gatherings with drink are contraindicated for anyone with alcohol problems. - 'Know that you are ill' must always come with the equal clause of hope, and is not a substitute for clinical care.

<a id="c43"></a> ## C43 · Growing, yet feeling worse or stuck

*3 findings from Imrei Binah, Toras Chaim (part 1 of 4), Likkutei Sichos (part 3 of 3)*

> The more I grow, the more faults I find in myself. Am I getting worse? Or I'm full of what I've already reached, and nothing new opens up.

**What is really going on.** Two different complaints share this condition. The first: new faults appear as a person grows. Imrei Binah says the more one recognises divine light, the more one feels even small wrongs. Likkutei Sichos says rising makes finer faults count, as a stain matters on a scholar's garment and not on others'. Sensitivity is read as a sign of light, not decline. The second: stagnation. Toras Chaim says no new level comes 'from something to something'. As long as mind and heart are full of what was already felt and reached, one cannot rise. The earlier self has to be set aside first, like a seed rotting before it sprouts, in prayer, in learning and even in livelihood. Imrei Binah also warns of the opposite blindness: someone who takes darkness for light and sees no conflict between prayer and following every desire. The evidence is modest.

**The false conclusions, and what the works set against them**

- *"I keep finding new faults; I must be getting worse."* → The more light one sees, the more one feels even subtle wrongs. Rising makes finer faults count. `IB·Q26` · `IB·Q30` · `LS3·Q25`
- *"What I've reached and felt should carry me further."* → While mind and heart are full of the earlier attainment, one cannot rise to a higher level; each new level requires setting the last one aside. `TC1·Q11` · `TC1·Q09`
- *"If I work harder and smarter, I'll get there."* → The flow from above comes after giving up 'my strength and cleverness'; one sows in tears and then reaps. `TC1·Q08` · `TC1·Q31`
- *"I'm fine; I don't notice anything wrong."* → A person can take darkness for light, praying with his mouth while following every desire all day, without feeling any conflict. `IB·Q24` · `IB·Q25` · `IB·Q29`

1. **Two stages of setting the self aside before a new level** · *from Toras Chaim (Bereishis vol. 1)* (1) Before prayer: a broken heart and 'who am I', which sets aside the crude sense of self. (2) In the songs before prayer, climb and strip off the earlier form. (3) In the silent prayer, stand before the King like a mute stone and bow; new light is received. (4) In livelihood, give up 'my strength and cleverness' first; success follows. (5) In learning, make yourself lowly; the less you feel yourself, the deeper you grasp. *When:* Each daily prayer, in the order of the liturgy, and whenever a new level of understanding or success is sought. *For:* Someone so full of his own attainments, feelings or efforts that he cannot grow. *Not for:* Someone with low self-worth or depression; 'who am I' and the broken heart can feed self-contempt. In livelihood, not a reason for financial passivity. `TC1·Q09` · `TC1·Q11` · `TC1·Q27` · `TC1·Q08` · `TC1·Q31` · `TC1·Q10`
2. **Letting light show the dark** · *from Imrei Binah* (1) Contemplate the greatness and awe of God's kingship until it is clear in heart and mind; accept His yoke. (2) Let that light show the coarse darkness you had taken for light. (3) Set the whole heart on one Father in heaven, with no other master beside Him. (4) Turning from empty pleasures then follows by itself. *When:* Daily, with acceptance of the yoke of Heaven. *For:* Someone who does not notice a divided life and feels 'fine'. Note this is the opposite of the over-sensitive grower. *Not for:* The scrupulous or shame-prone. The text's imagery and its 'seeing oneself as truly bad' can wound, and it is not for someone already finding too many faults. `IB·Q24` · `IB·Q25` · `IB·Q29` · `IB·Q30`

- From *"The closer I get, the worse I look to myself; I must be getting worse."* to **The more light you see, the more you sense subtle wrongs. Finer faults show up because you have risen.** Growing sensitivity is read as a sign of growing light, not moral decline, which stops the discouragement that would reverse the growth. *(Imrei Binah, Likkutei Sichos (vols. 37-39))* `IB·Q26` · `IB·Q30` · `LS3·Q25` - From *"What I've reached and felt is mine, and it should carry me further."* to **Every new level first requires letting go of the last, as a seed must rot before it grows.** Nothing new comes 'from something to something'. Only setting aside the felt self makes room for the higher light. *(Toras Chaim (Bereishis vol. 1))* `TC1·Q11` · `TC1·Q10` - From *"If I just work harder and smarter, I'll make it."* to **Success comes after letting go of 'my strength and my cleverness', like one who sows in tears and then reaps.** Giving up self-reliance is presented as the condition for receiving. *(Toras Chaim (Bereishis vol. 1))* `TC1·Q08` · `TC1·Q31`

**Where the works agree.** Growth changes the lens. What was invisible becomes visible, and what was an achievement becomes an obstacle. In both cases the self's view of its own progress is unreliable, and the way forward is not to hold on to the last stage.

**Where they differ.** Imrei Binah and Likkutei Sichos mainly reassure: new faults are a sign of light. Likkutei Sichos also says the demand for return rises with the level. Toras Chaim prescribes a broken heart and a mute standing before the King. Imrei Binah also addresses the opposite person, blind to his faults, with a program that would hurt the over-sensitive.

**Order and timing.** First, read new faults as a sign of more light; this reassurance comes before anything else. If growth has stalled from fullness, let go of the last level within prayer's order: lowering, climbing, standing mute. Carry the same letting go into learning and work. The 'light shows the dark' practice is only for someone who does not see his faults.

- For the scrupulous, 'finer and finer faults' can become an endless hunt; keep the emphasis on reassurance. - 'Who am I' and the broken heart can feed self-contempt in someone depressed or with low self-worth. - 'Give up hope in your own toil' must not become financial passivity or hopelessness. - Imrei Binah's imagery of unfaithfulness and 'seeing oneself as bad' can wound the shame-prone.
