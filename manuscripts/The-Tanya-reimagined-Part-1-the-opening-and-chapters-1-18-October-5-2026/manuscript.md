This part covers the book's doorway and its first long stretch. The title page and the compiler's foreword open it, told briefly, the way a friend might hand you a letter before you read the book it came with. Chapters 1-18 follow. They start where people really are, split between a pull toward the good and a pull toward the self. They name two souls, find a part of G-d under everything, and teach how the mind can hold a truth until the heart answers. They build a floor you can stand on (the beinoni, the one in the middle, whose bad never wins in thought, speech or deed), and they end under the mind altogether, in a love and a faith older than reasons. Each section retells the chapter plainly, names the unity in it, and gives a short sitting. Every source line was read in the Hebrew and checked against the source file (`Tanya — Hebrew text.txt`, line numbers as given). Where a chapter goes into lists or Kabbalah, the retelling compresses it and says so.

## Title page · Very near

The book has a long name and a short one. The long one is *Collected Sayings*. The short one is *The Book of the In-Between People*: the ones who are not saints and not villains, who get up every day and try.

Then comes a promise, put before a single argument. The whole book, it says, is built on one verse: "The thing is very near to you, in your mouth and in your heart, to do it." The book's job is to show *how* it is very near, "in a long way and a short way."

"The thing" is the whole of serving G-d. It sounds far away, like a mountain for other people to climb. The verse says no. It is in your mouth: words you can say today. It is in your heart: a love that is already there. It is in your hands: something you can do.

The book will spend fifty-three chapters showing why. What you are looking for is not outside you. It is already inside, waiting to be noticed.

"Near" usually means a short distance. By the end, the book will show there is no distance at all, only forgetting. The One is not far from where you sit.

**Sit with it** *(3 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Notice the old feeling that holiness belongs to other people. Let it be there. - **The line, softly:** "It is very near to me: in my mouth, in my heart, in my hands." - **Think it through.** Your mouth is here. Say one real word to G-d, even just "Thank You." Your heart is here. Your hands are on the table. The book isn't asking you to become someone else first. - **Where the picture breaks.** "Near" still sounds like two places close together. The book will say it is closer than that. - **From inside.** A small, plain confidence: this is for me, and it is for today. - **The edge.** Not yet. This is a doorway. The book will reach, in chapter 18, a love older than thinking. - **Into the day.** Say one sentence of prayer or Torah out loud today, slowly. (Anyone: one verse of Psalms, or your own words to G-d.) That's the "mouth" part of near.

*Source: Tanya, title page (line 18): כי קרוב אליך הדבר מאוד, בפיך ובלבבך לעשותו · Step 1 of the one process · Sitting L01*

## Compiler's foreword · A letter to the people who asked

Before any teaching, the author writes a letter. He writes to "all our people," great and small, as someone who has sat with them and heard them pour out their hearts.

He starts with an honest worry. Hearing a teacher in person is not like reading a book. A reader reads through his own mind. If his mind is confused and he walks in the dark, he can hardly see the good light hidden in books, "though the light is sweet to the eyes and healing to the soul."

Also, books built on human reasoning don't fit everyone. No two minds are moved by the same thing. Even the holy books of the Sages don't show each person his own place in them. Every soul, down to "the lightest spark," is tied into the Torah, and the Torah ties it to G-d. But not everyone gets to know which thread is his.

So he speaks to the ones he knows: the people who came to him in private and told him the secrets of their hearts. These pages, he says, are answers to their questions. He can no longer answer each person one by one, and people forget. So he wrote the answers down, so each could keep them "between his eyes," find rest for his soul, and good advice for whatever is hard for him.

And if you can't understand something here, bring it to the wise people in your town. And to them he makes a request: don't hide behind a false humility. Explain it.

The letter ends with a short note about the printing: many hand-copies had spread with many mistakes, so friends brought a clean text to the press.

*The Tanya speaks here of the Jewish people and its particular root; this adaptation applies it to every person.*

The question you carry about your inner life was asked before you asked it, and the answer was written with you in mind. You are one thread in a rope that is tied to the King.

**Sit with it** *(4 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Let one real question about your inner life come up. Don't try to answer it. - **The line, softly:** "My question was heard. I am one thread, tied to Him." - **Think it through.** A rope of many threads. Some thick, some thin. The thinnest is still part of the rope, and the rope is tied at the top. - **Where the picture breaks.** A rope is outside the one who holds it. The book will say (chapters 4-5) that the Torah and G-d are one, so the tie is no rope at all. - **From inside.** Being spoken to. Not alone with it. - **The edge.** Not yet. This names the bond; the chapters to come will show how close it is. - **Into the day.** Bring your one question to someone wiser this week, and ask it plainly.

*Source: Tanya, Compiler's foreword (line 54): כולהו מתקשראן באורייתא, ואורייתא – היא המקשרת אותן להקדוש ברוך הוא · Step 1 of the one process · Sitting L14*

## Chapter 1 · Two voices in one person

The book opens with an oath. The Talmud says that before a soul is born, it is made to swear: "Be righteous, and don't be wicked. And even if the whole world tells you that you're righteous, be in your own eyes like someone wicked."

Right away the Tanya asks the hard questions. Another teaching says, "Don't be wicked in your own eyes." And besides, if a person sees himself as wicked, his heart will sink. He'll be sad, and he won't be able to serve G-d with joy. But if it doesn't bother him at all, he might grow careless. So which is it?

To answer, the Tanya lays out five kinds of people from the Talmud: the righteous who has it good, the righteous who has it hard, the wicked who has it good, the wicked who has it hard, and the beinoni, the one in the middle. It makes a surprising point. The beinoni is not someone with half good deeds and half bad. The great sage Rabbah called himself a beinoni, and he never stopped learning for a moment. He could not have been wrong about being half-sinful. So the beinoni must be something else, something the book will spend chapters explaining. (The chapter works through several proofs from the Talmud and Zohar here; we give only the conclusion.)

Then the root of the answer. Every person, the good and the bad alike, has two souls.

One soul comes from the side that hides G-d. It lives in the blood and keeps the body alive. From it come the harder traits, each from one of four "elements": anger and pride from fire, which rises; the craving for pleasures from water, which makes things grow; silliness, mockery, boasting and empty talk from air; and laziness and sadness from earth. And this same soul, the Tanya adds, is also where a person's natural kindness and mercy come from. It is a mixed soul: mostly self, with some good in it.

The chapter goes on to say that the souls of the nations come from a lower source with no good in it at all, and that their kindness is done for their own sake. It says this plainly, and we pass it on as it is.

The other soul, the second one, is the subject of the next chapter.

*The Tanya speaks here of the Jewish soul and its particular root, and contrasts it with the nations; this adaptation applies the teaching of the two souls to every person.*

The fight inside you is not proof that you are a fraud. It is two voices with two sources in one person. Seeing that clearly is the first step toward the One who is under both.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of this morning: one moment of impatience, one moment of kindness. Let both be there. - **The line, softly:** "There are two voices in me. Neither one is the whole of me." - **Think it through.** Fire rising: anger, pride. Water spreading: wanting. Air: empty talk. Earth: heaviness, sadness. Find one of these in your own week, without judging it. Then remember: your natural kindness grows from the same ground. - **Where the picture breaks.** "Two souls" can sound like two people. It is one person, one body, one small city that both want to rule (chapter 9). - **From inside.** Relief. The pull inside is not the whole of you. You can look at your anger without denying it and without being it. - **The edge.** Not here. This chapter only maps the ground. - **Into the day.** When a harsh feeling rises today, name it quietly: "That's the other voice." Don't argue with it.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 1 (line 82): דלכל איש ישראל, אחד צדיק ואחד רשע, יש שתי נשמות · Step 1 of the one process · no sitting directly*

## Chapter 2 · Breathed out from the inside

There's a second life in you, under the one that wants and worries. The Tanya says it plainly: it is *truly a part of G-d above*. Not a gift sent from far away. A part.

It gives a picture. "He breathed into him the breath of life." When you blow a real breath, from deep in your chest, what comes out comes from your inside. That's how your soul came from Him: out of His innermost.

Then a second picture. A child comes from the father's mind. Every part of the child grew from that first seed, even the toenails. The seed went down, stage after stage, for nine months, until part of it became nails. And even now the nails are fed from the head.

So it is with every soul, from the greatest to the simplest person who hardly knows a thing. Souls differ without end: some are like the head, some like the heels. But each came down level after level, through all the worlds. Each is still tied at the root, "in a wonderful and mighty union," to where it began, in His own wisdom. And He and His wisdom are one: He is the knower and the known.

How can that be? The Tanya admits it: no human mind can understand this fully.

Two short teachings follow. Because the simpler souls draw their life from the great ones of their generation, the Sages said that clinging to the wise is like clinging to G-d Himself. And parents matter: the soul comes to a child wrapped in a garment from them, so their holiness at the moment of conception helps. Yet the soul itself is its own. Sometimes a very high soul is born to very plain parents.

*The Tanya speaks here of the Jewish soul and its particular root; this adaptation applies it to every person.*

Nothing is outside Him, and the closest proof is the deepest you. Finding yourself all the way down and finding Him turn out to be the same finding.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Whatever you've decided about yourself today, let it be there. Don't argue with it. - **The line, softly:** "The deepest me is a part of G-d above." - **Think it through.** A breath from deep inside. A toenail still fed from the head. Then your own hands on the table. Whoever is moving them has a root in His wisdom. - **Where the picture breaks.** A child grows up and leaves home. This never leaves. - **From inside.** A floor under the floor. What you think of yourself is true on its own level. Underneath it is something nothing has touched. - **The edge.** The mind can't hold how a part of Him is in you. Don't try to grasp it. Rest in it. - **Into the day.** Before you stand up, say quietly: "Under all of this, a part of G-d above."

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 2 (line 86): ונפש השנית בישראל, היא חלק אלוה ממעל ממש · Step 2 of the one process · Sitting L01*

## Chapter 3 · Hold it until it warms

The G-dly soul has ten powers, the Tanya says, matching the ten sefirot above. They fall into two groups: the mind, and the feelings. The mind has three parts. Feelings like love and awe are its children.

Here is how it works. The first flash of a thought is called *chochmah*, wisdom. The Tanya reads the word as *koach mah*, "the power of *what*": a point that hasn't been worked out yet. When you take that point and think it through, "to its truth and its depth," that is *binah*, understanding. These two are the father and mother. Together they give birth to love and awe.

What should the mind think about? The Tanya gives it at once. How G-d *fills all worlds*. How He *surrounds all worlds*. How *everything before Him counts as nothing*. When a person thinks about this deeply, awe is born: a kind of shyness before a greatness with no end. Then the heart catches fire with love "like flames of fire," a longing and thirst for Him, until the soul feels it could pour itself out. "My soul longs, even faints." "My soul thirsts for G-d." (The chapter adds that this thirst comes from the fire in the heart, while the mind is like water.)

Then comes the hinge, the third part of the mind: *da'at*, knowing. The word is the Bible's word for the closest bond of all, "Adam *knew* Eve." Da'at means binding. You tie your mind to the greatness of the Infinite with a strong knot, and you *fix your thought there and don't let it drift*.

And here is the warning. Even someone wise, who understands all this about G-d, if he doesn't bind his mind and stay there, won't get real love and awe. He'll get "false imaginings" instead. So da'at is what keeps the feelings alive.

Understanding the One and meditating on the One are not two jobs. Understanding held long enough is the meditation, and the heart answering is its fruit.

**Sit with it** *(6 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Let your mind be as busy as it is. You're not emptying it. You're about to give it something to hold. - **The line, softly:** "He fills this place, He surrounds it, and before Him it is as nothing." - **Think it through.** Take one part at a time. He fills this room, this chair, this hand. He surrounds all of it the same, near and far. Then: before Him, all of it counts as nothing. Now hold that one thought, the way you'd carry a full cup, without putting it down. - **Where the picture breaks.** Father and mother give birth, and the child exists on its own. Here the children are feelings, and they're born only if the parents stay together long enough. - **From inside.** When the mind stays, something may warm: first a hush before a greatness with no end, then a pull toward it. If nothing warms, the staying still counts. - **The edge.** The mind is trying to hold what has no edge. Let it keep trying, gently. That's the practice. - **Into the day.** Pick one line of today's prayer, or one verse of Psalms, and don't let your mind leave it until you've finished it.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 3 (line 112): ויתקע מחשבתו בחוזק בגדולת אין סוף ברוך הוא, ואינו מסיח דעתו · Step 6 of the one process · Sitting L07*

## Chapter 4 · Embracing the King

The soul also has three garments, three ways it goes out into the world: thought, speech and action. When these are filled with the Torah's commands (thinking Torah, speaking Torah, doing mitzvot) the whole soul is dressed in them. Love is the root of all the "do" commands, because the one who really keeps them is the one who wants to cling to G-d. Awe is the root of all the "don't" commands: you don't want to rebel against the King, or, deeper, you are ashamed to do what He hates right in front of Him.

These garments, the Tanya says, are higher than the soul itself. Why? Because the Torah is His wisdom and will, and He and His wisdom are one.

But how can anyone hold the Infinite? No thought can grasp Him at all. Here the Tanya brings a saying: "Where you find G-d's greatness, there you find His humility." He narrowed His will and wisdom down into 613 commands and their laws, into letters of ink on parchment, so that a mind can grasp them and a hand can do them.

That's why the Torah is compared to water. Water runs from a high place to a low one. The Torah came down from its place, His own wisdom, step after step through all the worlds, until it was dressed in physical things: food, money, objects, a page.

And so, when the soul is dressed in Torah from head to foot, it is wrapped in G-d's own light. This is why the Sages said: one hour of return and good deeds in this world is better than all of the World to Come. There, souls enjoy a glow of G-d. Here, in the doing, you hold Him Himself.

Then the picture. When you embrace a king, it makes no difference if he's wearing one robe or many. The king's body is inside them. And if the king embraces *you* with his arm, it's still his arm, even through the sleeve. "His right hand embraces me."

The Infinite that no thought can reach chose to be held. Every mitzvah is a sleeve with the King's arm inside it.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of one good thing you did with your hands this week. Let it be small. - **The line, softly:** "The King is inside this." - **Think it through.** Water running downhill, all the way to the lowest place. A king in many robes. Then the strap of tefillin, the coin you gave, the page you read. (Anyone: the kindness you did, the charity you gave.) Each is a robe with Him inside. - **Where the picture breaks.** A king's body has an end. What's inside this robe has none. - **From inside.** Held. A closeness that doesn't wait for you to feel it. - **The edge.** No thought grasps Him. The edge is crossed not by thinking but by doing. In the deed you hold what the mind can't. - **Into the day.** When you do one mitzvah or one kind act today, stop for a moment and say: "The King is inside this."

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 4 (line 127): הרי זה כמחבק את המלך דרך משל · Steps 4 and 14 of the one process · Sitting L14*

## Chapter 5 · Holding and held

The last chapter said that no thought can grasp Him. This one looks closer at what "grasp" means.

When you understand something, your mind wraps around it. It holds the idea, surrounds it. And at the same time, your mind is wrapped inside the idea. While you're really understanding it, you're in it.

Take a law in the Talmud. If Reuven claims this and Shimon claims that, the ruling is this. Maybe that case never happened and never will. Still, it came up in G-d's will and wisdom that if it did, this would be the ruling. So when you learn that law and truly understand it, your mind is holding G-d's own will and wisdom. And your mind is dressed in it.

This is "a wonderful union, with no union like it anywhere in the physical world." One in every direction.

That's why knowing Torah stands higher than all the other mitzvot. The others dress the soul from outside. But when you understand Torah, His wisdom is also *inside* you.

So Torah is called bread, food for the soul. Bread goes into you and becomes your own flesh and blood. Torah that you learn well, until you really grasp it, becomes one with you. It turns into the life of your soul, from the Life of all life inside it. "Your Torah is within my innards."

The Tanya adds: learning "for its own sake" means learning to bind your soul to G-d, each person by his own mind. And a closing note: the mitzvot are like clothing around the soul, while Torah is both food inside and clothing around, and the words you say aloud surround you too.

To know one small, exact thing of His is to hold Him and be held by Him at once. The mind touches the Infinite by holding something small and true.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Bring to mind one thing you learned recently, from the Torah or a holy book. - **The line, softly:** "I hold His wisdom, and it holds me." - **Think it through.** A mind wrapped around an idea; an idea wrapped around the mind. Reuven, Shimon, a ruling that may never be needed, and still it's His will. Then bread becoming your own body. The line you learned is now inside you like food. - **Where the picture breaks.** Bread gets used up. This food is His own wisdom, and it never grows less. - **From inside.** Learning stops feeling dry. It feels like being fed. - **The edge.** His wisdom is grasped only as it's dressed in the laws before us. You touch the Infinite by holding something small and exact. - **Into the day.** Learn one law or one verse today slowly, until you could explain it to someone else.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 5 (line 134): והוא יחוד נפלא שאין יחוד כמוהו ולא כערכו נמצא כלל בגשמיות · Step 14 of the one process · Sitting L14*

## Chapter 6 · The other side

"G-d made this one opposite that one." Just as the G-dly soul has ten powers and three garments, so does the other soul, the one from the side that hides Him. It too has a mind and feelings. The Tanya notes that feelings follow the mind: a child wants small things and gets angry over small things, because his mind is small.

When a person thinks, speaks or acts from that side, his thought, speech and hands become that side's clothing. This is everything done "under the sun," of which it is said, "all is vanity." It is every word and thought that is not for G-d, His will and His service. That's what *sitra achra* means: "the other side." Not the side of holiness.

And what is holiness? Only G-d resting on something. And G-d *rests only on a thing that is given over to Him*. The angels are given over to Him in fact. A person is given over to Him in potential, since every person has in him the power to give up his life for G-d. That is why the Presence rests even on one person learning Torah, and on every gathering of ten.

But whatever is not given over to Him, whatever stands as a thing of its own, doesn't get its life from His face. It gets it from His "back," through countless steps down and narrowings, until a tiny bit of light can hide inside it, in a kind of exile, just to keep it in being so it won't fall back into nothing. That's why this world is called a world of shells: hard things happen here, and the wicked often win. (A note in the text adds: even so, the Infinite fills this lowest earth, dressed inside the four worlds.)

The chapter then divides the shells into two levels. The lowest has no good in it at all. From there, it says, come the souls of the nations, unclean animals, forbidden foods, and every forbidden act. It says this plainly, and we pass it on as it is. The next chapter describes the higher, mixed level.

*The Tanya speaks here of the Jewish soul and the nations; this adaptation applies the teaching to every person.*

Even what seems to stand on its own is held in being by a hidden spark of Him. The only real question is which way it faces: toward itself, or given over.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes; best on a steady day, not a heavy one)* - **Arrive.** Think of one conversation today that was really about making yourself look good. Just notice it. - **The line, softly:** "He rests only where I am given over to Him." - **Think it through.** Two directions for every moment: for Him, or for me. Then the hidden part: even the moment that was for me was kept in being by Him, out of sight, so it wouldn't fall into nothing. - **Where the picture breaks.** His "back" sounds like He turned away. The note says He fills it all. The hiding is His doing. - **From inside.** The part of you that wants to be something grows a little quieter. There is room. - **The edge.** Without His hidden life, everything would be "nothing and zero, as before it was created." Stay with that for a moment. - **Into the day.** Before one thing you're about to do today, ask: "Who is this for?"

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 6 (line 146): ואין הקדוש ברוך הוא שורה אלא על דבר שבטל אצלו יתברך · Step 7 of the one process · Sitting L06*

## Chapter 7 · Not tied down

Between holiness and the shells with no good in them, there's a middle level. The Tanya calls it *kelipat nogah*, the "glowing shell." It holds most of ordinary life: the animal soul in a person, kosher animals and plants, and everything we think, say and do in this world that isn't forbidden but isn't for G-d either, only for the body's wants. Even what the body truly needs, if the aim isn't to serve G-d with it, belongs here. It is mostly self, with a little good mixed in.

Because it's in the middle, it can go either way. It can sink with the lower shells, or rise into holiness.

The Tanya's examples are warm. Someone eats good meat and drinks fine wine to open his mind to G-d and His Torah, or to honor Shabbat and the festivals. The life in that food is sorted out and "rises to G-d like an offering." Someone tells a joke to open his mind and gladden his heart for learning, the way the sage Rava did with his students. That rises too.

But one who gorges on meat and wine just to fill his cravings pulls that life down, for a while, into the fully bad. Still, not forever. Because the food was permitted, it can rise again with him when he returns to serving G-d. That's what *heter*, "permitted," means: *untied*. It "is not tied and bound in the hands of the outer forces, so that it could not return and rise to G-d." A trace stays in the body, the chapter says, which needs cleansing after death.

What is forbidden is different. Forbidden foods and forbidden unions come from the fully unclean shells and stay bound there, until the end of days, or until a person returns to G-d with a love so great that his sins become merits. That return comes from the depths of the heart. It is the thirst of someone who was far away, "like a dry and weary land." Because he was so far, his thirst is stronger than the thirst of the righteous.

The chapter also speaks frankly about sexuality. Desire spent without holiness, it says, falls low, and can rise again through sincere return and the Shema said with deep intent at bedtime. Some damage, it says, can't be undone. We give this briefly, as the chapter does.

*The Tanya speaks here of the Jewish soul and its particular root; this adaptation applies it to every person.*

Ordinary life isn't outside the holy. It is waiting. Your supper and your laughter can go where you go.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of tonight's supper, or a funny moment from today. - **The line, softly:** "What I use for Him rises with me." - **Think it through.** Good food eaten to have strength for good things. A joke that opens someone's heart. Then the word *heter*: untied. Even what you took only for yourself is not tied down. It can come back up with you. - **Where the picture breaks.** "Tied" suggests a place outside Him. Nothing is. "Bound" means hidden too deep to lift right now. - **From inside.** A tenderness toward ordinary things. The day stops splitting into holy hours and wasted ones. - **The edge.** The thirst of one who was far away, stronger than the thirst of the righteous. Love that grows from distance. - **Into the day.** Before one meal today, say: "I'm eating this so I'll have strength for good things." (A blessing before food, if you say one.)

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 7 (line 159): כלומר שאינו קשור ואסור בידי החיצונים שלא יוכל לחזור ולעלות לה׳ · Step 13 of the one process · Sitting L13*

## Chapter 8 · What you take in becomes you

This is a sober chapter. It asks you to notice what you let into yourself, because it becomes part of you.

Forbidden food, it says, is called *issur*, "bound," because even if someone ate it by mistake, meaning well, and then used its strength to pray and learn, its life doesn't rise into his prayer the way permitted food would. It stays bound.

The pull toward forbidden things, the chapter says, comes from the lower, unclean shells. The pull toward permitted pleasures is milder; it can still come back to holiness. But until it does, it's the other side. And even afterward a trace remains, "for everything one eats and drinks immediately becomes blood and flesh of his flesh." The chapter speaks of a cleansing the body needs after death, and of harder purifications for the soul after forbidden speech like mockery and gossip, or after wasting time one could have spent learning. We don't dwell on these; they show how seriously the book takes what enters a person.

Then it turns to the mind. Empty talk, it says, dirties the heart's feelings. But the wisdom of the nations, studied for its own sake, goes deeper: it dresses the mind itself. The exception: if you use such knowledge "as an axe to cut with," to earn a living so you can serve G-d, or to serve G-d and His Torah with it. That's why the great sages Maimonides and Nachmanides studied it.

*The Tanya speaks here of the Jewish soul and the nations; this adaptation applies it to every person.*

A person is one thing, not a set of sealed rooms. What goes in goes all the way in. Guarding the doors is part of becoming whole for Him.

**Sit with it** *(4 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of what you read, watched or listened to today. - **The line, softly:** "What I take in becomes me." - **Think it through.** Food turning into blood and flesh, right away. Then words and images doing the same in the mind. What did you feed your mind today, and what is it doing in you now? Then the axe: knowledge used to cut toward something good. - **Where the picture breaks.** Food needs a stomach. A thought needs nothing. It enters the moment you let it stay. - **From inside.** A clean kind of care, not fear. A wish to guard the doors of the self. - **The edge.** Not here. This is a chapter of limits, and it keeps them. - **Into the day.** Put down one thing you read or watch today that only fills time.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 8 (line 172): להיות כי מכל מאכל ומשקה נעשה תיכף דם ובשר מבשרו · Step 13 of the one process · no sitting directly*

## Chapter 9 · Two kings, one small city

Where does each soul live? The animal soul lives in the heart, the Tanya says, in its left chamber, the one full of blood. That's why cravings, pride and anger are felt in the heart. From there they spread through the body and rise to the brain, to think about them and get clever about them.

The G-dly soul lives in the brain, and spreads from there through the body, and also into the heart's right chamber. From there comes love of G-d "like flames of fire," in people who understand and think about what wakes love. So does the heart's joy in His beauty, when the eyes of the mind look at the King's glory.

Then the picture. The body is called "a small city." Two kings are at war over it. Each wants to rule, to have every citizen obey him. So the two souls fight over the body and all its limbs.

The G-dly soul wants to rule alone. It wants the mind full of thinking about G-d's greatness, the heart burning with love for Him, the mouth full of Torah, the hands busy with mitzvot. And it wants more than to win. It wants the inside of the heart to be *paved with love, full and overflowing, until it spills into the left chamber*, and changes the craving itself: turns the wanting for this world's pleasures into love of G-d. That's what "with all your heart" means, the Sages said: with both your inclinations.

Higher still is "love in delights," actually tasting the sweetness of G-d. That love, the chapter says, turns the bad completely good, by taking off its "filthy garments," the pleasures it wears.

And the animal soul wants the opposite. But, the chapter says, it wants it for the person's good, so he will overcome it. (The chapter points to a parable in the Zohar for this, in one line, without telling it.)

*The Tanya speaks here of the Jewish soul; this adaptation applies it to every person.*

The enemy is meant to be turned, not destroyed. The same force that wants can come home as love, and then even the other side was His from the start.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes; best on a steady day, not a heavy one)* - **Arrive.** Think of a craving you fought this week. Let it be there, without shame. - **The line, softly:** "This much wanting can also go to You." - **Think it through.** Two kings, one small city. Then the better hope: love so full it spills over into the other side and changes it. Feel how strong your wanting is. That strength is the raw material. - **Where the picture breaks.** In a war, you kill the enemy. Here, the enemy is meant to become a friend. - **From inside.** Not hatred of yourself but hope for yourself. - **The edge.** "Love in delights" is beyond effort; the book will call it a gift (chapter 14). Here it is pictured, not promised. - **Into the day.** When a strong wanting comes today, say: "This much wanting can also go to You."

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 9 (line 185): שיהיה תוכו רצוף אהבה מלא וגדוש עד שתתפשט גם לחלל השמאלי · Step 12 of the one process · Sitting L12*

## Chapter 10 · Darkness turned to light

What happens when the G-dly soul really wins?

There's one who fights so hard that he drives the bad out of the left side of his heart. But the bad hasn't turned good. A tiny speck of it is still there, so small it's bent over and hidden by the good. He may think it's gone. It isn't. If it were really gone, it would have turned to good. This person is called "a righteous one who is not complete." His hatred of the bad isn't total, so some small liking for it remains, and so his love for G-d isn't total either. There are countless levels of this, the chapter says, and most of the righteous in every generation are here.

Then there's the complete tzaddik. His bad has turned to good. How? By taking off its "filthy garments" completely: by being truly disgusted with the pleasures of this world taken only for the body. The more he loves G-d, with that "love in delights," the more he hates what is opposite to Him. "I hate them with a complete hatred."

Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai said: "I have seen the people of ascent, and they are few." They're called that because they lift the bad up into holiness. The Zohar tells of a voice calling out: "Who among you turns darkness into light and makes the bitter taste sweet?"

They're "people of ascent" for a second reason too. When they do good, it isn't only to quench their own thirst for G-d. It's for Him. "Who is a chasid? One who is kind to his Maker": who works to join G-d and His Presence here below. Like a son who loves his father and mother more than himself, and would give his life to rescue them.

(A bracketed note in the chapter shows, in Kabbalistic terms, how the two kinds of service lift and draw down together. We leave it at that.)

The deepest service isn't about getting close to Him for my sake. It's joining Him with His world, here, for His sake. And the dark, once turned, becomes the light.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes; best on a steady day, not a heavy one)* - **Arrive.** Think of why you pray or do good. Let the honest answer come. - **The line, softly:** "Darkness can turn to light, and bitter to sweet." - **Think it through.** A speck of bad, bent down and hidden. Then the complete turn: the dirty clothes taken off, and what was underneath was good. Then the son who runs to rescue his parents, not thinking of himself. - **Where the picture breaks.** A parent in danger needs rescue. G-d lacks nothing. The "need above" is His own wish, that it be done here. - **From inside.** A widening. The work can be for Him, not only for my closeness to Him. - **The edge.** Joining Him with His Presence below lies past feeling, in His will. Let it be bigger than you. - **Into the day.** Do one mitzvah or one kindness today only because He wants it.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 10 (line 194): די חשוכא מהפכן לנהורא וטעמין מרירו למיתקא · Step 12 of the one process · Sitting L12*

## Chapter 11 · Still there, above him

Now the other end. Opposite the righteous who is not complete is the "wicked one who has it good": a person in whom the good bows to the bad.

There are countless levels of this too. For some, the bad wins only a little, and only once in a while. It takes over part of the small city, not all of it. Maybe only in deeds, doing lighter wrongs. Maybe only in speech: a bit of gossip, some mockery. Maybe only in thought, dwelling on sin, which the Sages say is harder than the sin itself. Or wasting a moment when one could learn. At that moment he is called wicked. Afterward the good in him rises, he's sorry, he asks G-d to forgive him, and G-d forgives him if he returns properly.

For others, the bad wins more: in thought, speech and deed, in heavier wrongs, more often. But in between, he regrets. Thoughts of return come to him from the good in his soul, though not strongly enough to leave his sins behind. Of him the Sages said: "The wicked are full of regrets." That is most of the wicked, the chapter says. The good is still in them.

And the one who never regrets, to whom no thought of return ever comes? The bad has grown so strong that the good *has left his inside and stands as a surrounding presence above him*.

Even there it isn't gone. And the chapter closes with a line from the Talmud: "In every gathering of ten, the Presence rests."

No one is outside. Even when the good has left a person's inside, it hovers over him, waiting. What doesn't fit inside a person still holds him from beyond.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of someone you've given up on. Or of your own worst hour. - **The line, softly:** "Even there, it stands above him." - **Think it through.** The small city partly taken. Then the regrets that keep coming. Then the one with no regrets at all, and above him, like a roof he can't see, his soul still waiting. - **Where the picture breaks.** "Above" is not a place. It means what hasn't yet come inside. - **From inside.** Mercy. Less contempt, for others and for your own worst self. - **The edge.** What surrounds and doesn't enter. You can't grasp it, but you can know it's there. - **Into the day.** Think of one person you've written off, and wish him well in silence.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 11 (line 203): עד שנסתלק מקרבו ועומד בבחינת מקיף עליו מלמעלה · Step 4 of the one process · Sitting L04*

## Chapter 12 · The mind rules the heart

Now the Tanya defines the beinoni, the one in the middle. In him, the bad never grows strong enough to take over the small city. It never gets to use his mind, his mouth, or his hands. Only the G-dly soul's garments get dressed in his body: thoughts, words and deeds of Torah and mitzvot. He never sinned, and he never will. He's never called wicked, not even for a moment.

But the G-dly soul doesn't rule alone *inside*. Only at special times, like the Shema and prayer. Then he binds his mind to G-d's greatness and wakes up love like flames in his heart, and the bad on the left side bows and goes quiet.

After prayer, when that great mind leaves, the bad wakes up again. It craves this world's pleasures.

So why doesn't it win? Because *the mind rules over the heart*. This is how people are made. Anyone can, with the will in his mind, hold back from what his heart wants, not act on it, not say it, not even think about it, and turn his mind to something else entirely, especially to holiness.

The Tanya compares it to light. A little light pushes away a lot of darkness, all by itself. The wisdom of the G-dly soul pushes away the foolishness of the other side the same way.

Still, the beinoni isn't called righteous. The light wins only in the garments, the thoughts, words and deeds. The animal soul itself hasn't moved from its place. After prayer, his heart may crave anything, as if he had never prayed. What keeps him steady is a love that's hidden, not felt.

What does he do? A bad thought will come up by itself, from the heart to the mind. The moment he notices it's a bad thought, he pushes it away "with both hands." He doesn't welcome it, doesn't play with it. Dwelling on it on purpose would make him wicked for that moment, and the beinoni is never wicked.

The same with people. When resentment or jealousy or anger rises toward someone, he doesn't let it into his mind at all. The mind rules the heart to do the opposite: to be kind to that person, to bear with him to the very end, not to pay him back. Even to repay harm with good, the way Joseph did with his brothers.

You are more than your moods. The One gave the mind rule over the heart, and that rule is how His light gets into your hands.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of an irritation or a grudge from today. Let it be there. Don't feed it. - **The line, softly:** "The mind rules the heart." - **Think it through.** A small light in a dark room: the dark doesn't fight back, it just goes. Then the thought that rose on its own, and two hands gently pushing it away. Then Joseph, looking at the brothers who sold him, and feeding them. - **Where the picture breaks.** Light ends darkness. In the beinoni, the dark only hides. That's alright. The hiding is enough to live well. - **From inside.** Dignity. You don't have to feel differently to act differently. - **The edge.** Not here. This is the ground floor of freedom. - **Into the day.** Answer one irritation today with a kind sentence.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 12 (line 209): כי המוח שליט על הלב · Step 12 of the one process · no sitting directly*

## Chapter 13 · A lip of truth

Now the Tanya can explain the Sages' words: "In the beinoni, both judge him." Both inclinations are judges. It doesn't say both *rule*. If the bad ruled even for a moment, he'd be wicked for that moment.

So the bad inclination is like a judge who gives his opinion. But there's another judge who disagrees: the G-dly soul in the mind. And a deciding voice: G-d Himself, who helps the good. "If the Holy One didn't help him, he couldn't win." The help is a light G-d shines on the G-dly soul, like light over darkness.

This is why a person should "be in your own eyes like someone wicked." Not wicked. *Like* someone wicked. It means: don't believe the world when it says the bad in you has faded. Assume it's still there in full strength, maybe stronger with time from all the eating and drinking and ordinary life. Even someone who learns day and night can't take that as proof the bad is gone. His learning may only mean his mind rules.

The bad in the beinoni is like a sleeping person. During the Shema and prayer, when the heart burns with love, it sleeps. Afterward it can wake up. That's why the great sage Rabbah called himself a beinoni, even though he never stopped learning and his love was always burning. To himself, he was like a beinoni who prays all day.

Then a kind turn. Compared to the righteous, whose love is steady, the beinoni's love in prayer isn't called "true service," since it passes. "A lip of truth stands forever." But the Tanya says: for the beinoni, at his own level, it is complete service, true to its truth. *I call their love in their prayer, too, "a lip of truth that stands forever,"* because their G-dly soul has the power to wake it again, every single day, with the right preparation.

Why? Because truth is like Jacob, the "middle bar" that ran through the whole Tabernacle from end to end. Truth runs through every level, from the highest to the lowest, through the center point of each. Truth is "an inheritance without borders." Every level is like nothing next to the one above it, and still, each has its own truth.

The One is truth, and His truth runs through every level, yours included. Your love that fades is not fake. It is true at your level, and it can wake again.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Remember a prayer or a moment that moved you, and how it faded by lunch. - **The line, softly:** "It was a lip of truth." - **Think it through.** A sleeper who might wake: honest, not frightened. Then the middle bar, running through every board of the Tabernacle. Your level is one of those boards. The bar runs through you too. - **Where the picture breaks.** A sleeper wakes by chance. Your love wakes because you prepare and pray again. It's not left to chance. - **From inside.** Relief from two lies at once: that you're finished, and that you're fake. - **The edge.** "Truth without borders": every level is nothing next to the one above. The ladder has no top you can see. - **Into the day.** Tomorrow, pray one line with the attention of your best day, and trust it. (Anyone: say one sentence to G-d the same way.)

## Chapter 14 · I don't want to be cut off

Here is the good news of the whole first part: anyone can be a beinoni, at any time, at any hour.

Why? Because the beinoni doesn't have to be disgusted by the bad. That depends on the heart, and not every moment is the same. What he does is "turn from bad and do good" in fact: in deed, speech and thought. And there, every person has the choice and the power to act, speak and think even against what his heart wants.

Even while the heart is craving something, he can take his mind off it completely, by saying to his heart: *I don't want to be wicked, not even for a moment. I don't want to be separated and cut off from the One G-d in any way.* "Your sins separate you." I want my soul to cling to Him, dressed in His three garments, thought, speech and deed, in G-d, His Torah and mitzvot. Out of the love that's hidden in my heart.

The Tanya brings a proof. Even the simplest, most careless person can give his life for G-d rather than deny Him. "I'm surely no less than him." When he sins, it's only because a "spirit of foolishness" got into him. He imagines that this one sin doesn't separate him from G-d, and he forgets his hidden love. "But I don't want to be a fool like him and deny the truth."

Truly loathing the bad is different. That depends on the heart, and it comes only from a great love, tasting G-d's sweetness. Not everyone gets that. It's like a reward, a gift. That's why Job said, "You created the righteous": being a tzaddik is partly given. So the soul's oath says twice: "Be righteous" and also "Don't be wicked." Not everyone can be righteous. But everyone can not be wicked, not even for an hour.

Still, set times to work on the heart too. The Sages gave blunt pictures of how the body's pleasures end, all fine food and all this world's delights ending in decay (one of their sayings is about a woman's body, and the Tanya cites it). And the opposite: think about G-d's greatness and try to rejoice in Him, even if you know it's only in your imagination. Do your part, and G-d will do what is good in His eyes.

And habit rules everything. It becomes second nature. Get used to it and the bad starts to feel a little repulsive in truth; get used to rejoicing in G-d and He answers from above. Maybe a spirit from on high will come to you, and you'll serve G-d with real joy.

*The Tanya speaks here of the Jewish soul and its particular root; this adaptation applies it to every person.*

The One is one, and to be cut off from Him is the only thing you truly don't want. Saying so, at the moment of pull, is choosing to belong.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of a real pull from this week, one you wanted to follow. - **The line, softly:** "I don't want to be cut off from the One. Not for a moment." - **Think it through.** The heart wants what it wants. You can still say no, in the mind, the mouth, the hand. Then the simplest person who would give his life rather than deny G-d. You're surely not less than him. - **Where the picture breaks.** "Cut off" suggests you could fall outside Him. You can't fall outside Him. But you can turn away, and that is what you're refusing. - **From inside.** A quiet strength that doesn't depend on mood. The feeling of choosing to belong. - **The edge.** Beyond your work is "a spirit from on high." It is given, not made. Leave room for it. - **Into the day.** Say the sentence once, out loud, at a moment of pull.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 14 (line 232): כי אינני רוצה להיות מובדל ונפרד חס ושלום מה׳ אחד בשום אופן · Step 12 of the one process · Sitting L01*

## Chapter 15 · The hundred-and-first time

The prophet Malachi says: "You will see the difference between the righteous and the wicked, between one who serves G-d and one who has not served Him." Why two pairs?

"Serves," the Tanya says, is present tense. It means *in the middle of the work*. The work is the war with the bad inclination, keeping it from getting into the body. It is real, hard, constant labor. That's the beinoni.

The tzaddik is called a "servant of G-d," a title, like "wise man" or "king." He's already finished the war. His heart is empty of the bad.

Among beinonim, too, there are two kinds: one who serves, and one "who has not served Him." Even the second one isn't wicked. He's never done even a small sin, and he learns constantly. But he isn't fighting. Maybe he loves learning by temperament. Maybe he just doesn't feel much pull toward pleasures. He doesn't need to think about G-d's greatness to keep going. The hidden love all Jews inherit is enough for him. And since that love is inherited, not his own doing, he isn't called "serving." The same goes for someone who trained himself to learn until the habit became second nature.

The Talmud says: one who reviews his lesson a hundred times hasn't served; one who reviews it a hundred and one times has served. In those days, a hundred times was the habit. Like donkey drivers who charged one coin for ten miles, and two coins for eleven, because the eleventh was past their habit. So the hundred-and-first time outweighs all the others. To change a habit, you have to wake love for G-d by thinking about His greatness, or at least wake the hidden love in your heart. That's the fight, and that's the service.

*The Tanya speaks here of the Jewish people's inherited love; this adaptation applies it to every person.*

The One is served in the present tense. The struggle is not a sign you've failed. It is where you meet Him.

**Sit with it** *(4 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Think of something you do every day out of habit, good or neutral. - **The line, softly:** "Serving is present tense. I'm in the middle of the work." - **Think it through.** A hundred reviews, then one more. Donkey drivers charging double for the eleventh mile. Where is your eleventh mile? One more page, one more minute, one kindness past your usual. - **Where the picture breaks.** The donkey driver gets paid more. You're not paid; you're changed. The extra step is where love has to wake. - **From inside.** The struggle stops looking like failure and starts looking like the job. - **The edge.** Not here. This chapter is about the humble middle. - **Into the day.** Do one thing today one step past your habit.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 15 (line 241): ש״עובד״ הוא לשון הוה – שהוא באמצע העבודה · Step 6 of the one process · no sitting directly*

## Chapter 16 · Wings to fly upward

A great rule for the beinoni: the main thing is to rule over the nature on the heart's left side, with the light of G-d shining on the G-dly soul in the mind. Think about the greatness of the Infinite. From that, let awe come, to keep you from wrong, and love, to cling to Him by doing good.

But what if your mind can't make your heart catch fire? What if you think it through and the heart doesn't burn, and the love stays hidden, in your mind and in the secret places of your heart? (A short note says this depends on the soul's root, whether its powers are "born" into the open or still "hidden in the womb.")

Here is what to do. Let the heart understand, with the mind, the greatness of G-d: that everything before Him is truly as nothing. So *it is fitting that every living soul should long for Him, to cling to Him and be included in His light*. And your own soul, too, fittingly longs to leave its sheath, the body, and cling to Him. It lives in the body against its will, "like a living widow."

But no thought can hold Him, except as He is dressed in Torah and mitzvot, like embracing the King. So it's fitting to embrace Him with all your heart, soul and might: that is, to do the mitzvot, in deed, speech and thought.

Now when you think this through deeply, and your mouth and heart agree, and your mouth learns Torah and your hands do mitzvot because of what you understood, that understanding dresses itself in your deeds. It gives them life and "wings to fly upward," as if you had done them with burning love. Because without that understanding, you wouldn't have done them at all. You'd have tended only to your body's needs.

That's what the Sages meant: "A good thought, G-d joins to the deed." The felt love of the heart can dress itself in deeds on its own, because the heart is physical too, like the hands. But love that stays in the mind is too high to enter the deed by itself. So G-d joins it. And even without this, the chapter adds, deeds done from the hidden love in every Jewish heart still rise, a little less high.

*The Tanya speaks here of the Jewish soul; this adaptation applies it to every person.*

The soul longs to melt into the One, and He turns that longing into an embrace made of deeds. You don't need to feel on fire. What you understood gives your hands wings.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes; best on a steady day, not a heavy one)* - **Arrive.** Maybe you feel dry today. Let that be there. It's not a problem. - **The line, softly:** "It is fitting that my soul long for Him. So I'll embrace Him with what I do." - **Think it through.** Before Him, all is truly as nothing. So every soul should long to melt into His light, yours too, held in the body like a widow. But He can only be embraced where He's dressed. So: the deed. - **Where the picture breaks.** A widow is alone. Your soul is never alone. The longing itself is His, and He meets it in the deed. - **From inside.** Permission not to feel much, and a sense that what you understood counts. - **The edge.** The soul's wish to dissolve into Him is the edge. The chapter doesn't stop there. It turns it into an embrace. - **Into the day.** Do one mitzvah or kindness today because you understood it's fitting, feeling or no feeling.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 16 (line 257): גדפין לפרחא לעילא · Steps 7 and 14 of the one process · Sitting L06*

## Chapter 17 · Your mind is yours

Now the Tanya can explain the verse on the title page: "The thing is very near to you, in your mouth and in your heart, to do it."

At first glance, "in your heart" goes against experience. It is not "very near" to turn your heart from the world's pleasures to real love of G-d. The Talmud asks, "Is awe a small thing?" Love even more so. And "only the righteous have their hearts in their power."

But "to do it" is the key. The verse means the love that leads to action: the heart's quiet will, in the hidden places of the heart, even if it doesn't burn in the open. That *is* very near, and easy for anyone with a brain in his head. *For his mind is in his power, and he can think about whatever he wants.* When he thinks about the greatness of the Infinite, love for G-d will come, in his mind at least: a love that wants to cling to Him by doing His mitzvot.

"That is the whole person." "Today, to do them": today is the world of doing. The mind rules the heart by nature, and rules the mouth and all the limbs.

Except, the chapter says, for someone who is truly wicked, whose heart has him in its power. For him, the way starts with return. He must first break the iron wall between himself and his Father in heaven. How? With a broken heart and bitterness over what he did. "A broken spirit, a broken heart, G-d does not despise." When the heart breaks, the wall of the other side breaks too.

And something happens above. When a person does wrong, he drags down the spark of G-d that gives him life, into exile inside his animal soul. That's the secret of the Presence in exile. When his heart breaks, the Presence rises from its fall, and stands.

The One is always near, because your own mind is always yours to turn to Him. And even the one who is far can break the wall, and lift His Presence with him.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes)* - **Arrive.** Notice where your mind went on its own in the last hour. Don't judge it. - **The line, softly:** "My mind is mine. I can think of Him whenever I want." - **Think it through.** The heart may not obey. The mind does. Pick one true thing about G-d: He fills this place, He gives me life right now. Think it. Let it lead to one small deed. That's the love that leads to doing. Then, if you carry something heavy: the iron wall, and a broken heart that breaks it. - **Where the picture breaks.** "Iron wall" sounds as if He were on the other side. He's with you in the exile. When you rise, He rises. - **From inside.** A sense of owning your inner life. - **The edge.** Not here. This chapter is practical. - **Into the day.** Choose one thought about G-d to think on your next walk.

*Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim ch. 17 (line 265): כי מוחו ברשותו ויכול להתבונן בו ככל אשר יחפוץ · Step 6 of the one process · Sitting L07*

## Chapter 18 · Always with You

The Tanya now explains the word "very" in "very near."

Even someone whose understanding is short, who can't grasp G-d's greatness well enough to feel love and awe even in his mind, still has it very near. He can keep all the mitzvot, with his mouth and his heart, from the depth of his heart, truly, with awe and love. How? Through the hidden love in every heart, an inheritance from our fathers.

Where does it come from? The Patriarchs, Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, were a "chariot" for G-d, wholly given over to Him. So they were able to pass souls down to their children forever. Even the simplest, even those who have sinned, received at least the lowest level of holiness. (The chapter traces the path through the ten sefirot of the four worlds; we give it in one line.) Yet even that lowest level has all ten powers, including *chochmah*, wisdom. And inside that wisdom, through level after level, the light of the Infinite truly shines.

So the Infinite is dressed in the wisdom of every soul, whoever it is, and gives life to the whole soul from head to foot.

Why wisdom? Because *chochmah* is above understanding. It's the source of it. The Tanya reads the word again: *koach mah*, "the power of *what*": what isn't grasped or understood yet. That's why the Infinite, whom no thought can grasp, can dress itself there.

And that's why everyone believes in G-d, even the unlearned. Faith is above knowing. Before G-d, who is beyond all mind, everyone is simple. "I was a fool and did not know; I was like a beast with You. And I am always with You." Meaning: *in this, that I am "a fool" and "a beast," "I am always with You."*

That's why, the chapter says, even the most careless, even sinners, have usually given up their lives rather than deny the One, and suffered terrible things. Not because they reasoned it out. They may know little and think about it even less. It's just as if denying Him were impossible, with no reason at all. Because the One lights up and gives life to the whole soul through its wisdom, which is above reason.

*The Tanya speaks here of the Jewish soul and its inheritance from the Patriarchs; this adaptation applies it to every person.*

Under all your thinking, the Infinite is already resting in you, in the place before understanding. Your simplest "I am with You" is where you and He meet.

**Sit with it** *(5 minutes; if it starts to feel unreal or too big, stop and do something plain)* - **Arrive.** Let go of needing to understand this. You're allowed not to. - **The line, softly:** "I am simple before You, and I am always with You." - **Think it through.** A point before any thought, the "what?" before the answer. The Infinite rests there. Then the simplest people in history, who would not deny Him, without any argument. Find something in yourself that knows He is real, though you never worked it out. - **Where the picture breaks.** "Fool" and "beast" sound like insults. Here they are the closest words: the one who doesn't need to understand in order to stay. - **From inside.** A simplicity that doesn't need to win an argument. Being with Him before thinking anything. - **The edge.** This is the edge itself: faith above reason, where the Infinite rests in the soul. Don't look at it. Be it. - **Into the day.** Say "Shema Yisrael" once today without thinking anything, only meaning it. (Anyone: "You are One," said the same way.)
