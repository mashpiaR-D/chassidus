## 0. The short answer

Science does not have one theory of meditation. It has a handful of well-measured mechanisms (training attention; noticing where the mind has gone; quieting the brain's self-talk network; slow breathing that calms the body; pictures that move feelings more than words do; putting feelings into words; music that triggers the brain's reward system) and one ambitious framework that tries to tie them together: the brain as a **prediction machine**, which meditation retrains by loosening its grip on its habitual models of the world and the self.

Laid over the Jewish techniques, the picture that emerges (**ours**) is a three-phase process:

1. **Loosen.** Letter-permutation, chanting, rhythmic breath, quieting and looking-at-thoughts all reduce the pull of the habitual, self-centred stream of thought and of ordinary word-meanings.
2. **Install.** Hisbonenus, the picture before the Throne, speaking to God as "You", and the niggun build and charge a new high-level model ("I am held; there is nothing but Him") by elaboration, imagery, relationship and emotion.
3. **Consolidate.** Repetition over weeks, fixed times, cues through the day and the deed at the end turn the new model from a state into a trait.

Abulafia's road leans hard on phase 1; Chabad hisbonenus leans on phase 2; the Piaseczner does a short phase 1 and then phase 2; all three insist on phase 3. The strongest links in this chain are the attention, breathing and imagery findings. The weakest is the central claim of the predictive account: that loosening makes the mind more open to installing a new belief. That is theory, drawn mostly from psychedelic research, and it has never been tested on prayer or contemplation.

## 1.1 A few words, defined

- **Attention regulation**: choosing what the mind rests on and keeping it there. Measured by reaction-time tasks and by how often the mind drifts.
- **Mind-wandering**: thinking about something other than what one is doing. It happens very often and usually goes with lower mood (Killingsworth & Gilbert, 2010; Smallwood & Schooler, 2015) *[both reused]*.
- **Meta-awareness**: noticing what the mind is doing right now ("I am thinking about tomorrow"), as opposed to simply being inside the thought (Schooler et al., 2011) *[reused]*.
- **Default mode network (DMN)**: a set of brain regions, chiefly along the midline (medial prefrontal cortex at the front, posterior cingulate cortex and precuneus toward the back), that is more active when a person is not doing an outward task: remembering, imagining the future, thinking about oneself and other people. It was named when researchers noticed that these areas consistently *quiet down* when people start a goal-directed task (Raichle et al., 2001; Buckner, Andrews-Hanna & Schacter, 2008).
- **Self-referential processing**: thought that is about me: my plans, my standing, my story. The DMN is heavily involved in it.
- **Predictive processing**: a theory that the brain is chiefly a prediction machine. It constantly guesses what will come next, at many levels at once (from "the next sound" up to "who I am and how the world goes"), compares the guess with what arrives, and learns from the mismatch (**prediction error**) (Clark, 2013). A **prior** is an existing expectation or belief. **Precision** is how much weight the brain gives to a signal or a belief: a high-precision belief is held tightly and resists correction.
- **Absorption**: a trait, measured by the Tellegen Absorption Scale, for being wholly caught up in inner experience, imagery, music or nature (Tellegen & Atkinson, 1974) *[reused]*.
- **Hypo-egoic**: states in which the self is less in the foreground: less self-watching, less self-concern (Leary, Adams & Tate, 2006; Brown & Leary, 2016) *[both reused]*.
- **Nondual awareness**: in the contemplative literature, awareness that is not split into a subject over here and objects over there (Josipovic, 2014, 2021).
- **Autonomic nervous system**: the body's automatic control system. Its **sympathetic** branch gears up (heart rate, alertness); its **parasympathetic** branch calms down. **Heart rate variability (HRV)** is the beat-to-beat variation in heart rate; higher HRV generally goes with calmer, more flexible regulation.

## 1.2 Attention regulation and meta-awareness *(well established at the level of behaviour)*

The oldest and best-supported account is that meditation trains attention. **Lutz, Slagter, Dunne and Davidson (2008)** *[reused]* split practices into *focused attention* (hold one object, notice drift, come back) and *open monitoring* (hold nothing, watch whatever arises). A fine-grained scanning study caught the focused-attention cycle in the act: drifting, noticing the drift, shifting back, holding, each phase lining up with a different brain network (**Hasenkamp et al., 2012**, *imaging study, small*) *[reused]*. Large reviews propose that mindfulness works through a few interacting parts: attention control, body awareness, emotion regulation (by reappraisal and by exposure) and a changed perspective on the self (**Hölzel et al., 2011**, *theory*; **Tang, Hölzel & Posner, 2015**, *review*). The last review is honest that the neural mechanisms "remain unclear" and that more rigorous studies are needed.

Two benchmarks keep this in proportion. Across 163 studies of non-clinical meditators, effects were medium on average (r ≈ .28), strongest for emotion and relationships, weaker for thinking tasks, and almost three-quarters of the studies first found had to be excluded for method problems (**Sedlmeier et al., 2012**, *meta-analysis*). For mindfulness programs against active comparisons, benefits for anxiety, depression and pain are real but small-to-moderate (Goyal et al., 2014, *meta-analysis*) *[reused; see part-a §1.3]*.

**Status:** that practice changes attention and meta-awareness: **well established**. Exactly how, in the brain: **moderate**, with many small studies.

## 1.3 The default mode network and the self *(established that it quiets; its meaning is debated)*

A striking, repeated finding is that meditation quiets the DMN. Experienced meditators showed relatively less activity in its two main hubs (medial prefrontal and posterior cingulate cortex) across three different kinds of meditation, and stronger coupling between the posterior cingulate and control regions (**Brewer et al., 2011**, *imaging study*) *[reused]*. A follow-up showed this quieting goes *beyond* what an ordinary effortful task produces (**Garrison et al., 2015**, *imaging study*). Because the DMN carries so much self-related thought, many researchers read this as a brain signature of less mind-wandering and less self-referential thinking (Brewer et al., 2011).

Three cautions. First, a meta-analysis of 78 imaging studies found that different styles of meditation produce **reliably different** brain patterns, and that shared activation across styles is "the exception rather than the rule" (**Fox et al., 2016**, *meta-analysis of imaging studies*). "Meditation quiets the DMN" is a family resemblance, not a law. Second, the DMN is also active in rich, meaningful inner thought, including imagining and remembering (Buckner et al., 2008), which is exactly what hisbonenus and the Piaseczner's picturing use; so less DMN is not automatically the goal of every Jewish technique (§2.4). Third, scanner studies are small, and meditators in them are self-selected.

**Status:** DMN quieting during focused and mantra-style practice: **moderate, replicated**. That this is *the* mechanism of benefit: **speculative**.

## 1.4 The families *(a map, not a finding)*

**Dahl, Lutz and Davidson (2015)** *[reused]* sort practices into **attentional** (train attention and meta-awareness), **constructive** (build a way of seeing and feeling, such as compassion or values, by perspective-taking and reappraisal) and **deconstructive** (loosen the habit of taking thoughts and the self as solid, by inquiry). Part-a §1.2 placed hisbonenus as constructive with an analytical engine held in place by attention. The same group later proposed four trainable dimensions of well-being (awareness, connection, insight, purpose), each with its own mechanisms and evidence of plasticity (**Dahl, Wilson-Mendenhall & Davidson, 2020**, *theory/review*). The most useful point for us: **constructive practices are the least studied**, and the Jewish tradition is mostly constructive.

A second distinction that matters here: **not all meditation is relaxation.** Theravada focused and open styles produced signs of parasympathetic calming, but two Tibetan Vajrayana styles, one of them deity visualization, produced *sympathetic arousal* and an immediate jump on demanding visual tasks (**Amihai & Kozhevnikov, 2014**, *experiment with practitioners*). Abulafia's "fear and trembling" and the Piaseczner's "effort that drags the soul out of hiding" belong on the arousal side of this line, not the relaxation side (**ours**).

## 1.5 Predictive-processing accounts *(theory; promising, speculative)*

The newest models describe meditation in the language of prediction:

- **Focused attention as down-weighting habit.** Paying deliberate attention to one thing settles the mind by *down-weighting* (giving less precision to) habitual automatic reactions and the pull of distracting thoughts (**Lutz, Mattout & Pagnoni, 2019**, *theory*).
- **Deconstructive meditation as shallower prediction.** Meditation "gradually reduces counterfactual temporally deep cognition": less modelling of what might happen, what happened, what could be otherwise, including the modelled self. Focused attention, open monitoring and nondual practice sit on one continuum, each relinquishing more deeply engrained habits of prediction (**Laukkonen & Slagter, 2021**, *theory*). The authors call for experiments to test it.
- **Relaxed beliefs.** From psychedelic research, the REBUS model ("relaxed beliefs under psychedelics") proposes that psychedelics lower the precision of *high-level* priors, the deepest beliefs, so that signals from below can revise them; the authors extend this to heavily weighted religious and philosophical views (**Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019**, *theory*). A review sets meditation and psychedelics side by side as two routes to altered self-consciousness (**Millière et al., 2018**, *review*).
- **Religion as prediction.** A framework for religious experience in these terms: temporal-lobe mechanisms in visions and ecstasy, multisensory areas and the DMN in self-transcendence, the "theory of mind" network (the brain's system for modelling other minds) in prayer, and frontal top-down mechanisms in holding supernatural beliefs (**van Elk & Aleman, 2017**, *theory/review*).
- **When losing the self is bliss and when it is illness.** The same active-inference framework (a form of predictive processing in which action also serves to fulfil predictions) is used to explain why "selfless" states are sought after by contemplatives and devastating in depersonalization: the difference may lie in whether the person still infers that they are *in control* (**Deane, Miller & Wilkinson, 2020**, *theory*). This is the most useful paper here for safety (§5).

**Status:** attractive, unifying, and **largely untested**. It explains old findings well; it has made few new predictions that have been checked. We use it below as a language for a hypothesis, not as a result.

## 1.6 Absorption and "inner-sense cultivation" *(correlational plus one small RCT)*

T. M. Luhrmann's work is the best-developed science of *theistic* practice. People high in absorption report more vivid prayer imagery and more experiences of God's presence, and practice also matters (Luhrmann, Nusbaum & Thisted, 2010) *[reused]*. Prayer is a learned attentional skill, "inner-sense cultivation" (Luhrmann & Morgain, 2012) *[reused]*. A month of imaginative prayer, against Bible-study lectures, raised imagery vividness and unusual sensory experiences (Luhrmann, Nusbaum & Thisted, 2013, *small RCT*) *[reused]*. A review argues that absorption is a general "talent" for spiritual experience, linked to hearing God in prayer, mystical experience with psychedelics *or placebo*, and presence in nature and music; absorption "seems to enable that which must be imagined to feel more real" (**Lifshitz, van Elk & Luhrmann, 2019**, *review*). Luhrmann's book extends this across traditions, including newly Orthodox Jews: people *work* to make God real, through stories, absorption, inner-sense training and prayer (**Luhrmann, 2020**, *ethnography*).

**Why it matters here.** Absorption is the likely *individual-difference* variable in every Jewish technique: the same letters or picture will do very different things in a high- and a low-absorption person. It is also a risk marker (§5).

## 1.7 Self-transcendent experience, nondual awareness, hypo-egoic states

- **Self-transcendent experiences** (mystical experience, awe, flow, peak experiences) share two parts: a lessening of self-salience and a feeling of connection (**Yaden et al., 2017**, *review*) *[reused]*. Their neural basis is proposed, not settled.
- **Nondual awareness.** One study had practitioners do focused attention or nondual meditation in the scanner. The usual see-saw between the brain's outward-task networks and its inward, self-related network (the DMN) grew *stronger* in focused attention and *weaker* in nondual meditation (**Josipovic et al., 2012**, *imaging study, small*). Josipovic describes nondual awareness as background awareness that precedes concepts and relaxes dualities such as self and other (**Josipovic, 2014**; **2021**, *theory*). A questionnaire now measures nondual states (Hanley et al., 2018) *[reused]*.
- **Hypo-egoic** states reduce self-focus without effortful self-control (Leary et al., 2006; Brown & Leary, 2016) *[reused]*.

Part-a (step 7) and part-b (step 9) already drew the line: bitul and efes are not the same claim as ego dissolution or nondual awareness. What science can offer is narrower: these states have measurable correlates (reduced self-salience, altered network coupling), and different techniques seem to reach different ones.

## 2.1 Letter permutation and chanting the Names (Abulafia; Heikhalot-style repetition)

**What is done** (from `ABULAFIA.md` §3, attested): alone, at night, the student writes and rapidly permutes letters "until the heart grows warm", then sings aleph with each letter of the Name, **one letter to one drawn-out breath**, with fixed pauses (one breath, two, five, twenty-five) and no stray word or thought between; the head traces each vowel (up for cholam, down for chirik, forward for shuruk, sweeps for tzere and kamatz); then the voice and pen fall away and the work continues in thought. What arises: warmth, new understanding, fear and trembling, weakness, sometimes forms and voices. Abulafia's own theory: letters are the raw matter of thought; permuted strings have no meaning, so "a letter can hold only one thought at a time" and each new order pushes out the old picture, "untying the knots" (§4.2).

**Likely mechanisms, in order of evidence:**

1. **Semantic satiation: meaning comes loose from the word.** Saying a word over and over makes its meaning feel thinner. The classic study found that rapid verbal repetition reduced the rated intensity of a word's meaning (**Lambert & Jakobovits, 1960**, *experiment*). Brain recordings show the effect is not just tired ears: repeated primes reduced the brain's meaning-response to related words (the N400, an electrical signal of meaning-processing) even when the primes changed in typeface or pitch, which suggests "semantic memory can be directly satiated" (**Kounios, Kotz & Holcomb, 2000**, *EEG experiments*). A later set of experiments supports the specific account that repetition weakens the *link* between a word-form and its meaning (**Tian & Huber, 2010**, *experiments*). Abulafia goes further than satiation: his strings never had a meaning to begin with, and the order keeps changing. **Ours:** his technique is a way of keeping the language system busy at the level of sound and shape while denying it the meanings that normally pull in memories, pictures and self-talk. This fits his own claim that meaning is what "stirs" the soul to a picture (ABULAFIA §4.2) remarkably well.
2. **Mantra-type quieting of the self-network.** Silently repeating a single word, in 23 people who had never meditated, produced a *widespread* drop in brain activity centred on the DMN, without the usual matching rise elsewhere, and a behavioural drop in self-generated thought (**Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2015**, *imaging study*). The authors suggest this may explain the calming effect of mantra practices. In a small pilot, mantra meditation suppressed the DMN more than a slow finger-tapping task, and more with training (**Simon et al., 2017**, *pilot*). The imaging meta-analysis found mantra recitation to be one of four styles with a reliably distinct brain pattern (Fox et al., 2016). *Moderate.*
3. **Slow, paced breath.** "One letter, one breath, drawn out as long as you can" is breath control by another name. A systematic review of slow breathing (under 10 breaths a minute) found raised HRV and respiratory sinus arrhythmia (the heart speeding and slowing with each breath), more alpha and less theta in EEG, and reports of comfort, relaxation, alertness and less anxiety (**Zaccaro et al., 2018**, *systematic review, 15 small studies*). Reciting the rosary or a yoga mantra slowed breathing to about six a minute and produced "striking, powerful and synchronous" increases in the body's own cardiovascular rhythms, with higher baroreflex sensitivity (the reflex that steadies blood pressure) (**Bernardi et al., 2001**, *experiment, 23 adults*). Around six breaths a minute is roughly the "resonance" rate used in HRV biofeedback (**Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014**, *review*). **Ours:** a single long chanted breath per syllable, with rests, very likely puts the chanter in this slow range, though nobody has measured Abulafian chanting. *Moderate for the mechanism; untested for the practice.*
4. **Rhythmic head movement.** We found no study of vowel-tracing head movements. What can be said: the movements are small, slow and tied to the breath, which makes them a further rhythmic anchor for attention; they add a body channel to the letter, as Abulafia says ("your head, because it is outside; your heart, in picturing it"). *No evidence either way.*
5. **Absorption and arousal.** Abulafia's reports (heat, trembling, hair standing up, a sense of being anointed, weakness, forms, voices) are not relaxation. They look like the arousal-side states of Vajrayana deity practice (Amihai & Kozhevnikov, 2014) combined with the absorption-driven unusual experiences of intensive imaginative prayer (Luhrmann et al., 2013; Lifshitz et al., 2019). Two further ingredients raise the odds: **expectation** (the student is told what may come) and **setting** (alone, night, dim light or many candles). In predictive terms, strong expectation plus weakened ordinary input is a recipe for experiences shaped by the expectation (van Elk & Aleman, 2017; Lifshitz et al., 2019). This says nothing about whether what comes is true (§4); it says the conditions reliably favour vivid experience. *Theory built on moderate findings.*
6. **A near analogue: glossolalia.** Speaking in a stream of meaningless syllables in prayer changed blood flow in frontal and parietal lobes and the left caudate in a preliminary scan (**Newberg et al., 2006**, *pilot imaging study*). Worth noting only because it too is sound without meaning in a devotional frame.

**Arzy and Idel (2015)** read the Abulafian reports of seeing one's own form and of the soul leaving the body alongside neurological cases of bodily doubling and dissociation. That is a humanities-neuroscience comparison, not a measurement.

**Prediction (ours):** Abulafian chanting should look, physiologically, like *both* a mantra (slow breath, high HRV, DMN down) *and* an arousal practice (sympathetic surges at the "fear and trembling" points). That mix is unusual and would be worth measuring.

## 2.2 Entering the letters of prayer (the Baal Shem Tov and the Maggid); Heikhalot ascent

**What is done:** attention placed on the words of fixed prayer, on their letters and sounds, "entering" them, rather than on their plain meaning or on one's requests. The Heikhalot literature uses long repetitive hymns of Names and praises.

**Likely mechanisms:** focused attention on a rhythmic verbal stream (§1.2); partial loosening of everyday meaning by attending to letter and sound (§2.1, point 1); mantra-like repetition in the hymns (§2.1, point 2); absorption (§1.6). One scanning study found that *improvised* prayer to God activated the brain's social-cognition network (temporal pole, medial prefrontal cortex, temporo-parietal junction, precuneus), whereas a *formal* memorised prayer (the Lord's Prayer) did not (**Schjoedt et al., 2009**, *imaging study, Danish Christians*). **Ours:** fixed liturgy, prayed by entering its letters, is closer to the mantra end; hisbodedus (§2.6) is closer to the conversation end. Chassidic prayer moves between both, which may be part of its design. *Indirect evidence.*

## 2.3 The Name before the eyes, and picturing (Shivisi; the Piaseczner; Arizal yichudim)

**What is done:** picturing the four-letter Name before one's eyes ("I have set the Lord before me always"); the Piaseczner's pictures (standing before the Throne like a son, taking part in the Exodus "as if you were there", the sky and its other side, a desert with one's father near); the Arizal's yichudim, which combine and picture letters of Names in set orders.

1. **Imagery works like weak perception.** Visual mental images use the same machinery as seeing, down to primary visual cortex, and function as "a weak form of perception" (**Pearson et al., 2015**, *review*). This is why a pictured scene can carry some of the force of a seen one.
2. **Pictures move feelings more than words.** Imagining events raised emotion more than processing the same content verbally (Holmes & Mathews, 2005, *experiments*; 2010, *review*) *[reused; part-a step 4]*. The Piaseczner's rule that "strong thought needs pictures in it" (Hakhsharat 98) is, in these terms, a claim that imagery is the emotional amplifier of a thought. *Moderate, tested mainly for anxiety.*
3. **Visualization practice builds a specific capacity.** Tibetan practitioners of deity visualization, after meditating, showed a "dramatic increase" on imagery tasks that practitioners of open-presence meditation and controls did not (**Kozhevnikov et al., 2009**, *experiment*); the authors read this as access to heightened visuospatial resources during the practice rather than a general improvement. The same style produced arousal, not relaxation (Amihai & Kozhevnikov, 2014). *Preliminary.* **Ours:** the Arizal's yichudim, which require holding and moving letter-forms, are the closest Jewish analogue to deity visualization; nothing has been measured.
4. **Kataphatic prayer training.** A month of imaginative prayer raised imagery vividness and unusual sensory experiences (Luhrmann et al., 2013) *[reused]*. This is the closest experimental parallel to the Piaseczner's picturing, and it carries its own safety note (§5).
5. **A picture of a relationship, not only a scene.** The Piaseczner's central picture is relational (a son before his father). Imagery combined with attachment is taken up in §2.6.

**The Piaseczner's own two observations match the science closely.** "If you cannot picture the holy thing, come at it sideways; straining kills the image" (Mevo 395) and "a device used daily stops working" (Shema, only three times a week; Hakhsharat 436) describe effortful imagery failing and emotional responses *habituating* (weakening with repetition), both basic findings of experimental psychology.

## 2.4 Hisbonenus: thinking a truth through until it is real

**What is done:** one line of teaching, held for a long time, in breadth (many examples), length (carried into particulars) and depth (into its root), with the mind brought back when it drifts, until "the heart answers" (ONE-PROCESS step 6).

1. **Elaboration, not mere repetition.** Holding a thing in mind longer does not deepen it unless the meaning is worked (Craik & Watkins, 1973) *[reused; part-a step 6]*. Breadth, length and depth are a precise description of elaborative processing.
2. **From "knowing that" to "feeling that".** Teasdale's model distinguishes *propositional* meaning (a true sentence) from *implicational* meaning (a felt, whole-situation sense that moves emotion), and argues that only the second changes how one feels (Teasdale, 1993) *[reused]*. "I knew this before too, but I only knew it" (Tzav VeZeruz 72) is this gap in one line. Part-a rated this its strongest parallel.
3. **Reappraisal.** Reinterpreting a situation is the emotion-regulation strategy with the best evidence (Webb et al., 2012, *meta-analysis*) *[reused]*. Hisbonenus is reappraisal of *everything at once*: this cup, this worry, this self, seen as held and said into being.
4. **Belief updating (predictive terms; theory).** A hisbonenus is an attempt to install and strengthen a high-level model ("there is nothing but Him; I am held") and give it high precision, so that it shapes how ordinary input is read. Two theoretical routes are relevant. One: memories and beliefs become briefly changeable when reactivated together with new emotional experience, which has been proposed as a mechanism of change in psychotherapy (**Lane et al., 2015**, *theory with target-article commentary; contested*). Two: loosened priors may be easier to revise (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019, *theory*). **Ours:** hisbonenus reactivates the old model on purpose (it starts from "where you stand", the actual cup, the actual worry) and pairs it with a new, emotionally charged reading. That is the structure Lane et al. describe. It is a hypothesis.
5. **Attention as scaffolding.** Binding and returning without arguing train focused attention (§1.2) and avoid the rebound that comes from pushing thoughts away (Wang et al., 2020) *[reused]*.

**What is different about hisbonenus.** Most meditation science studies practices that *reduce* conceptual processing. Hisbonenus *increases* it, on purpose, on one theme. So the prediction (**ours**) is not "DMN down" but something more like "self-referential, narrative DMN down while meaning-making and imagery (which also use DMN hubs) are recruited for one theme". This has never been scanned.

## 2.5 Ayin, bitul, quieting, and looking at thoughts (the Maggid; the Piaseczner's hashkatah)

**What is done:** the Maggid's teaching of becoming *ayin* (nothing) before God; the Piaseczner's hashkatah: watch the thoughts for a few minutes ("what am I thinking?"), feel the head empty, then tie the emptied mind to one verse; gaze at the small hand of a clock; "looking at a thought dims it; dwelling in it feeds it" (PIASECZNO §3, steps 2-3).

1. **Meta-awareness decouples.** Noticing a thought *as a thought* is the core of meta-awareness (Schooler et al., 2011) *[reused]*, and stepping back from thoughts ("decentering") is a measurable capacity (Bernstein et al., 2015) *[reused]*. The Piaseczner's "look at, not in" is almost a definition of decentering.
2. **Naming calms.** His "ask: what am I picturing now?" resembles affect labeling, which reduced amygdala response in the scanner (Lieberman et al., 2007) and acts as implicit regulation (Torre & Lieberman, 2018) *[both reused; part-a step 1]*.
3. **Quieting the self-network.** Brief open-monitoring and focused styles quiet the DMN (§1.3).
4. **Shallower prediction.** In Laukkonen and Slagter's terms (§1.5), stopping the stream of planning is exactly a reduction of "temporally deep" modelling. The Piaseczner's own device for stopping it ("think that you have no tomorrow to plan", Derekh HaMelekh 90) targets the future-modelling that the theory names. *Theory.*
5. **Nondual and hypo-egoic states** (§1.7) are the closest scientific descriptions of what quieting and bitul feel like from inside. The line from part-a and part-b stands: bitul before Him is not the same claim as a change in self-experience.
6. **Hashkatah is short, and followed by a verse.** The Piaseczner keeps it to "a few minutes" and then fills it. Abulafia's emptying is also filled at once, with letters on every breath. **Ours:** in predictive language, both clear a space and then *install* something in it. That is the design of the model in §3.

## 2.6 Hisbodedus and speech to God

**What is done:** the Piaseczner's (and Breslov's) speech to God in one's own language, aloud, before a pictured Throne; naming the thing itself; stressing "You"; keeping words that burst out; "mouth first, the heart follows" (PIASECZNO §3, steps 7-8).

1. **Prayer uses the brain's system for real relationships.** Improvised personal prayer recruited the same network used to think about other people's minds, as if God were a real, responsive partner (Schjoedt et al., 2009, *imaging study*). Van Elk and Aleman (2017) fold this into their framework.
2. **Naming the feeling.** Affect labeling (Lieberman et al., 2007; Kircanski et al., 2012) *[reused]*.
3. **Disclosure.** Writing or speaking about personal emotional experience has small but reliable benefits: 146 randomised studies, average effect r ≈ .075 (**Frattaroli, 2006**, *meta-analysis*; the method comes from **Pennebaker, 1997**, *review*). Hisbodedus is disclosure *to someone*, which the lab paradigm is not.
4. **God as an attachment figure.** The religion-as-attachment model (Granqvist, Mikulincer & Shaver, 2010; Granqvist et al., 2012) *[reused; part-a step 4, part-b step 10]*: a secure base and safe haven. The Piaseczner's "a son before his father" is an attachment picture.
5. **Making God real by practice.** Luhrmann's account (§1.6) is essentially a theory of why hisbodedus deepens with repetition: the inner sense of a responsive Other is trained.

*Strength: moderate for labeling; small for disclosure; preliminary for prayer imaging; theory for attachment as the mechanism.*

## 2.7 Niggun

**What is done:** a wordless or near-wordless tune, often repeated, alone or with others; the Piaseczner: "at first you sing to wake the soul; then it sings by itself" (Hakhsharat 279); Abulafia: "combining is like the ears' hearing" (Gan Na'ul 64).

1. **Music engages the reward system.** Music that gives "chills" engaged brain regions for reward and emotion (ventral striatum, midbrain, amygdala, orbitofrontal cortex) (**Blood & Zatorre, 2001**, *imaging study*), and peak musical emotion released dopamine in the striatum, with anticipation and peak in different sub-regions (**Salimpoor et al., 2011**, *imaging study*). *Moderate.*
2. **Many routes from music to feeling.** Juslin and Västfjäll list mechanisms besides conscious appraisal: brain-stem reflexes, conditioning, emotional contagion, visual imagery, episodic memory and musical expectancy (**Juslin & Västfjäll, 2008**, *theory*). A niggun learned at a farbrengen carries episodic memory and conditioning with it.
3. **Bonding and synchrony.** Singing together in time increases cooperation (**Wiltermuth & Heath, 2009**, *experiments*); a meta-analysis found weak-to-moderate effects of synchrony on prosocial behaviour (**Mogan, Fischer & Bulbulia, 2017**, *meta-analysis*), but a later analysis argues that participants' expectations and unblinded experimenters may inflate these effects (**Atwood, Schachner & Mehr, 2022**). Music may bond through self-other merging and through endorphin release in exertive rhythmic activity (**Tarr, Launay & Dunbar, 2014**, *review*). *Weak-to-moderate, with a live replication question.*
4. **Breath and wordlessness.** Sung phrases lengthen the out-breath (compare Bernardi et al., 2001), and a niggun without words gives the mind rhythm and melody with no semantic load, which is Abulafia's letters by another route (**ours**).

## 2.8 Devekus in all acts: background attention through the day

**What is done:** cleaving to God during ordinary acts, not only in a sitting: "Shivisi" as a standing awareness; blessings and small acts as reminders; the manual's "one fixed moment today".

1. **Prospective memory**, remembering to do something at the right moment, works partly by effortful monitoring and partly by *spontaneous retrieval* when a well-chosen cue appears (**Einstein & McDaniel, 2005**, *review*). **Ours:** the halachic day (blessings before eating, mezuzah, washing) is a dense field of cues; devekus in action can be read as training spontaneous retrieval of one intention.
2. **If-then plans** attach an intention to a cue and have medium-to-large effects on follow-through (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006, *meta-analysis*) *[reused]*.
3. **Habit** forms slowly, over weeks to months (Lally et al., 2010) *[reused]*.
4. **Informal practice.** Among mindfulness practitioners, informal practice in daily life was associated with well-being and psychological flexibility (**Birtwell et al., 2019**, *correlational survey*).
5. **A background, not a foreground.** Nondual traits, measured by the NADA (Hanley et al., 2018) *[reused]*, are the closest measured construct to a steady background awareness during activity.

*Evidence: indirect throughout. Nobody has studied "sustained background devotion" as such.*

## 2.9 The body: swaying, bowing, effort

**What is done:** swaying in prayer, bowing, praying aloud, the Piaseczner's hard effort ("dance hard at Simchat Torah"), and his warning that fervour from movement may be "nerves, not fire".

1. **Rhythm.** Gentle rhythmic rocking during a nap hastened sleep and strengthened sleep-related brain rhythms in a small study (**Bayer et al., 2011**, *experiment*). That shows rocking reaches the brain's state; it does not show what swaying in prayer does.
2. **Arousal.** Vigorous movement and loud voice raise physiological arousal. The Piaseczner's worry, that arousal may be taken for depth, fits the general finding that bodily arousal is interpreted through context (**ours**; we did not cite the classic misattribution studies, whose replication record is mixed).
3. **Synchrony and exertion** (§2.7, point 3).
4. **Caution about "embodied" claims.** Popular claims that body postures change hormones and mindset have failed large replications, for example "power posing" (**Ranehill et al., 2015**, *large replication*). Any claim that a bodily practice changes the mind needs direct evidence.
5. **Ritual.** Rituals, as a class, may regulate emotion, signal group membership and settle performance anxiety (Hobson et al., 2018, *review*) *[reused]*.

*Evidence: weak. This is the least-studied family.*

## 3. One model: how this tradition's meditation works (**our hypothesis**)

*Everything in this section is ours. It is a scientific hypothesis, stated so that it could be wrong.*

## 3.1 The model in one sentence

**Jewish and Chassidic meditation works by (1) loosening the grip of the mind's habitual self-models and word-meanings, (2) installing, in that loosened space, one high-level model of reality and the self (the unity; "I am held") by elaboration, imagery, relationship and emotion, and (3) consolidating it by spaced repetition, cues and deeds, so that the new model becomes the default through which ordinary life is read.**

## 3.2 The three phases, and what drives each

| Phase | What changes (science terms) | Jewish techniques that mainly serve it | Mechanisms (§§1-2) | Evidence for the mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Loosen** | Less self-referential thought; habitual meanings and predictions held less tightly (lower precision); attention freed | Abulafian permutation and chanting; entering the letters; niggun without words; hashkatah; looking at thoughts; ayin | Attention training; satiation; mantra DMN quieting; slow breath; meta-awareness; decentering | Moderate |
| **2. Install** | A new high-level model built and given weight; it becomes emotionally real (implicational, not only propositional) | Hisbonenus; picturing before the Throne; speech to God as "You"; the Shema with readiness; niggun that "sings by itself" | Elaboration; imagery-emotion; reappraisal; attachment; reward; reconsolidation (theory) | Moderate for parts; the "install after loosening" link is theory |
| **3. Consolidate** | The new model generalises from sitting to day; state becomes trait | Fixed times; repetition over weeks; the deed at the end; blessings as cues; devekus in action; the fellowship | If-then plans; habit; prospective memory; spaced practice; social support | Moderate (plans, habit); indirect (devekus) |
| **Gate (throughout)** | Arousal and loosening kept within what the person can hold | Stop-rules; exit (eat, drink, smell, be glad); "if nothing comes, leave it a week"; not alone | Non-monotonic dose (Britton, 2019); control-inference (Deane et al., 2020) | Theory plus adverse-effect data (§5) |

## 3.3 Where the three roads sit on the model

- **Abulafia** puts most of the weight on phase 1, and does it with more force than any other Jewish method (meaning removed, breath and body recruited, night, solitude, expectation). His phase 2 is *received*, not built: the "influx" that writes in the cleared space. His phase 3 is the exit ritual and the teacher.
- **Chabad hisbonenus** puts most of the weight on phase 2. Its phase 1 is mild (binding attention, coming back without arguing). It loosens the self not by emptying the mind but by filling it with one meaning so large that the self it held "gives way" (ABULAFIA §6.1). Phase 3 is the deed and "know *today*".
- **The Piaseczner** runs all three in miniature: a few minutes of quieting (1), then a verse, a picture, a tune and speech (2), then "clothe the stirring at once in a mitzvah" and fixed times (3).

## 3.4 Why the order might matter (the weakest link, stated plainly)

The model predicts that **phase 2 works better after phase 1**: a truth installed after the habitual stream has been quieted should take hold more deeply than the same truth contemplated cold. That is what the predictive accounts suggest (§1.5), and it is what the Piaseczner and Abulafia both *do*. But the evidence for "loosen, then install" comes from theory and from psychedelic research (REBUS), not from meditation or prayer. It is the single most important untested claim in this file.

## 3.5 What would test it

Each test below is feasible with ordinary research tools. None has been done.

1. **Order test (the key one).** Randomise religious participants to (a) five minutes of Piaseczner-style quieting then a hisbonenus on "held", (b) the same hisbonenus without quieting, (c) quieting then a neutral reading. Measure felt security (State Adult Attachment Measure, Gillath et al., 2009 *[reused]*), trust-in-God (Rosmarin et al., 2011 *[reused]*), and whether the change lasts a week. The model predicts (a) > (b) > (c) on the target belief.
2. **Satiation test of Abulafian chanting.** Compare chanting permuted letters, chanting a meaningful phrase, and silent rest. Measure the N400 to related words afterwards (Kounios et al., 2000 method), breathing rate and HRV, and self-generated thought. Prediction: permuted letters satiate meaning and reduce self-generated thought more than the meaningful phrase.
3. **Arousal-plus-calm signature.** Record heart, breath and skin conductance during Abulafian-type chanting. Prediction: slow-breath HRV rise with sympathetic surges at reported "trembling" moments, unlike ordinary mantra.
4. **Hisbonenus is not mantra.** Scan hisbonenus against mantra repetition. Prediction: mantra lowers DMN broadly; hisbonenus lowers self-referential hubs less and recruits meaning and imagery regions for one theme.
5. **Consolidation.** Randomise sittings that end in a planned deed against sittings that do not. Prediction: better week-long carry-over of the target belief and behaviour.
6. **Dose and safety.** Compare gentle, short hisbonenus with intensive, solitary, breath-paced chanting on the adverse-effect measures in §5. Prediction: more unusual perceptual and depersonalisation experiences with intensive chanting, especially in high-absorption people.
7. **Individual differences.** Measure absorption (Tellegen & Atkinson, 1974) at the start. Prediction: absorption moderates every effect above, strongest for picturing and chanting.

**What would count against the model:** if quieting before hisbonenus made no difference or made it worse; if Abulafian chanting behaved just like any mantra; if deeds at the end added nothing; if hisbonenus scanned like mantra.

## 4. Where science cannot follow

This needs saying clearly and with respect.

- **Science studies experience and mechanism, not the metaphysics.** A scan can show that improvised prayer uses the brain's system for other minds (Schjoedt et al., 2009). It cannot show whether Someone is there. Showing *how* an experience arises does not show that its object is unreal, any more than explaining the eye disproves the light. Nor does it show the object is real. The question is simply outside the method.
- **The soul, the yechidah, the Essence.** The tradition's claims that the soul is "a part of God above", that the yechidah is always one with the Essence, that bitul is giving oneself to Him and not only a change in how the self feels: none of these is a claim about brain states, and no measurement bears on them. Part-a and part-b made the same point step by step.
- **Prophecy and the influx.** Abulafia's influx from the Active Intellect, the ruach hakodesh the tradition speaks of, the Piaseczner's "higher sight": science can describe the conditions under which people report such things (absorption, expectation, arousal, solitude) and their correlates. It cannot adjudicate their source. Both traditions already test the fruit, not the phenomenon: Abulafia and Vital test every spirit against Torah and character; the Mitteler Rebbe suspects any arousal the self can feel. That is a criterion science can respect without sharing.
- **What science *can* legitimately add.** It can say which techniques are likely to produce which states, in whom, at what risk, and with what lasting effects on mood, attention, relationships and behaviour. It can also warn where a vivid experience is more likely to be produced by the method's conditions than by anything else. That is useful to a tradition that already distinguishes true fire from "nerves".

## 5. Safety

Part-b ("Adverse effects and counterfeits") already summarised the main research: difficulties are not rare (Schlosser et al., 2019; Goldberg et al., 2022; Farias et al., 2020), they span perception, emotion, body and sense of self (Lindahl et al., 2017), lasting harms link to dysregulated arousal and dissociation (Britton et al., 2021), and psychosis is rare but documented (Kuijpers et al., 2007) *[all reused]*. Risk rose with repetitive negative thinking, **deconstructive-only practice**, and **retreats**; it was lower in religious practitioners (Schlosser et al., 2019). This section adds the mechanism view and applies it.

## 5.1 What the mechanism view adds

1. **More is not always better.** Many mindfulness-related processes follow an inverted U: helpful up to a point, then harmful (**Britton, 2019**, *review*). Intensity, duration and solitude are the dials.
2. **Selfless bliss and depersonalization may share machinery.** The active-inference account suggests the same loosening of the self-model can feel like peace or like horror, depending on whether the person still infers control (Deane et al., 2020, *theory*). This is why *who* practises, and *with whom*, matters as much as the technique.
3. **Arousal practices are different from calming ones** (Amihai & Kozhevnikov, 2014). A method that produces trembling and a sense of dying is loading the sympathetic system, not resting it.
4. **Imagery training increases unusual experiences** in some people (Luhrmann et al., 2013), and absorption predicts both spiritual experience and unusual perception (Lifshitz et al., 2019).

## 5.2 Applied: Abulafian intensive practice against gentle hisbonenus

| Risk factor (from the research) | Abulafian practice as written | Gentle hisbonenus (manual) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deconstructive-only practice (Schlosser et al., 2019) | Yes: meaning removed on purpose | No: constructive, meaning built |
| Intensity and duration; retreat-like conditions | High: long, night, repeated | Low: short sittings |
| Solitude | Required ("no one will hear your voice") | Not required; can be with others |
| Sleep loss | Night practice favoured | Not asked |
| Breath control | Central: one long breath per letter, set pauses | None (manual guard: no breath method) |
| Arousal (sympathetic) | High: fear, trembling, weakness expected | Low to moderate: "the deeper, the less he feels it" |
| Expectation of visions and voices | Yes, with instructions for them | No; visions not sought |
| Absorption-dependent imagery | Yes (picturing angels, the court) | Some (the mashal), let go |
| Ends in grounding | Yes: exit ritual (eat, drink, smell, be glad) | Yes: a deed |
| Teacher and screening | Required by Abulafia (character, a teacher who has arrived) | Guide plus stop-rules |

**Reading (ours):** Abulafian practice, done as written and alone, stacks nearly every risk factor the research has identified: deconstructive, intensive, solitary, nocturnal, breath-paced, arousing, and primed to expect perceptual experiences. Abulafia knew this and built in screening, a teacher and stop rules; Kaplan warned that some Kabbalistic practices are dangerous (thread 07). Gentle hisbonenus sits at the other end of almost every dial. The Piaseczner's quieting sits in between, and his own limits (a few minutes, then fill it; softly, never forcefully; the Shema device only three times a week) match the research's picture of a safer dose.

## 5.3 What this suggests for the builders (consistent with `ABULAFIA.md` §6.4-6.5 and part-b)

1. **Keep the manual's exclusions.** No chanting or permuting of Names, no breath-and-head drill, no seeking of visions. The science supports this on mechanism grounds, separately from halachah.
2. **Treat Abulafian peak signs as stop-signs in our setting.** Feeling the soul leave the body, thinking one will die, seeing forms, hearing voices, limbs going weak: inside Abulafia's frame these were goals; without a teacher, research on depersonalization and dysregulated arousal says stop and ground.
3. **Keep quieting short and filled.** A minute or two, then a line (the manual already does this).
4. **Keep the exit.** Abulafia's exit and the manual's "do something plain with your hands" are what the research would design: re-engage the body and the ordinary world.
5. **Know who is more at risk.** High absorption, a history of trauma or dissociation, repetitive negative thinking, sleep loss, and anyone drawn to intensive solitary practice. Part-b's suggestion to add "feeling unreal for days" to the danger line is reinforced here.
6. **Never alone for intensive work.** The Baal Shem Tov's "a person alone is in danger" (cited in ABULAFIA §6.4) and the research agree.

## 6. Strongest and weakest links

**Strongest (mechanism well supported, fit to the technique close):** - *Imagery moves emotion* (Holmes & Mathews; Pearson et al.) → the Piaseczner's picturing and the Name before the eyes. - *Slow paced breath calms and raises HRV* (Zaccaro et al.; Bernardi et al.) → one-breath-per-letter chanting and long sung phrases. - *Repetition loosens word-meaning; mantra quiets the self-network* (Kounios et al.; Tian & Huber; Berkovich-Ohana et al.) → Abulafian permutation and chanting. - *Elaboration and implicational meaning* (Craik & Watkins; Teasdale) → hisbonenus's breadth, length and depth and "I only knew it". - *If-then plans and habit* (Gollwitzer & Sheeran; Lally et al.) → the deed at the end and fixed times.

**Weakest (theory, or a long analogy):** - *"Loosen, then install"*: that quieting makes a new belief easier to take in. Theory, borrowed from psychedelic research. Untested for prayer. - *Predictive-processing descriptions of hisbonenus* in general: a useful language, not evidence. - *Head movements and swaying*: no direct evidence at all. - *Devekus in action as prospective memory*: a plausible analogy; nothing measured. - *Nondual awareness and bitul*: the experiences may overlap; the claims do not.
