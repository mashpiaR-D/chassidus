<a id="c35"></a> ## C35 · I can't let go of a grudge

*4 findings from Tanya, Toras Chaim (part 1 of 4), Derech Mitzvosecha, Inyanah Shel Toras HaChassidus*

> Someone wronged me and it replays. When I'm hurt I hold it forever. I quietly hate certain people. He owes me, so why should I do right by him?

**What is really going on.** Tanya: rancour is a rising of the heart, and the mind by its nature can rule it and act the very opposite, with kindness and patience toward the one who wronged you. Anger is a moment when faith has left: had he believed this came from God, he would not be angry. The one who harmed you was one messenger (the reader adds that he is still accountable). Remembering your own failings helps you hear insult without anger. Toras Chaim is honest about the limit: the mind cannot rewrite a trait, only rein it like an animal, and rejoicing in suffering never means the pain stops being pain. A heart soft as a reed forgives and makes up quickly; one hard as a cedar keeps its wrath forever. Derech Mitzvosecha: souls are included in one another, so hating another cuts a piece from yourself; take on love of your fellow before prayer. Inyanah: doing right is not a trade, so you don't hold back what you owe because he owes you.

**The false conclusions, and what the works set against them**

- *"He did this to me, so my anger is justified."* → Had you believed it came from God, you would not be angry. The person was one messenger, even though he stays accountable. `TAN·Q30`
- *"That's my nature; I can't forgive and I can't change."* → The mind can't rewrite a trait, but it can rein it and make it bend like a reed, to forgive and pass over. `TC1·Q13` · `TC1·Q16` · `TC1·Q17`
- *"Forgiving means pretending it didn't hurt."* → Nature isn't reversed. The suffering stays suffering; faith finds hidden good in it, and the mind governs the reaction. `TC1·Q14` · `TC1·Q15`
- *"What I feel about him is my own business."* → He is included in you. Hating him pushes away part of yourself, though the separation is only bodily. `DM·Q02` · `DM·Q03`
- *"He owes me, so I can hold back what's his."* → Even when he owes you, you may not withhold what he entrusted to you. The right act is its own purpose. `INY·Q18`

1. **Rein the grudge and act the opposite** · *from Tanya, Toras Chaim* (1) When anger or rancour rises, recognise it as a rising of the heart that the mind can overrule. (2) Don't try to make the hurt stop hurting. Rein your response. (3) Remember that what came to you came from Heaven; he was a messenger, though still accountable. (4) Remembering your own failings, hear the insult without answering. (5) Don't repay in kind. Act with kindness and patience toward him, as far as is safe and wise. (6) Let the mind pass over the trait: forgive and be quick to reconcile. *When:* In the moment of insult or loss, and as a lasting shaping of character. *For:* People who hold grudges, are quick to anger over slights, or are rigid and unforgiving. *Not for:* Never for someone in ongoing abuse, coercion or danger. Protection comes first, and accepting insult must never mean submitting to mistreatment. `TAN·Q02` · `TAN·Q30` · `TAN·Q24` · `TC1·Q13` · `TC1·Q14` · `TC1·Q15` · `TC1·Q16`
2. **Take on love of your fellow before prayer** · *from Derech Mitzvosecha* (1) Before praying, accept on yourself 'love your fellow as yourself'. (2) Contemplate that every soul includes the others, and that the separation is only bodily. (3) See hatred as cutting away a part of yourself. (4) Then pray whole. *When:* Daily, just before prayer. *For:* Someone carrying quiet resentment or feeling cut off from others. *Not for:* Must not be used to push someone to stay close to a person who harms them. `DM·Q04` · `DM·Q01` · `DM·Q02` · `DM·Q03`

- From *"He did this to me, and my anger is justified."* to **It was decreed from Heaven, and he was one of many messengers. Anger is a moment when faith has left.** Placing the source in God takes away the target the anger needs, while the wrongdoer stays accountable, so the mind can rule the heart. *(Tanya)* `TAN·Q30` · `TAN·Q02` - From *"I must hold on to my hurt; it's too much to bear quietly."* to **Keeping your own failings in view makes you humble before others and lets you take insult and hardship without anger.** Memory of one's own faults softens the demand that others be faultless. *(Tanya)* `TAN·Q24` · `TAN·Q23` - From *"That's my nature; I can't forgive and I can't change."* to **The mind can't change a trait's essence, but it can rein it in and make it bend like a reed.** The goal becomes honest and reachable: not a new nature, but a reined one. *(Toras Chaim)* `TC1·Q13` · `TC1·Q16` · `TC1·Q17` - From *"He's separate from me. What I feel about him is my business."* to **He is included in you and you in him. Hating him cuts away a piece of you.** Seeing the other as part of oneself turns love into wholeness rather than a burden. *(Derech Mitzvosecha)* `DM·Q01` · `DM·Q02` · `DM·Q03` - From *"He owes me, so I can hold back what's his."* to **Doing right is not a trade. The right act is its own purpose.** Obligation doesn't depend on the other person's behavior, so his wrong doesn't license yours. *(Inyanah Shel Toras HaChassidus (On the Essence of Chassidus))* `INY·Q18`

**Where the works agree.** The grudge is a movement of the heart that the mind can govern. Its response need not follow the feeling, and acting the opposite, kindly and patiently, is within reach. The works also agree that the other person's wrong doesn't cancel your obligations to him.

**Where they differ.** Their grounds differ. Tanya works through faith (it came from Heaven) and one's own failings; Derech Mitzvosecha through the unity of souls; Toras Chaim through the mind's rein on a rigid trait, honest that pain stays pain; Inyanah through duty as its own purpose. Tanya asks for evident affection toward the offender, while Toras Chaim aims at forgiving and reconciling.

**Order and timing.** In the moment: rein the reaction and don't answer in kind. Afterward: place what happened in a larger frame (from Heaven, one's own failings, the unity of souls). Daily before prayer: take on love of one's fellow. Over time: soften the trait from cedar toward reed. Where there is danger, protection comes before all of this.

- Victims of ongoing abuse, coercion or danger must not be told to bear it, to be kind to the abuser, or to count insult as praise. Protection and reporting come first. - 'It was decreed from Heaven' can become a spiritual bypass that silences a victim or excuses the wrongdoer. The texts keep him accountable. - 'Remember your own sins' must not turn into self-blame for victims. - A grudge tied to fresh grief or trauma needs time and care, not a quick demand to forgive. - Reconciling does not require restoring trust to an unsafe person.

<a id="c36"></a> ## C36 · I judge people and can't love them

*4 findings from Tanya, Sefer HaSichos (part 1 of 2), Sefer HaSichos (part 2 of 2)*

> I can't stop seeing what's wrong with people. I'm harsh about others' faults and easy on my own. My love stops at the edge of my own kind, and wrongdoers make me furious.

**What is really going on.** Tanya says the judge does not know the other's place: his trade, his streets and his temperament may set before him a war the judge never fought. Honesty and love are reconciled by hating the wrong and loving the hidden spark. Pity for that spark cancels hatred, and a thought of another's evil is to be pushed out like smoke. The Rebbe Rayatz finds the problem in self-satisfaction: what one sees as bad in another is one's own, like the Temple basin made of mirrors. He points to seeing each person's good, rebuking only in private and with love, and asking what caused a fall. His later talks add delaying anger by an hour and a minute, turning it on oneself, and starting from the other's good and one's own lack.

**The false conclusions, and what the works set against them**

- *"I'm obviously better than him."* → You haven't reached his place. His livelihood and streets may set before him a war you never fought. Every kindness shown to you calls for more humility, not a higher seat. `TAN·Q12` · `TAN·Q25`
- *"The problem is them."* → The general flaw is that everyone is pleased with himself. What one sees as not-good in another is one's own not-good. `SHS1·Q17` · `SHS1·Q19`
- *"Honesty won't let me love people like that."* → Hate the evil in them and love the hidden good, the spark within. Seeing each person's good is not naivety. `TAN·Q15` · `SHS1·Q18`
- *"My anger at wrongdoers is justified and needs to come out now."* → Put it off for an hour and a minute and it shows itself as foolishness. If anger is due, direct it at yourself. `SHS2·Q28` · `SHS2·Q29`
- *"Their fall is none of my business."* → If the other's state doesn't bother you, it is a sign you don't care about yourself either. The pain belongs inside you, and the other should get a warm face. `SHS1·Q29` · `SHS2·Q31`

1. **Before judging, ask about his place** · *from Tanya* (1) When you are blessed or succeed, let each kindness make you humbler, not higher. (2) Before judging, ask about his place: his livelihood, what his eyes see all day, his temperament. (3) Ask whether you have fought a war as hard as his. (4) Refuse to lord it over others or speak against them. *When:* At the moment the verdict or the swelling of pride arises; also at the fixed reckoning hour. *For:* The successful, the learned, the habitual judge. *Not for:* Not a mood of worthlessness, which the same work rejects as sadness. Never a reason to excuse harmful conduct. `TAN·Q12` · `TAN·Q25`
2. **Love the hidden good, push out the evil thought** · *from Tanya* (1) In your own life, put the soul first and the body second; love of others follows from that. (2) In someone who does wrong, separate the two: hate the wrong, love the hidden spark. (3) Wake pity for that spark in exile; pity cancels hatred. (4) If a thought of another's evil arises, push it out like smoke. *When:* Whenever contempt arises; the inner work at a fixed hour. *For:* Those who cannot love certain people, or who judge constantly. *Not for:* Not a demand to trust or stay close to someone who harms you. Pushing thoughts out 'like idolatry' is not for someone with intrusive-thought OCD. `TAN·Q15` · `TAN·Q32`
3. **The mirror, and covering the other** · *from Sefer HaSichos (Rayatz)* (1) When you see a fault in another, take it first as a picture of your own. (2) With others, start from their good; with yourself, start from what is missing. (3) Rebuke only with love and in private, never in public. (4) Look for the good in each person. (5) Where someone has fallen away, don't only sigh. Ask what caused it and look for good counsel to bring him close. *When:* Whenever judgment of another arises; a standing stance. *For:* The critical, the self-satisfied, and the resigned bystander. *Not for:* Victims of real harm. 'It's your own fault you see it' must never silence a report of abuse or wrongdoing. `SHS1·Q17` · `SHS1·Q18` · `SHS1·Q19` · `SHS1·Q29` · `SHS2·Q30`
4. **Cooling the flare** · *from Sefer HaSichos (Rayatz)* (1) When anger flares at a wrong, put it off for an hour and a minute. (2) Use the time to look at yourself: what is happening with you, and what will become of you. That cools the fire. (3) If anger is still due, aim it at yourself, not at him. (4) Let his lack pain you inside, and show him a warm face and draw him close. *When:* In the moment of anger. *For:* Someone quick to anger or harsh in judgment. *Not for:* Not for someone who already turns every failing inward with self-hatred; 'be angry at yourself' can feed it. Not a reason to stay silent about real danger. `SHS2·Q28` · `SHS2·Q29` · `SHS2·Q31` · `SHS1·Q15` · `SHS1·Q16`
5. **Mind over the emotions, over a year** · *from Sefer HaSichos (Rayatz)* (1) Look at yourself honestly, the good parts and the bad. (2) Uproot the plainly bad traits: envy, lying, gossip, cursing. (3) Clean the good parts of years of idle talk and mockery. (4) Let the emotions be run by the mind. (5) Let them act with full warmth on what the mind understands, until the two are one. *When:* The work of the whole year, daily. *For:* A person whose understanding does not reach his feelings or conduct. *Not for:* Fasts and self-mortification only within limits that never harm health. Not for the depressed or those with eating disorders. `SHS1·Q15` · `SHS1·Q16` · `SHS1·Q20` · `SHS1·Q21` · `SHS1·Q22`

- From *"I'm obviously better than him."* to **You haven't reached his place. His livelihood and his streets set before him a war you may never have fought.** When effort, not outward conduct, is the measure, every verdict on another becomes a question about oneself, and the ranking falls apart. *(Tanya)* `TAN·Q12` - From *"I can't love people like that."* to **Hate the wrong in them and love the hidden good, and let pity for that exiled spark cancel the hatred.** Separating the person from the wrong removes the conflict between honesty and love; compassion does what commanded feeling cannot. *(Tanya)* `TAN·Q15` · `TAN·Q32` - From *"The problem is them."* to **What you see as bad in another is your own. Fix it, and you'll stop seeing it in him.** The Temple basin was made of mirrors so that one would see one's own flaws. Fault-finding becomes self-diagnosis. *(Sefer HaSichos (Rayatz))* `SHS1·Q19` · `SHS1·Q17` - From *"My anger at people who do wrong is justified."* to **Put it off an hour and a minute and it shows itself as foolishness. **Where the works agree.** All agree that judgment should turn back on the judge, that the person is to be kept apart from the wrong, and that love comes through compassion and seeing the hidden good, not through willing a feeling. They agree that rebuke, when needed, should be private, loving, and aimed at bringing the person close.

**Where they differ.** Tanya keeps a real hatred of the evil and explains the other through his circumstances, and it would push the thought of his evil out of the mind entirely. The Rayatz reads the fault one sees as one's own (the mirror) and asks for active concern: finding out what caused the fall. The later talks add the delay of anger and the rule of starting from the other's good and one's own lack.

**Order and timing.** In the moment: delay the anger, push out the verdict, and ask about his place. Then turn to yourself: what in me does this show? Meet him with his good and a warm face; rebuke only privately and with love. Over the long term: the fixed reckoning and the year-long work of letting the mind run the emotions.

- 'What you see in another is your own' must never silence a victim or a report of abuse, and must not undermine legitimate moral or safety judgment. - Separating the person from the wrong is not a demand to trust or stay near someone who harms you. - 'Be angry at yourself instead' can feed self-hatred in someone already self-punishing. - Pushing thoughts out 'like idolatry' can worsen intrusive-thought OCD. - Fasts and mortification only within limits that never harm health.

<a id="c34"></a> ## C34 · Anger takes me over

*8 findings from Likkutei Torah (part 1 of 3), Likkutei Torah (part 2 of 3), Likkutei Torah (part 3 of 3), Toras Chaim (part 1 of 4), Derech Mitzvosecha, Biurei Tanya, Chanah Ariel, Sheeris Yehudah*

> When I'm angry, judgment leaves me. I see only faults, have no mercy, and say and do things I'd never do. Small things set me off, and I tell myself my anger is righteous.

**What is really going on.** The works agree that anger is emotion without the mind's rule. Likkutei Torah (Matot) says feelings not led by the mind stand alone, so anger has no mercy in it; the remedy is the mind ruling the heart, 'soft as a reed'. Toras Chaim says anger switches the eye to the bad eye, and reasoning follows the eye, so a small fault swells a hundredfold. Derech Mitzvosecha, in a parable, says a narrow mind fills up with one judgment while a broad one leaves room for another side, and kindness stays hidden underneath. Likkutei Torah (Shir HaShirim) says narrow awareness is like a child's. Chanah Ariel contrasts being held inside anger like a prisoner with holding it in the hand. Sheeris Yehudah, in asides, says anger over honor leads to error and asks for slowness. Likkutei Torah (Sh'lach) says anger spends the soul's fire and should be turned against one's own evil. Biurei Tanya warns that cruelty can dress itself as holy zeal.

**The false conclusions, and what the works set against them**

- *"In my anger I see clearly how bad he is."* → Anger has switched your eye. It inflates the fault and shrinks the good, and your reasoning follows the eye. A broader mind can find another side of the person too. `TC1·Q25` · `DM·Q26`
- *"My anger is righteous; I'm right and my honor was slighted."* → Anger over honor is exactly when judgment fails. Zeal can be a cruel nature dressed as holiness, and a love of honor stays invisible until it is crossed. `SY·Q21` · `BT·Q05` · `BT·Q08`
- *"Anger just happens to me; it takes over; that's who I am."* → There is a way to hold anger in the hand instead of being held by it like a prisoner. `CA·Q11` · `CA·Q12`
- *"I'm too angry to be kind right now."* → Kindness is still hidden in the heart during anger, and being addressed as merciful draws it out. `DM·Q21` · `DM·Q22`
- *"This small thing really is that big."* → Like a child, a narrow mind is stirred by everything against its will. The wider the awareness, the harder it is to become angry. `LT3·Q34` · `LT3·Q35` · `DM·Q24` · `DM·Q25`
- *"Being angry will get me what I need."* → Ask whether anger will help your actual aim at all. If it won't, dress the recoil in calm speech. `CA·Q37`

1. **Hold the anger instead of being held by it** · *from Chanah Ariel* (1) Notice when the anger has hold of you and your judgment is leaving. (2) Ask whether being angry at the one who wronged you will help your actual aim at all. (3) If it won't, cover the recoil and dress it in kindness: speak calmly, more than once or twice, and in several ways, until the purpose is reached. (4) Let whatever sharpness remains be something you hold, not something that holds you. *When:* In the moment of provocation. *For:* Someone whose anger overrides his judgment. *Not for:* Not a license to intimidate a household or students with 'controlled' anger. `CA·Q11` · `CA·Q12` · `CA·Q37`
2. **Slow down before deciding** · *from Sheeris Yehudah* (1) Notice when wounded honor or pressure is driving your response. (2) Remember that whoever comes to anger comes to error. (3) Give the question calm and slowness. Do not press, and do not be pressed. (4) In practical work, check carefully and slowly, not in haste. *When:* In the moment of judging or deciding. *For:* Anyone who must decide under emotional pressure. *Not for:* The text offers this in asides inside legal rulings, not as a treatment of anger. `SY·Q21` · `SY·Q22` · `SY·Q08`
3. **Widen the mind so mercy mixes in** · *from Likkutei Torah, Toras Chaim, Derech Mitzvosecha* (1) See that anger not led by the mind has no mercy in it. (2) Remember that anger has switched your eye. Look deliberately for the other side of the thing and the merit of the person. (3) Bring understanding into the feeling so that kindness and mercy mix into the anger. (4) Be soft as a reed, not hard as a cedar. *When:* In the moment of anger, and as a trait built over time. *For:* Someone whose anger shuts out all mercy, or who is set off by small things. *Not for:* Victims of abuse. Looking for the other's merit must never mean staying in danger or excusing harm, and softness does not mean giving up self-protection. `LT2·Q22` · `LT2·Q23` · `TC1·Q25` · `DM·Q26` · `LT3·Q35`
4. **Turn the fire toward your own wrong, in prayer** · *from Likkutei Torah* (1) See that anger, envy and craving spend the limited inner fire you have. (2) Twice a day, in Shema, contemplate God's oneness deeply, fixing the thought and not only remembering it. (3) In that heat, turn the indignation onto the wrong in yourself: why am I like this? (4) Let this spend and cool the raw anger, so none is left to harm others. *When:* Shema, morning and evening, daily. *For:* A stable person whose anger and passions drain his spiritual energy. *Not for:* Anyone with self-hatred, depression or self-harm risk. 'Rage at your own evil' can become self-attack. `LT1·Q25` · `LT1·Q26` · `LT1·Q27` · `LT1·Q28`

**Where the works agree.** Anger is the mind withdrawing and the feeling standing alone. A verdict reached in anger is unreliable, because the eye and the reasoning bend with it. The remedy is to restore or widen the mind, not to deny that one feels. Kindness is not destroyed by anger; it waits underneath.

**Where they differ.** Likkutei Torah (Matot) and Toras Chaim ask for softness 'like a reed'. Chanah Ariel lets some severity remain as a tool held in the hand. Likkutei Torah (Sh'lach) redirects anger against oneself. Sheeris Yehudah is practical: slowness. Biurei Tanya is suspicious of zeal itself. Derech Mitzvosecha and Likkutei Torah (Shir HaShirim) give parables rather than programs.

**Order and timing.** In the moment: notice you are held by it, slow down, and ask whether it will help (Chanah Ariel, Sheeris Yehudah). Then widen: remember the bad eye and look for the other side (Toras Chaim, Derech Mitzvosecha). Over time: the mind rules the heart (Likkutei Torah). In daily prayer, and only for the stable: turn the fire inward. Afterwards: test whether the 'zeal' was real.

- For someone being abused or wronged, anger may be a true signal. 'Look for his merit' and 'be soft as a reed' must never mean excusing harm or staying in danger. - Turning anger against one's own evil can become self-attack in anyone depressed, self-hating or at risk of self-harm. - Chanah Ariel's 'holding anger as a tool' is not a license to intimidate family, students or employees. - Anger with violence or threat of violence needs outside help and safety measures; the texts do not address this. - Telling someone their anger is 'only a bad eye' can invalidate a victim's accurate perception.

**Truths for the guide** *(candidates, each checked against its passages; see CHECKS.md)*

- **Anger changes the eye you see with** · While anger holds you, it changes the eye you look through: faults swell, the good disappears, and reasoning follows the eye. A verdict reached in anger reports on the anger, not the whole truth about the other person. *Check: yes.*
- **Hold the anger instead of being held by it** · You can be inside your anger like a prisoner, or you can hold it in your hand. The question 'will this help what I actually want?' is the handle. *Check: yes.*
- **Kindness is under the anger, not gone** · Even at the height of anger, the capacity for mercy is still present, hidden beneath. Remembering yourself as kind, or being addressed that way, can draw it out. *Check: yes.*

**What the core works do not cover.** No treatment of anger's protective role, or of what victims of anger should do. Nothing on calming the body or on repair after an outburst. Derech Mitzvosecha and Likkutei Torah (Shir HaShirim) give parables, and Sheeris Yehudah gives asides, not programs. The tears over small things in LT3:P10 are only partly about anger.
