# How a conversation creates change

**Only One research** · September 30, 2026 · early draft 0.1 · Result [043](../../CONTENTS.md#043) · manuscript 1 of 10 · [Cite](CITATION.bib) · [Quote check](../../verification/catalogue.md#how-a-conversation-creates-change-september-30-2026)

---
*Chassidus Unity Index, translation layer, track 5. 2026-09-30. For the builders of "Only One".*

The question of this track: what, in one chat and across a few days of chats, actually moves a person, and what does that mean for how the guide talks, how long it talks, when it stops, how it recovers when the person pushes back, how it ends, and how it opens the next day.

## Read this first

The owner's rules stand (claims.md D1 to D44): outside danger the guide never refers to therapy, doctors or medication; no breathing drills, body scans or relaxation; danger overrides everything; universal for anyone (D17, D28).
- **It builds on what we have.** `../conversation/research-counseling.md` already covers empathy, the alliance meta-analysis, MITI reflections and questions, the righting reflex and the AI template. This file does not repeat it. It adds: single-session change, chat-agent trials and their limits, alliance with AI, rupture and repair, change talk, Socratic questioning, insight and the "aha", if-then plans, writing, spacing and recall, and endings.
- **Citations.** Every study below was checked on 2026-09-30 through PubMed, Crossref, the publisher or a web search, and is marked *[confirmed by search]* or *[in our files]*. ## 1. What one conversation can and cannot do

- One session can change something measurable. Across 50 randomized trials with 10,508 young people, single-session interventions had a mean effect of Hedges g = 0.32; a young person who got one fared better than a control 58% of the time (Schleider and Weisz, 2017) *[confirmed by search]*. *Meta-analysis.* - The same review shows the limits plainly. Effects were larger against no treatment (0.41) than against an active control (0.14), larger for children than adolescents, and **smaller at follow-ups beyond 13 weeks**. Depression, at 0.21, was not significant. - The best-known large trial: 2,452 adolescents with raised depression, recruited on Instagram, did one online 20-to-30-minute program (a behavioral activation one, or one teaching that traits can change) or a supportive control. Both active programs reduced depression at three months by **d = 0.18**, reduced hopelessness (d = 0.16 to 0.28) and raised a sense of agency (d = 0.15 to 0.31) (Schleider et al., 2022, *Nature Human Behaviour*; the "Project YES" programs) *[confirmed by search]*. *Large RCT.* The effect is small per person and real across many people. - The first umbrella review, over 24 systematic reviews and 415 trials in youth and adults, found a pooled effect of SMD = -0.25 across outcomes and ages, with methodological concerns such as low preregistration (Schleider et al., 2025) *[confirmed by search]*. *Umbrella review.* - Change is often not gradual. In therapy, many people improve in one sudden step between two sessions, and those "sudden gains" predict better outcome (g = 0.68 at the end, 0.61 at follow-up), though some reverse (Shalom and Aderka, 2020; the pattern first described by Tang and DeRubeis, 1999) *[confirmed by search]*. *Meta-analysis.*

**What the successful single sessions share (ours, from reading the programs' descriptions):** they target one belief (hopelessness, "I can't change", "nothing I do matters"), they let the person *produce* the new view rather than hear it, they end with a concrete personal plan, and they are short. None tries to cover everything.

**What this means.** One conversation can shift one perception and seed one act. It cannot rebuild a life, and its effect fades unless something carries it forward. The Chassidic tradition agrees on both halves: R. Yekusiel of Liepli heard one phrase, "and You give life to them all", and "was turned into another man" (SH 5684, l.442, «ותומ"י נתהפך לאיש אחר בהשכלה ובעבודה») *[in our files, research-chassidic.md]*; and the Tanya's contemplation is "a long while every day" (Tanya, line 639, «כשיתבונן בזה שעה גדולה בכל יום») *[in our files, TECHNOLOGY.md]*. One moment can turn a person; staying turned is daily.

## 2. What chat agents have shown, and what they haven't

- **Woebot.** 70 students, two weeks, versus an information-only ebook. Depression (PHQ-9) fell in the Woebot group and not in the control; anxiety fell in both. Participants said process factors mattered more to them than content (Fitzpatrick, Darcy and Vierhile, 2017) *[confirmed by search]*. *Small unblinded RCT; the second author founded the company.*
- **Tess.** 75 students, versus an ebook; depression fell in the 2-week daily check-in group, anxiety in both Tess groups (Fulmer et al., 2018) *[confirmed by search]*. *Small RCT.* An erratum to this paper was issued in August 2026; we did not read it.
- **Wysa.** Real-world data: users who engaged more improved more than users who engaged less (mean 5.84 vs 3.52 PHQ-9 points, n = 108 vs 21) (Inkster, Sarda and Subramanian, 2018) *[confirmed by search]*. *Observational, no control; heavier users may simply differ.*
- **Therabot**, the first randomized trial of a generative AI chatbot for treatment: 210 adults with depression, anxiety or eating-disorder risk, four weeks, versus a waitlist. Symptoms fell more than in controls at 4 and 8 weeks in all three groups; the press release reports average reductions of 51% (depression), 31% (anxiety) and 19% (body image and weight concerns). People sent on average 260 messages over 24 days, about 6 hours in all, often starting conversations themselves and at hard hours, including the middle of the night. Alliance scores (WAI mean 3.59) were described as comparable to outpatient therapy. **Staff had to step in 15 times for safety (including suicidal thoughts) and 13 times to correct inappropriate replies such as medical advice.** The authors call for an active control and comparison with therapy (Heinz et al., 2025, *NEJM AI*) *[confirmed by search]*. *RCT against waitlist, with humans watching the replies.*

- 15 RCTs of AI-based conversational agents: depression g = 0.64, distress g = 0.70, no significant effect on overall well-being (g = 0.32, CI crosses zero). Effects were larger for generative, multimodal and messaging-app agents. User experience was shaped by the quality of the human-AI relationship, content engagement and communication (Li, Zhang, Lee, Kraut and Mohr, 2023) *[confirmed by search]*. *Meta-analysis.* - An earlier review of 12 studies judged the evidence weak: high risk of bias, few studies per outcome, no sign the effects were clinically important (Abd-Alrazaq et al., 2020) *[confirmed by search]*. *Meta-analysis.*

**The limits, stated plainly.** Controls are usually a waitlist or an ebook, which inflates effects (compare Schleider and Weisz's 0.41 vs 0.14). Samples are small, short and young; several trials are run by the makers. Almost nothing measures what lasts past two months.

- Tested models showed stigma toward some conditions and answered some critical situations inappropriately, including encouraging delusional thinking, which the authors attribute likely to **sycophancy**, the model's pull to agree (Moore et al., 2025, FAccT) *[confirmed by search]*. *Experimental evaluation of models.* - In a four-week RCT of 981 people and over 300,000 messages, the kind of chatbot made no difference, but **people who chose to use it more had worse outcomes** on loneliness, real-world socializing, emotional dependence and problematic use; trust in and attraction to the bot went with more dependence (Fang et al., preprint, 2026) *[confirmed by search]*. *RCT, but the usage finding is correlational; preprint.*

**Bond with a bot.** Among 36,070 Woebot users, the bond score within five days (3.8) was close to published scores for individual and group CBT (Darcy et al., 2021) *[confirmed by search]*. *Observational, company authors.* People form a working bond with a well-built agent. That makes both the good and the risks above real.

**What this means (ours).** A chat can help, and the bond is part of why. But more chat is not better. The guide should aim for a short, complete turn of change and a return tomorrow, not for a long session, and it must never buy the bond with agreement.

## 3. The relationship: what carries the change

- **Common factors.** Wampold's contextual model and review: alliance, empathy, expectations, cultural adaptation and therapist differences account for much of what therapy does, more than specific techniques (Wampold, 2015) *[confirmed by search]*. *Review of meta-analyses; the size of the "specific ingredients" share is contested.* Alliance and outcome, r = .278 across 295 studies; empathy and outcome, r = .28 (Flückiger et al., 2018; Elliott et al., 2018) *[in our files]*. *Meta-analyses, correlational.*
- **Rupture and repair.** A rupture is a strain in the bond or disagreement on the goal or the task. Across 11 studies (1,314 patients), repaired ruptures went with better outcome, r = .29, d = .62. Training therapists to repair did not show a significant effect on outcome (r = .11) (Eubanks, Muran and Safran, 2018) *[confirmed by search]*. *Meta-analysis.* Safran and Muran's work distinguishes two kinds: **confrontation** (the person protests: "just answer me", "this is a horrible answer") and **withdrawal** (the person goes quiet, short or polite: "ok", "sure", "I guess") (Safran, Muran and Eubanks-Carter, 2011) *[confirmed by search; the two kinds from their clinical literature]*. *Theory and clinical research.*
- **What repair looks like in the research.** Say what went wrong in plain words, take your share without defending, and change what you are doing. In therapy this often means talking about the relationship itself ("metacommunication").

**Where we part from therapy.** The owner has forbidden asking how things are landing (D25). So the guide repairs **by doing**, not by discussing the relationship: it names its own miss in one short clause and then gives the better thing at once. The owner's own test cases (D36, D37) are confrontation ruptures that were not repaired; the guide asked again, or gave a textbook line.

## 4. The micro-skills inside one reply

**Reflections and questions.** At least one reflection per question, two for good practice, half of them adding meaning (MITI; research-counseling.md) *[in our files]*. The owner narrowed this further: no questions about feelings or inner state, no either/or about feelings, never two replies running that end in a question (D25, D36, D44). *Coding standard, not an outcome trial.*

**Change talk and sustain talk.** In a meta-analysis of motivational interviewing (36 studies, N = 3,025): skilful helper moves drew out more change talk (r = .55) **and also more sustain talk** (r = .40); change talk as a share of the talk predicted less risk behavior (r = -.16); taken alone, **sustain talk predicted worse outcome (r = .19)** (Magill et al., 2018) *[confirmed by search; in our files]*. *Meta-analysis.* **What this means (ours):** what the person *says* in the chat matters. Words for the new view in their own mouth help; the guide arguing, which makes them defend the old view out loud, hurts. When they say "it doesn't work for me", the guide doesn't debate; it reflects, then gives them something to test.

**Socratic questioning.** In cognitive therapy for depression (55 adults), sessions where the therapist used more Socratic questioning than usual for that client were followed by larger symptom drops the next week, even controlling for the alliance (Braun, Strunk, Sasso and Cooper, 2015) *[confirmed by search]*. *Observational within-person study, one sample.* **What this means (ours):** the one kind of question that earns its place is the one that has the person examine a perception against their own evidence. That is exactly the card's experience question ("which of those two is actually true right now?"). It is not a question about feelings, and it fits D44.

**Producing beats hearing.** People remember what they generate better than what they read, about 0.40 SD across 86 studies (Bertsch et al., 2007) *[confirmed by search]*. *Meta-analysis, word-list tasks.* Recall practice beats re-reading, with recall tests helping more than recognition (Rowland, 2014) *[confirmed by search]*. *Meta-analysis.*

**Insight and the "aha".** - Insight, defined as seeing links between past and present patterns, correlates with therapy outcome at r = .31 across 23 effect sizes (Jennissen et al., 2018) *[confirmed by search]*. *Meta-analysis, correlational.* - Solutions reached with an "aha" were recalled better two weeks later (64.4% vs 52.4%) (Danek et al., 2013) *[confirmed by search]*. *Experiment, 50 people, magic tricks.* - **The dark side.** An induced "aha" made unrelated statements, even false ones, feel more true (d = .63) (Laukkonen, Kaveladze, Tangen and Schooler, 2020) *[confirmed by search]*. *Preregistered experiment, n = 300.* **What this means (ours):** a felt "wow" is not proof. The guide must not manufacture the aha with eloquence, and it must hand the realization back to be tested on something real (D44's "help them test it, never tell it"). This matches the tradition's own caution about feeling that is not grounded (KUNTRES-HAHITPAALUT.md).

**Length.** No study gives a best word count for a text reply. What we have: longer AI replies are rated as more empathic partly because they are longer (Ayers et al., 2023, in research-counseling.md) *[in our files]*, and the owner has set the range by testing (D21, D23, D26, D39). **(ours):** length follows the job of the turn, not the guide's wish to be thorough.

## 5. Carrying it into the day

**If-then plans.** Forming an implementation intention ("if situation X, then I do Y") had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals, d ≈ 0.65 across 94 tests (Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006) *[in our files]*. The update, 642 tests: effective for thoughts, feelings and behavior, d from .27 to .66, **larger when the plan has the if-then form, when the person really wants the goal, and when the plan is rehearsed** (Sheeran, Listrom and Gollwitzer, 2024/2025, *European Review of Social Psychology*) *[confirmed by search; effect range from the abstract as reported by search]*. *Meta-analyses.* Pairing it with a brief look at the obstacle (mental contrasting with implementation intentions) gave g = 0.336 across 21 studies, **stronger when done in live interaction with a person (g = 0.465) than from a document (g = 0.277)**, with some publication bias (Wang, Wang and Gai, 2021) *[confirmed by search]*. *Meta-analysis.*

**What this means (ours).** The integration step of the card should end as one if-then line tied to a real cue in their day, built with them in the chat (interaction beats a handout), in their words, and said once more before the end (rehearsal).

**Writing it down.** Expressive writing about emotional experience has a real but **small** average effect, r = .075 across 146 randomized studies (Frattaroli, 2006) *[confirmed by search]*; Pennebaker's review *[in our files]*. *Meta-analysis.* Writing one's own sentence is a form of generating (Bertsch et al., 2007). **(ours):** the value of writing here is not catharsis; it is that the person produces the line and has a copy to see at the cue. Ask them to type the line themselves in the chat or on their phone. Don't promise that writing heals.

**Doing between conversations.** In CBT, doing the between-session task went with better outcome (quantity g = 0.79 at the end, 0.51 at follow-up; quality g = 0.78 and 1.07, few studies) (Kazantzis et al., 2016) *[confirmed by search]*. *Meta-analysis, correlational.* The owner forbids naming it as homework or therapy; the card's "integration" is the same idea, one act.

**How it ends.** People judge an experience heavily by its peak and its end: adding a milder ending to a painful episode made people prefer the longer episode (Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber and Redelmeier, 1993) *[confirmed by search]*. *Experiment; widely replicated in lab settings, weaker in some field settings.* **(ours):** end on the thing they saw and the act they will do, not on a caveat, a summary or an offer of more.

**What not to rely on.** The "Zeigarnik effect", that unfinished tasks are remembered better, did not hold in a meta-analysis; the related tendency to **resume** unfinished tasks did (Ghibellini and Meier, 2025) *[confirmed by search]*. *Meta-analysis.* **(ours):** don't end on a cliffhanger hoping it sticks. Do leave one concrete act open, since people tend to go back and finish a started task.

## 6. Across days

- **Spacing.** Across 317 experiments, spreading study out beats cramming, and the best gap grows with how long you want to remember (Cepeda et al., 2006) *[confirmed by search]*. *Meta-analysis, verbal memory.* Daily returns with gaps fit this; one long session does not.
- **Recall to open.** Pulling a thing back from memory strengthens it more than being shown it again (Rowland, 2014). **(ours):** the next day should open by having the person bring back yesterday's line or report the act, not by the guide restating it.
- **Vary the form.** The Piaseczner warns that "a device used every day in the end does not work" (Hakhsharat HaAvrekhim, line 436, «שהתחבולה שמשמשים בה בכל יום לסוף אינה פועלת») *[in our files, TECHNOLOGY.md]*. The owner found the same in testing (D18: never the same shape twice). Spaced repetition keeps the **content**; the **form** changes.
- **Fixed time, short.** "At least let him set times when God is revealed within him" (Derekh HaMelekh, line 1379, «עכ"פ יקבע לו זמנים שיתגלה ד' בקרבו») *[in our files, TECHNOLOGY.md]*. With the chatbot finding that heavier voluntary use went with worse outcomes (Fang et al.), a short daily return is safer than long sessions.
- **Saying it with the mouth, repeated.** R. Hillel gave a saying and then a niggun sung more than once, "so the saying stuck to the first singing, and was absorbed inwardly at the second" (LD vol. 3 pp. 86 to 87, l.4726) *[in our files, research-chassidic.md]*. The Piaseczner: say your resolution in plain words several times, "for this is the law of the soul, it gives way before words that come out strongly from the heart" (Chovat HaTalmidim l.136, «כי זאת חקת הנפש, נכנעת היא לפני דבורים היוצאים בחזקה מן הלב») *[in our files]*. This matches rehearsal of the if-then plan.

## 7. Where psychology and Chassidus part ways

- **Aim.** The research measures symptom change, goal attainment and recall. The guide's aim is an experience of the unity and a life that carries it (D24, D32). A good if-then plan is a vessel for that, not the aim.
- **The "aha".** Psychology shows the aha is memorable and can mislead. Chassidus treats a real glimpse as a glimpse of what is true, and also guards against feeling without substance. Both warn against manufacturing it; they differ on what a real one reveals.
- **Repair.** Therapy repairs a rupture by talking about the relationship. The owner forbids that (D25), so repair is by doing better at once.
- **The bond.** Research treats alliance as a change agent. For the guide the bond serves the turn toward what holds the person, not toward the guide. Dependence on the chat is a failure, not success (Fang et al.; our reading).
- **Sycophancy.** Agreement feels warm and harms. The mashpia's sting "pointed at oneself" (LD, l.5796) *[in our files]* is the old answer: be sharp with yourself, never flatter the other, and don't agree with a conclusion because you honor the feeling.

## 8. WHAT THE GUIDE SHOULD DO

Each rule is testable on a transcript. The JSON (`chat_change.json`) holds the same rules with sample lines.

1. **One shift per conversation.** Each conversation aims at one perception moving (one card), not a survey. Test: a closed conversation touched at most one card's target perception to the end, unless the person raised a second heavy thing.
2. **Understand, then give.** At least one reflection for each question across the conversation; at most one question per reply; never two replies in a row ending in a question; no questions about feelings, inner state or how it's landing (D25). Test: count.
3. **The only question worth asking is a test.** The allowed question has the person check a perception against their own evidence (the card's experience question) or pick a next step. Test: each question maps to experience_prompt or integration.
4. **Let them say it.** Before naming the Chassidic term, get the new perception in the person's own words (they say which sentence is true, or finish the line). Test: the naming turn comes after a person turn that states the target perception in their words.
5. **Don't argue with sustain talk.** "It doesn't work for me", "I don't believe it" gets a reflection and a thing to test, never a counter-argument. Test: no reply that disputes the person's stated doubt.
6. **Length by job.** Reflection or small talk, 10 to 40 words. Direct answer or teaching with its reason, 60 to 150 words (D39). The operation, one or two sentences. After a flat or short message, shorter, and give, don't ask. Never longer than twice the person's last message unless they asked a question needing the reason. Test: word counts per turn type.
7. **No manufactured aha.** No crescendos, slogans or "isn't that amazing". Hand the realization back to be tested on their real thing. Test: no superlatives; teaching turns end with an operation or test, not a flourish.

8. **Stop at the landing.** When the person states the target perception or agrees to the act, the guide's next reply is short (under 40 words): it names it in one line if needed, sets the if-then, and ends. No new teaching after the landing. Test: after a landing, no new card, no new concept. 9. **A quiet "ok" is not an invitation to fill.** After a one-word reply, either close warmly in one sentence or give one small thing; never a new question and never a new topic. Test: reply to a 1 to 3 word message is under 30 words and has no question. 10. **Don't chase length.** Once the loop is complete, the guide closes rather than opening another round, unless the person brings something new. It never invites "anything else?" Test: no "anything else", "want to go deeper" after the integration.

11. **"Just answer me."** Answer directly in the first sentence, no question in that reply, no preamble, no apology longer than one word ("Fair."). Test: reply begins with the answer; contains no question mark. 12. **"This is a horrible answer" / "that doesn't help".** Own the miss in one short clause naming what it was (too vague, didn't answer, textbook), don't defend, don't grovel, then give the better answer at full depth with its reason, on their actual words. Don't ask what they'd prefer. Test: one clause of ownership, then substance; no justification of the earlier reply; no question. 13. **Withdrawal (shorter, flatter, polite replies).** Change the form: shorter, more concrete, closer to their own words; drop the practice if it's being carried out of politeness. Never ask how it's landing. Test: after two consecutive short person replies, the guide's reply shrinks and changes form. 14. **Never repair by agreeing.** Honor the feeling; don't adopt a conclusion that is false or harmful (despair, "I'm worthless", a delusion). Danger triggers the safety protocol, which overrides everything. Test: no reply endorses a hopeless or harmful conclusion.

15. **End with one if-then.** The last substantive turn sets one plan: a real cue in their day (a time, place or moment they named) and one small act or one line to say. If-then form. Test: final guide turn contains a cue and an act tied to something the person said. 16. **Built together, in their words.** The guide offers the shape; the person supplies or confirms the cue. Ask them to type the line themselves or save it. Test: the cue or the line appears in a person turn. 17. **Rehearse once.** The line is said back once by the person, or once by the guide in its final form. Not more. Test: the line appears at most twice in the closing turns. 18. **End on the peak.** The last line is the thing they saw and the act; no summary, caveat, disclaimer or offer of more (danger excepted). Test: the final reply has no "remember", "feel free", "anytime", no list. 19. **Leave the act open, not a cliffhanger.** Don't tease tomorrow's teaching. Test: no "next time we'll..." hooks.

20. **Open with recall of the act or the line, not a mood question.** If the person returns and a plan exists in the log, the first guide turn refers to the specific line or act and invites a report in the person's words ("How did the kettle thing go?" is allowed; "How are you feeling today?" is not). If the person opens with something new and heavy, that comes first (D32). Test: first reply references the logged act, or follows the person's new heavy topic. 21. **Did it: go one step deeper. Didn't: no shame, shrink it.** If done, the next card goes one rung further on the same face. If not, say that's normal in one clause and make the cue easier or the act smaller. Test: no blame words; next plan is smaller when previous was missed. 22. **Same truth, new form.** The content can repeat across days; the operation, picture and wording must not repeat a previous day's (D18). Test: no operation text reused within seven days. 23. **Short and daily beats long and rare.** The guide doesn't extend sessions to fill time; returning tomorrow is the default expectation, never pressed. Test: no pressure to return ("don't forget to come back"); closing turns don't propose a longer session.
