## Read this first

**The teaching, in one line.** Everything, you included, is being given its being by G-d right now. You are not the one holding yourself or your world up (FRAMEWORK U1; the Baal Shem Tov's reading in Shaar HaYichud VehaEmunah 1, quoted there as Q01).

**The trouble it answers.** FRAMEWORK section 2 names it: nearly everyone lives inside the picture "I am a separate thing, and I hold myself and my world up." This file asks what psychology has measured about that picture: where it comes from, what it costs, and what changes in a person who stops living inside it.

**What this file is.** A map for the builders. Each entry has five parts:

1. **The idea**, in plain English.
2. **The research**: who, what they found, and how strong it is.
3. **What it explains** about "I'm holding everything up", and what changes when a person stops.
4. **The bridge** to the teaching.
5. **Where the bridge stops.**

**What it is not.** - It is not the guide's voice. None of these terms should reach a person as vocabulary. - It is not advice to anyone. There are no instructions here for the reader's breath, body or attention, and no referrals. - It is not proof. Psychology measures what people feel, believe and do. It cannot test whether anything is being given being right now. The teaching stands or falls on its own ground. Psychology can show the felt effect of holding it, and where the felt effect is easy to confuse with something else.

**Marks.** - **Ours** marks our own synthesis or inference. Everything not marked ours is the researchers' finding or claim, as far as we could check it. - **[in our files]** means the reference was already checked and listed in `part-a.md`, `part-b.md` or `SCIENCE.md`. - **[confirmed by search]** means we confirmed it on 2026-09-30: author, year, title and journal against Crossref, plus the finding against the abstract (from PubMed or Semantic Scholar) or, for books and papers with no abstract online, against the publisher's page or a reliable summary. Where only the citation could be confirmed and not the details, the entry says so.

**Strength labels** (the same as part-a): *meta-analysis*, *registered replication*, *RCT*, *experiment*, *imaging study*, *correlational*, *review*, *theory*, *clinical theory* (a clinician's model built from practice, not from measurement), *pilot*, *replication problem*.

**The honest gap.** We found no study of the Chassidic teaching of being held as such, and no study of Chabad contemplation. The closest measured work is Rosmarin's research on trust in G-d among religious Jews (entries 5 and 11). Everything else is evidence by analogy.

## The picture in one page *(ours)*

Read across the sixteen entries below, psychology gives a fairly clear portrait of the person who is "holding everything up", though no single study draws it:

- They learned early, or late, that nobody reliably held them, so they became their own only source of safety (entries 1 and 3). - They overestimate how much rides on their control, and they carry that load as a job that never ends (entries 4 and 6). - They worry, because worry feels like doing something about the future (entry 5). - They fight their own inner states, and the fight keeps the states alive (entry 7). - They meet their failures with harshness, because the whole structure depends on them not failing (entry 8). - Their minds loop back to the self and its problems (entry 14).

And it gives a fairly clear portrait of what changes when the load comes off:

- Felt security makes people go out and act more, not less (entry 1). - Accepting what cannot be controlled goes with less distress, and it frees effort for what can be (entries 6, 7 and 13). - Kindness toward one's own failure goes with more effort to repair, not less (entry 8). - Trust in a good G-d goes with less worry, and mistrust with more (entries 5 and 11).

The teaching says something psychology cannot say: that the load was never yours, because you are not the one doing the being. Psychology can say what it looks like, from the inside and from the outside, when a person lives as if that were true.

<a id="e1"></a> ## 1. Attachment and felt security

**The idea.** A small child plays and explores when a trusted adult is nearby, and runs back to that adult when frightened. John Bowlby called the adult a **secure base** (the place you go out from) and a **safe haven** (the place you run back to). Adults keep the same system all their lives. **Felt security** is the inner sense that someone reliable has you, whether or not they are in the room.

**The research.** - Bowlby built the theory (Bowlby, 1988, *A Secure Base*) [confirmed by search]. Mary Ainsworth's home observations and the "Strange Situation" gave it its first measured patterns: secure children used the mother as a base to explore from and were comforted by her return (Ainsworth, Blehar, Waters and Wall, 1978, *Patterns of Attachment*) [confirmed by search]. *Theory; observational studies.* - Mikulincer, Shaver and Pereg (2003) describe two insecure strategies adults use when they cannot count on being held. One is **hyperactivation**: staying on constant alert for threat, separation and betrayal. The other is **deactivation**: denying the need for others, keeping distance, and relying compulsively on oneself as the only dependable source of safety and comfort [confirmed by search]. *Review and theory, built on many studies.* - Raising a person's felt security, even briefly and in the lab, is followed by better mood, more compassion and more tolerance of others (Mikulincer and Shaver, 2007) [in our files]. *Review of experiments.* - Security frees people to go out. In a study of 167 married couples, a partner who was available, did not interfere, and encouraged strongly predicted how fully the other partner took on a new challenge (Feeney and Thrush, 2010) [confirmed by search]. *Observational study with coded behaviour.* - Being held changes how the brain meets threat. Sixteen married women facing the threat of a mild shock showed less activity in threat-response brain systems while holding their husband's hand, less so while holding a stranger's hand, and the better the marriage, the bigger the effect (Coan, Schaefer and Davidson, 2006) [confirmed by search]. Women looking at a photo of their partner while in pain rated the pain lower and showed more activity in a brain region linked to safety signals (Eisenberger et al., 2011) [confirmed by search]. *Small imaging studies.* - **Social baseline theory** proposes that the human brain *expects* to have others who share risk and lower the effort of meeting goals. Without them, more effort is needed, and a person who has to define the self as independent faces more risk and more effort (Coan and Sbarra, 2015) [confirmed by search]. *Theory, built on the studies above.*

**What it explains.** "I'm holding everything up" is, in attachment terms, very close to **deactivation**: the stance of someone who learned that holding would not reliably come, and so became their own only holder. Social baseline theory adds the cost. A mind that assumes it carries everything alone is running on the expensive setting all the time. That fits the tiredness people describe.

What changes when a person stops is not what they might fear. The secure-base research says the opposite of passivity: people who feel held go out, try things and take on challenges *more*. Security is the condition for exploring, not a replacement for it. *(Ours:)* this matches FRAMEWORK U1's "after": "You still plan and still work hard, as someone being given the next moment rather than someone who has to manufacture it."

**The bridge.** Part-a (step 4) already found this the strongest felt parallel in the ladder: "held, not holding" is very close to felt security, and a G-d who surrounds and fills is, psychologically, a safe haven that goes everywhere. The teaching says the holding is total and constant: if it stopped, you would not fall, you would not be (FRAMEWORK, "Fear").

**Where the bridge stops.** - Attachment research is about a *relationship* with a figure beside you who may or may not come. The teaching is about the *being* of the thing, you included. The Tanya refuses even the picture of G-d in the world like a soul in a body, because a soul and a body are two things at their root (FRAMEWORK Q67). Attachment describes how being held *feels*. It does not describe what the teaching says *is*. - Felt security goes up and down, and in the lab it can be switched on by a word on a screen. The teaching's claim does not depend on anyone feeling it. - Secure attachment is earned through years of good care. The teaching says being held is already true of everyone, including those whose care failed. Psychology can only say how that claim will *land* for someone whose care failed (entry 2).

**The idea.** Many believers relate to G-d the way a child relates to a trusted parent: as someone to run to in distress and to go out from with confidence. Psychologists of religion have studied this as an attachment bond, with the same secure and insecure patterns found between people.

**The research.** - Lee Kirkpatrick proposed treating G-d as an attachment figure (Kirkpatrick, 1992; Kirkpatrick and Shaver, 1990; the full account in Kirkpatrick, 2005, *Attachment, Evolution, and the Psychology of Religion*) [confirmed by search]. He set out two ideas that still frame the field. **Correspondence**: people who were securely held by their parents tend to picture G-d as a secure holder. **Compensation**: people who were not may find in G-d the reliable holder they lacked. *Theory.* - A meta-analysis of 11 studies with 1,465 people found support for both: sudden religious conversions went with insecure childhood attachment, and gradual religious change with secure attachment, with small to medium effects (Granqvist and Kirkpatrick, 2004) [confirmed by search]. *Meta-analysis; but all the studies relied on adults' memories of childhood.* - A later review concluded that a believer's felt relationship with G-d meets the criteria of an attachment bond, and that the compensating route can build "earned security" (Granqvist, Mikulincer and Shaver, 2010) [in our files]. Among Israeli Jews, threat made G-d-related words come to mind faster, and "G-d" made secure-base words come to mind faster (Granqvist, Mikulincer, Gewirtz and Shaver, 2012) [in our files]. *Review; priming experiments, a method with broad replication troubles, so treat as suggestive.* - Attachment to G-d has two insecure dimensions: **anxious** (fear of being abandoned by G-d) and **avoidant** (keeping G-d at a distance), and they relate to mood and personality (Rowatt and Kirkpatrick, 2002; measured by Beck and McDonald, 2004) [in our files]. *Correlational; measure validation.* - Granqvist (2020, *Attachment in Religion and Spirituality*) gathers the field [confirmed by search]. *Book-length review.*

**What it explains.** People will hear "you are being held by G-d" through the picture of holding they already carry. Someone who was held well will find it natural. Someone who had to hold themselves up may hear it in one of two ways: as the first reliable holding they have met (the compensation route), or as one more promise from someone who was not there (the avoidant pattern). For someone anxious toward G-d, "He surrounds you from every side" can feel like being watched. Part-a (step 4) already flagged this.

What changes when the picture shifts from anxious or avoidant to secure: the review evidence says the religious bond, like the human one, can become a real safe haven and a base to go out from.

**The bridge.** This is the most direct research on "being held by G-d" that exists. It shows that the felt sense of being held by G-d is real as a psychological bond, that it does what human holding does, and that it can be earned later in life by people who did not get it early.

**Where the bridge stops.** - Attachment research studies a G-d *representation*: the picture of G-d in a person's mind. It cannot say whether Someone is there, and it is careful not to. The teaching is not a claim about a picture; it is a claim about what is holding the picture and the person who has it. - The attachment model is a relationship between two: me, and a figure who holds me. The teaching goes further than that. From His side, nothing stands apart as a second thing at all (FRAMEWORK U4; part-a step 7). No attachment construct holds that, and none should be stretched to. - Compensation theory is sometimes read as "people believe because they need to". That describes a motive in some believers. It says nothing about whether the belief is true, in either direction.

<a id="e3"></a> ## 3. The holding environment (Winnicott)

**The idea.** The English word "held" comes into psychology through the pediatrician and psychoanalyst Donald Winnicott. Before a baby can hold itself together, it is held: in arms, and in a whole environment that is steady enough that the baby does not have to notice it. He called this the **holding environment**. When holding is good enough, the child does not have to become its own caretaker too soon.

**The research.** - Winnicott (1960, "The theory of the parent-infant relationship") set out **holding** as the first phase of care: the environment that holds the infant, physically and in every other sense, while the infant cannot yet hold itself [confirmed by search]. *Clinical theory.* - Winnicott (1958, "The capacity to be alone") argued that being able to be alone is one of the most important signs of emotional maturity, and that it grows from a paradox: the child's experience of being alone *while someone trusted is present* [confirmed by search]. *Clinical theory.* - Winnicott (1960, "Ego distortion in terms of true and false self", collected in *The Maturational Processes and the Facilitating Environment*, 1965) described a **false self**: when the environment fails, the child builds a compliant self that looks after things and meets demands, a self-caretaking defence that hides and protects the spontaneous true self [confirmed by search; the paper's details are from reliable summaries, not the full text]. *Clinical theory.* - **Strength.** These are a clinician's models, drawn from practice, not from measurement. They are very influential and were a direct source for later attachment research, but they were not tested in controlled studies the way entries 1 and 2 were.

**What it explains.** Winnicott gives the most vivid picture of the "holding it all up" self: a self that took over the job of caretaking because nobody else did it reliably, and then could not put it down. Its job was protective, and it worked. The trouble is that it never ends, and the person underneath does not get to live.

What changes, in Winnicott's frame, is not that the caretaker self is attacked or torn down. It is that a holding reliable enough appears, and the true self can risk coming out. The capacity to be alone, in his account, grows *out of* being held, not out of self-sufficiency. *(Ours:)* this is close to FRAMEWORK U1's "held instead of holding up": the relief comes from a holding that was there all along, not from a new effort.

**The bridge.** *(Ours.)* Winnicott's "capacity to be alone" gives a precise psychological shape for what the teaching offers a person who is alone: the holding is present when no one else is. His model says a person can be alone without being abandoned only if something holds them in the background. The teaching names what holds them in the background: the One who gives them being right now, "You surround me and fill my body and my spirit" (FRAMEWORK Q03).

**Where the bridge stops.** - Winnicott's holding is a mother, and later an inner memory of her. It is a story about development and the past. The teaching's holding is not a memory taken in; it is happening now, to everyone, whatever their childhood was. - Winnicott's "true self" means spontaneous aliveness. The Chassidic true self is the soul as "a part of G-d above" (part-a step 2). The two share a name and not a meaning. - A clinical model is a way of seeing, not evidence. We use it for its picture, not as proof of anything.

<a id="e4"></a> ## 4. Perceived control and over-control

**The idea.** **Perceived control** is the sense that what you do affects what happens to you. Having some is good for people. But people also overestimate it, and the need for it can grow past what any person can carry. **Over-control** is when the effort to control becomes the problem.

**The research.** - A major review laid out both sides: perceived control is linked to better mental and physical health, but there are also mismatches between how much control people seek and how much they have, and real costs, personal and social, of seeking and holding control (Shapiro, Schwartz and Astin, 1996) [confirmed by search]. *Review.* - People act as if they can control pure chance. In Langer's experiments, cues that belong to skill situations (such as choosing your own lottery ticket) made people behave as if they had control over chance outcomes (Langer, 1975) [confirmed by search; details from the classic account, not the full text]. *Experiments.* - Healthy minds lean toward too much sense of control. A meta-analysis of 75 studies (7,305 people) found that both depressed and non-depressed people showed a positive bias in judging their control and similar things, and the bias was *larger* in the non-depressed; the old claim that depressed people see more accurately held up only as a very small effect (Moore and Fresco, 2012) [confirmed by search]. *Meta-analysis.* - More control is not always welcome. Burger (1989) reviewed and tested conditions under which people react badly to an increase in their own control [confirmed by search; citation only, the abstract was not available]. *Experiments.* - Does losing control push people toward G-d? Kay and colleagues found in three experiments that lowering people's sense of personal control increased belief in a controlling G-d (Kay, Gaucher, Napier, Callan and Laurin, 2008) [confirmed by search]. But a large registered replication in the United States and the Netherlands found moderate to strong evidence of *no* such experimental effect, while people who in general felt less in control did believe more in a controlling G-d, in the United States only (Hoogeveen, Wagenmakers, Kay and van Elk, 2018) [confirmed by search]. *Experiments; registered replication; replication problem for the experimental claim.*

**What it explains.** "I'm holding everything up" is partly an **overestimate of control** carried as a **duty**. The ordinary mind already overestimates its control (Moore and Fresco). In calm times that bias is harmless, even pleasant. Under pressure it turns into a job with no edges: if it depends on me, then every bad outcome is my failure, and every future outcome is mine to prevent.

What changes when a person stops is not that they lose control they really had. It is that they stop carrying the control they never had. The review literature treats this sorting (what is mine to do, what is not) as the heart of healthy control (entry 6).

**The bridge.** FRAMEWORK's "Control" theme says exactly this sorting in the teaching's terms: "Your deeds matter; the home is built by them (Q66). And the being of the outcome, and of you, is not yours to make (Q01). You do your part with full strength and stop carrying the part that was never yours." Psychology has found the costs of carrying the second part.

**Where the bridge stops.** - Psychology sorts control into what is controllable and what is not, *for this person, in this situation*. The teaching's line runs somewhere else: even what you do control, you do not give being to. The deed is yours; the being of the deed, and of you doing it, is not. - Compensatory control theory reads belief in G-d as a patch for lost control. The key experiment did not replicate. And even where the link holds, a motive for believing tells us nothing about whether the belief is true.

<a id="e5"></a> ## 5. Intolerance of uncertainty, and worry as an attempt to hold the future

**The idea.** **Worry** is a chain of anxious thoughts about what might go wrong. **Intolerance of uncertainty** is finding it hard to bear not knowing how things will turn out. The two feed each other. Worry feels like doing something: as if, by going over the danger again, a person is keeping it from happening. In the terms of this project, worry is an attempt to hold the future up.

**The research.** - Borkovec and colleagues began the modern study of worry, describing it as a mostly verbal, hard to stop chain of negative thought about uncertain future outcomes (Borkovec, Robinson, Pruzinsky and DePree, 1983) [confirmed by search; citation only, no abstract online; description from the standard account]. They later reviewed the **positive beliefs** that keep worry going, such as the belief that worrying helps prevent bad outcomes or prepares a person for them (Borkovec, Hazlett-Stevens and Diaz, 1999) [confirmed by search; same caveat]. *Early experiments; review.* - Intolerance of uncertainty sits at the center. Freeston and colleagues studied why people worry and pointed to intolerance of uncertainty and to beliefs about worry (Freeston, Rhéaume, Letarte, Dugas and Ladouceur, 1994) [confirmed by search; citation only]. In a small test of the resulting model (24 people with generalized anxiety, 20 without), intolerance of uncertainty was the pivotal factor that separated the two groups, and the model's four factors classified 82% of people correctly (Dugas, Gagnon, Ladouceur and Freeston, 1998) [confirmed by search]. *Small clinical comparison.* - A review argues that fear of the unknown may be a, or even *the*, fundamental fear under anxiety (Carleton, 2016) [confirmed by search]. *Review; the authors call it provocative.* - The **contrast avoidance model** proposes that chronic worriers use worry to *stay* in a negative state, so that a sudden bad turn will not hit them from a calm place (Newman and Llera, 2011) [confirmed by search]. *Theory and review.* - Most worries do not come true. Twenty-nine people with generalized anxiety logged their worries and later checked the outcomes: 91.4% of the worried predictions did not happen, and people who saw more of their worries fail to come true improved more in treatment (LaFreniere and Newman, 2020) [confirmed by search]. *Small treatment study.* - One theory holds that the body's stress response is always "on" by default, and is held back only when safety is perceived; in this view intolerance of uncertainty is not learned but is where every organism starts, and it eases only where safety has been learned (Brosschot, Verkuil and Thayer, 2016) [confirmed by search]. *Theory.* - **The most direct bridge in the literature.** Rosmarin and colleagues proposed and tested, in two studies, a model in which a person's beliefs about G-d affect worry *through* intolerance of uncertainty; the support was strongest for *negative* beliefs about G-d (Rosmarin, Pirutinsky, Auerbach, Björgvinsson, Bigda-Peyton, Andersson, Pargament and Krumrei, 2011) [confirmed by search]. In a Jewish community sample of 565, trust in G-d went with less anxiety and depression and more happiness, and mistrust in G-d with the reverse (Rosmarin, Pargament and Mahoney, 2009) [confirmed by search]. A two-week trust-in-G-d program for religious Jews reduced worry and stress against a waitlist (Rosmarin, Pargament, Pirutinsky and Mahoney, 2010) [in our files]. *Correlational with mediation analysis; correlational; small RCT.*

**What it explains.** This is the clearest psychological picture of the 4 a.m. brace FRAMEWORK U1 describes. The worrier is not idle. They are working: running the future over and over, as if the running kept it upright. The positive beliefs about worry are the belief "I am holding it up", stated about the future. Contrast avoidance adds that the brace itself is chosen, half knowingly, to avoid being caught off guard. And LaFreniere and Newman show how little of the work was needed.

What changes when a person stops: the research on intolerance of uncertainty suggests that what helps is not more certainty about outcomes, which no one has, but a different relationship to not knowing. Rosmarin's findings suggest that for believers, what they believe about G-d is part of that relationship, and that a *mistrustful* picture of G-d does more harm than a trusting one does good.

**The bridge.** *(Ours.)* The teaching does not remove uncertainty about outcomes. FRAMEWORK is strict about this: "Being held is about being, not outcomes. Never promise an outcome" (U1, misreadings). What it changes is *what* is uncertain. The outcome stays unknown. Being held does not. FRAMEWORK's "Fear" theme says it: "The fear doesn't have to leave to become smaller than what holds it." In the terms of this research, the teaching offers a floor of certainty under the uncertainty, instead of a guess about the outcome.

**Where the bridge stops.** - In psychology, tolerating uncertainty is the goal, and treatment trains it. The teaching is not a technique for tolerating anything. It is a claim about what is true, and its value for the person does not rest on lowering their worry. - If "being held" is heard as "so it will turn out fine", it becomes one more positive belief about the future, and it will break the first time things do not turn out fine. That is the teaching misheard, and the research shows why it would fail. - Rosmarin's work measures beliefs about G-d. It cannot say which beliefs are true.

<a id="e6"></a> ## 6. Locus of control, and its limits

**The idea.** **Locus of control** is where a person expects the causes of their life to lie: inside (my own actions) or outside (chance, fate, powerful others). Internal locus is usually counted as the healthy one. But the picture is more complicated, and one of the complications is exactly the person who believes everything depends on them.

**The research.** - Rotter (1966) defined locus of control as a general expectation, internal or external, about what controls the rewards in one's life [confirmed by search]. *Theory and measure.* - Belief that G-d controls one's health turned out to be a dimension of its own, not the same as chance or other people, and it was built into a separate scale (Wallston et al., 1999, "Does God determine your health? The God Locus of Health Control Scale") [confirmed by search; citation only]. *Measure validation.* - Rothbaum, Weisz and Snyder (1982) proposed a **two-process model**: people keep a sense of control not only by changing the world to fit their wishes (**primary control**) but also by bringing themselves into line with forces they cannot change (**secondary control**). Among its forms are *vicarious* control, gained by aligning with powerful others, and *interpretive* control, gained by understanding and finding meaning in what cannot be changed [confirmed by search]. *Theory.* Later reviewers revised and defined the concept (Morling and Evered, 2006) [confirmed by search; citation only]. Heckhausen and Schulz (1995) built a life-span theory on the same two kinds of control [confirmed by search; citation only]. *Theory.* - Folkman (1984) argued that coping works best when it fits how controllable the situation really is [confirmed by search; citation only]. A meta-analysis of 329 samples and 58,946 people found that **coping flexibility**, including a good fit between strategy and situation, is linked to better adjustment, with a small to moderate effect, r = .23 (Cheng, Lau and Chan, 2014) [confirmed by search]. *Theory; meta-analysis of correlational studies.*

**What it explains.** *(Ours, from the above.)* "Everything depends on me" is internal locus of control pushed past where it fits: applied to things no one controls. The research on fit says that is where internal locus stops helping. A person who takes the controllable part as theirs does well. A person who takes the uncontrollable part as theirs too is doing a job that cannot be done, and every outcome that goes wrong becomes a personal failure.

What changes when a person stops: they do not flip to "nothing depends on me" (the external pole). The two-process model describes a third option: act fully where action works, and align with what is larger where it does not. The flexibility research says the fit between the two is what matters.

**The bridge.** The teaching gives a clear version of this sorting. FRAMEWORK's "Control" theme: "Your deeds matter... And the being of the outcome, and of you, is not yours to make." Wallston's finding that "G-d controls" is its own dimension, not the same as chance, fits: to the believer, the outside cause is Someone, not luck.

**Where the bridge stops.** - Locus of control asks who *causes* outcomes. The teaching's claim is about who gives *being*, including to the one who acts. A person can have a strong, fitting internal locus for their own deeds and still, in the teaching's terms, not be holding themselves up at all. The two questions sit at different levels. - Secondary control "by aligning with powerful others" is a psychological strategy for keeping a sense of control. The teaching does not ask a person to align with G-d in order to feel in control. It asks them to see what is already so.

<a id="e7"></a> ## 7. Acceptance versus control of inner states (ACT)

**The idea.** A lot of "holding it together" is holding *feelings* down: pushing fear away, not letting sadness in, keeping a lid on. **Acceptance** means letting an inner state be there without fighting it, while still acting on what matters. It is not approval and it is not giving up. **Acceptance and Commitment Therapy** (ACT), developed by Steven Hayes and colleagues, is built on the claim that the fight against inner states is itself a main source of suffering.

**The research.** - Hayes and colleagues named **experiential avoidance**: being unwilling to stay in contact with one's own thoughts, feelings and bodily states, and trying to change or escape them even when that causes harm. They proposed it as a common thread across many problems (Hayes, Wilson, Gifford, Follette and Strosahl, 1996) [confirmed by search]. The therapy is set out in Hayes, Strosahl and Wilson (1999, *Acceptance and Commitment Therapy*) [confirmed by search]. *Theory and review.* - A review of the model and its outcomes found the evidence broadly supportive but not mature, and not enough to say ACT beats other active treatments (Hayes, Luoma, Bond, Masuda and Lillis, 2006) [confirmed by search]. A meta-analysis of 39 randomized trials with 1,821 patients found ACT outperformed control conditions with a moderate effect, g = 0.57 (A-Tjak et al., 2015) [confirmed by search]. *Review; meta-analysis.* - Outside therapy: in community samples of women, a tendency to accept negative emotions went with less negative feeling during a sad film, and, measured before stress arrived, predicted fewer depressive symptoms later (Shallcross, Troy, Boland and Mauss, 2010) [confirmed by search]. In more than 1,000 people, habitual acceptance of one's thoughts and feelings predicted better psychological health even after controlling for related habits, and in the lab (156 people) it predicted a smaller negative reaction to a stressor (Ford, Lam, John and Mauss, 2018) [confirmed by search]. *Correlational, prospective, lab and diary studies.* - Fighting a thought keeps it alive: suppressed thoughts come back (Wegner, Schneider, Carter and White, 1987; Wegner, 1994; meta-analysis in Wang, Hagger and Chatzisarantis, 2020) [in our files]. *Experiments; meta-analysis.*

**What it explains.** The person holding everything up is usually also holding their own inner life in a grip: I must not be afraid, I must not fall apart. The research says the grip costs more than it saves. The fear is made bigger by the fight, and the energy spent on the fight is lost to action.

What changes when a person stops: in the Ford and Shallcross studies, accepting people still *had* negative feelings. What they had less of was the second layer, the reaction to the reaction. That is a precise description of the shift, and it is not a promise that the fear goes away.

**The bridge.** FRAMEWORK U1's "from inside" says: "The worry is still there, but it isn't standing on you." And "Fear": "The fear doesn't have to leave to become smaller than what holds it." The acceptance research gives that sentence a measured shape: the feeling remains, and its grip changes. Part-b (step 12) shows the same match for the Tanya's "act as though you did not hear it", which is turning to a chosen act, not suppressing.

**Where the bridge stops.** - ACT is a set of skills a person practises on their own mind, and it uses mindfulness exercises. This project does not. The teaching is not a way to handle feelings. It is a truth about what holds the person who has them, and the change in the feelings is a side effect. - ACT loosens a person's grip on thoughts ("a thought is just a thought"). Contemplating the teaching goes the other way: it binds the mind *to* a truth until it is as real as something seen (part-a step 6, where this difference is laid out in full). - ACT asks people to choose their own values. In the teaching, the purpose is given: He wants a home here (FRAMEWORK Q32).

**The idea.** **Self-compassion** means meeting your own failures and pain with the kindness you would show a friend, remembering that everyone fails, and seeing the failure clearly without drowning in it. Its opposite is harsh self-judgment. Kristin Neff defined and measured it.

**The research.** - Neff (2003) defined self-compassion by three pairs: self-kindness against self-judgment, a sense of common humanity against isolation, and balanced awareness against over-identifying with one's pain [confirmed by search], and built the scale to measure it (Neff, 2003, *Self and Identity* 2(3)) [in our files]. *Theory; measure validation.* - Self-compassion is strongly linked to less psychological distress: a meta-analysis found a large inverse correlation, about r = -0.54 (MacBeth and Gumley, 2012) [in our files]. *Meta-analysis of correlational studies; says nothing about cause.* - In five studies, self-compassion softened people's reactions to bad events, and when it was induced, it led people to own their part in a negative event *without* being overwhelmed by it (Leary, Tate, Adams, Allen and Hancock, 2007) [confirmed by search]. *Experiments and correlational studies.* - Kindness toward oneself after failure *increased* the drive to improve: more motivation to make amends after a wrong, and more time studying after failing a test, compared with a self-esteem boost or no intervention (Breines and Chen, 2012) [in our files]. *Four experiments.* - A meta-analysis of 27 randomized trials of self-compassion programs found moderate improvements in stress, depression, anxiety and self-criticism, and a large one in rumination (g = 1.37) (Ferrari et al., 2019) [confirmed by search]. *Meta-analysis; we did not read its quality ratings.* - A caution already in our files: repeating "I'm a lovable person" made people with low self-esteem feel *worse* (Wood, Perunovic and Lee, 2009) [in our files]. *Experiments.*

**What it explains.** If everything rests on me, I cannot afford to fail, and harsh self-judgment becomes the enforcer that keeps the structure standing. Many people fear that without the enforcer they will stop trying. The Breines and Chen experiments say the reverse: people treated kindly after failure tried *harder* to repair and improve. Leary's fifth study says they also took *more* responsibility, not less.

What changes when a person stops: the failure is still seen and owned. What goes is the verdict that the failure is the whole person, and with it the need to hide.

**The bridge.** FRAMEWORK's "Shame" theme: "Shame hides. Guilt that knows it is not the whole of you can repair." Its door for perfectionism: "Your worth was never riding on the score." Part-a (step 2) links this to the Tanya's "do not be wicked in your own eyes" (FRAMEWORK Q11). *(Ours:)* being held takes away the premise the enforcer rests on: if you are not holding yourself in being, your standing was never built out of your record.

**Where the bridge stops.** - Self-compassion is something you *give yourself*. In the teaching, you are not the source of your own standing at all; you are being given being. The comfort does not come from inside the self. - Self-compassion rests on common humanity: everyone fails. The teaching rests on something else: the deepest self is not a thing you made, and no stain reaches it (FRAMEWORK U2, Q09). - The teaching never reassures a person out of a real wrong, and never hands a low person a positive sentence to repeat (FRAMEWORK D05; Wood et al.). Self-compassion done badly can slide into both.

<a id="e9"></a> ## 9. Awe and the small self

**The idea.** **Awe** is what we feel before something so vast that the mind has to stretch to take it in. One of its most reliable effects is the **small self**: a person feels smaller, but not worse; less at the center, and more part of something.

**The research.** - Awe has two core features: perceived vastness, and a need to accommodate, that is, to stretch one's frame (Keltner and Haidt, 2003) [in our files]. *Theory.* - Awe shrinks the felt self. Across daily life, field and lab studies in several cultures (about 2,100 people), awe, but not other positive feelings, made the self feel smaller, and this small self went with a stronger sense of belonging to a group (Bai et al., 2017) [in our files]. *Multiple studies.* - The famous claims that awe makes people kinder and more humble are weak. Piff et al. (2015) and Stellar et al. (2018) reported them [in our files]; a 2026 series of six replication attempts produced awe reliably but reproduced almost none of the downstream effects (Graziosi et al., 2026) [in our files]. *Replication problem.* - Awe has a threatening form, fear-tinged, with lower well-being (Gordon et al., 2017) [in our files]. - Awe changes how people read the world. In five studies, awe increased belief in supernatural agents and the tendency to see intention in random events, and this was driven by awe *lowering* tolerance for uncertainty (Valdesolo and Graham, 2014) [confirmed by search]. *Experiments.*

**What it explains.** The person holding everything up is, in their own experience, at the center of their world, because everything is resting on them. Awe is the one well-measured state that moves a person off the center without shaming them. Bai's participants felt small and *belonging*, not small and worthless. *(Ours:)* a self that feels smaller inside something larger has less to carry, because it is no longer the thing everything rests on.

**The bridge.** Part-a (step 3) found the structure of awe in the moment the teaching describes: "for a second it is so", before an ordinary thing. FRAMEWORK U1's "from inside": "For a moment things look new, as if you had just walked into the room." The small self is close to what the teaching calls being less in the way (FRAMEWORK U6).

**Where the bridge stops.** - Awe research studies an emotion set off by vast *things*: mountains, stars. The teaching points at a plain thing, a cup or a table, and the vastness is a truth about it. The feeling may be awe, or a quiet love, or nothing at all, and the truth does not change. - Valdesolo and Graham's finding cuts both ways. A skeptic reads it as awe making people see agents who are not there. A believer may read it as awe opening a person to what is there. The experiment cannot decide between them, and neither can we. - "If the word withdrew, the thing would not be" can set off the threatening kind of awe. Part-a's rule holds: say both halves together. The word is saying it now, with care. - Small self is not self-erasure. Part-a and part-b both warn against reading bitul as ego dissolution.

<a id="e10"></a> ## 10. Existential grounding and terror management

**The idea.** Humans know they will die. **Terror management theory** (TMT) proposes that much of what people do, holding on to a worldview and working to feel worth something, is a buffer against that knowledge. The buffer has to be kept up, and when it is threatened, people defend it. A related idea, the **meaning maintenance model**, says people need the world to make sense, and when one source of sense is shaken, they shore up another.

**The research.** - A meta-analysis of 277 experiments found that reminding people of death produced a moderate effect (r = .35) on defending their worldview and self-esteem, larger with American participants and college students (Burke, Martens and Faucher, 2010) [confirmed by search]. *Meta-analysis.* - But a 17-lab replication with 1,550 people could not reproduce a classic mortality-reminder effect under any condition, with or without the original authors' help (Klein et al., 2022, Many Labs 4) [confirmed by search]. *Registered replication; replication problem.* - A review argues that religion serves the terror-management function especially well, and that death reminders increase belief in an afterlife and in supernatural agents (Vail, Rothschild, Weise, Solomon, Pyszczynski and Greenberg, 2010) [confirmed by search]. *Review.* - In three studies, people high in **intrinsic** religiousness (faith lived for its own sake) did *not* react to a death reminder by defending their worldview, as others did; and affirming their faith lowered how much death was on their minds, but only for the intrinsically religious (Jonas and Fischer, 2006) [confirmed by search]. *Experiments.* - The meaning maintenance model proposes that threats to the self, uncertainty, rejection and death reminders all lead people to reaffirm meaning, often in a different area from where the threat came (Heine, Proulx and Vohs, 2006) [confirmed by search]. *Theory and review.*

**What it explains.** TMT gives a picture of "holding up" at the deepest level: a person holding up a worldview and a sense of worth, against the fact of death, and having to defend them whenever they are shaken. The replication failures mean we should not lean on the lab effect. The picture of a defence that must be constantly maintained still describes something many people recognize.

*(Ours:)* Jonas and Fischer's finding is the interesting one for this project. The people who did not need to defend were the ones whose faith was lived for its own sake. It is as if they were held somewhere the reminder of death did not reach, so they did not have to hold the defence up.

**The bridge.** FRAMEWORK's "Fear": "Anyone else can let go of you. This cannot, because if it stopped you would not fall, you would simply not be." And "Suffering" cites the Tanya's letter to a sufferer: when a person pictures his own coming into being from nothing at every moment, how can he think it is bad for him (Q39)? The teaching puts the ground under the person at the level of being itself, received each moment, not at the level of a worldview the person has to defend.

**Where the bridge stops.** - TMT treats religion as a *function*: a buffer that manages terror. That is a claim about why people hold beliefs. It cannot say whether the beliefs are true, and the core lab effect is now in doubt. - The religious buffer TMT describes rests mainly on the promise of life after death. The teaching of being held is about *now*: being given being this moment. It makes no promise about how or when the person will die. - The teaching is not offered as a cure for the fear of death. It is offered as what is true, and a person may hold it and still be afraid.

<a id="e11"></a> ## 11. Trust in G-d and religious coping, positive and negative

**The idea.** **Religious coping** means using one's faith to meet hard things. Kenneth Pargament showed that it comes in two broad kinds. **Positive** religious coping includes seeing G-d as caring and working with Him. **Negative** religious coping includes feeling punished or abandoned by G-d. The same faith can be used in either way.

**The research.** - Pargament (1997, *The Psychology of Religion and Coping*) set out the field [confirmed by search]. The many methods of religious coping were measured (Pargament, Koenig and Perez, 2000), sorted into positive and negative patterns (Pargament, Smith, Koenig and Perez, 1998), and given a brief scale (Pargament, Feuille and Burdzy, 2011) [all in our files]. *Theory; measure validation.* - Across studies, positive religious coping goes with better adjustment to stress, and negative religious coping with worse (Ano and Vasconcelles, 2005) [in our files]. *Meta-analysis of correlational studies.* - **Spiritual struggles**, such as anger at G-d or feeling abandoned, can be measured, and they matter (Exline, Pargament, Grubbs and Yali, 2014) [in our files]. *Measure validation.* - Among Jews: trust in G-d went with less anxiety and depression and more happiness, and mistrust in G-d with the reverse, while general religiousness was mostly unrelated to mental health (Rosmarin, Pargament and Mahoney, 2009) [confirmed by search]. A two-week trust-in-G-d program reduced worry (Rosmarin et al., 2010) [in our files]. *Correlational, n = 565; small RCT.* - Surrender to G-d's providence was the core of a contemplative program for chronic worriers; the results were promising but come from a pilot (Knabb, Frederick and Cumming, 2017) [in our files]. *Correlational plus pilot.*

**What it explains.** For the person holding everything up, "G-d is in charge" can go two ways. It can become relief: someone good holds what I cannot. Or it can become a new weight: someone is in charge, and He is against me, or He has left. The research says clearly which way helps and which way harms. Rosmarin's 2009 finding sharpens it for Jews: it was not how religious people were that mattered, but whether they *trusted*.

What changes when a person moves from the negative to the positive pattern: the research is correlational, so it cannot show the move causes the benefit. But the small trust-in-G-d trial suggests trust can be learned, and that learning it lowers worry.

**The bridge.** The teaching's holding is benevolent at its root. FRAMEWORK U3: the hiding is not His absence; it is His might, held inside kindness (Q15). Part-a's rule for step 3: when "if the word withdrew, it would not be" is heard as a threat, answer with the other half at once, "the word is saying it now, with care". That rule is, in coping terms, a guard against negative religious coping.

**Where the bridge stops.** - Coping research studies religion as a *resource* people use in response to stress. The teaching is a truth to be known before any trouble, and it is not held for the sake of coping. - Negative religious coping is not something to argue a person out of. FRAMEWORK forbids explaining anyone's suffering (D03). Psychology agrees from its side: meaning helps when a person makes it, not when it is handed to them (Park, 2010, in our files). - Most of this research was done with Christians. Rosmarin's work is the main exception, and it is small.

**The idea.** **Gratitude** is noticing that something good came to you from outside yourself, and appreciating it. It can be a passing feeling or a lasting habit of mind.

**The research.** - Writing weekly lists of blessings raised well-being on several measures, most reliably positive mood, compared with listing hassles or neutral events (Emmons and McCullough, 2003) [in our files]. Grateful people as a trait can be measured (McCullough, Emmons and Tsang, 2002) [in our files]. *Experiments; measure validation.* - A review found gratitude strongly related to well-being, a link that may be unique and causal, and judged gratitude interventions promising but with real limits (Wood, Froh and Geraghty, 2010) [confirmed by search]. *Review.* - A meta-analysis found gratitude interventions beat doing nothing but did no better than other active psychological exercises; the effects are small (Davis et al., 2016) [in our files]. *Meta-analysis.* - Gratitude toward G-d: in a national sample of older adults, feeling grateful to G-d reduced the harmful effect of stress on health, mainly among women (Krause, 2006) [confirmed by search]. In 405 adults, the link between religious commitment and gratitude ran entirely through gratitude to G-d, and gratitude to G-d added to mental well-being beyond general gratitude, the more so the more committed the person was (Rosmarin, Pirutinsky, Cohen, Galler and Krumrei, 2011) [confirmed by search]. *Correlational.*

**What it explains.** *(Ours.)* Gratitude is the posture of receiving. The person holding everything up counts their life as made and defended by them: what I have, I earned, and I must guard it. Gratitude notices the part that was given. That is one reason it sits so close to the teaching: it is the ordinary, everyday feeling of *being given*.

What changes when a person moves toward it: the measured effects are real but small, and the research does not support promising a mood lift.

**The bridge.** FRAMEWORK U1's line is in gratitude's grammar: everything, you too, is *being given* its being right now. Part-a (step 2) links Modeh Ani, the thanks said on waking, to gratitude practice. The Rosmarin 2011 finding suggests that for believers, thanks aimed at G-d is not the same as general gratitude, and adds something of its own.

**Where the bridge stops.** - Gratitude research treats thanks as a well-being exercise. The blessing and the morning thanks are owed and said whether or not they lift the mood (part-b step 11). - Gratitude usually follows a good gift. The teaching says being is given in hard times too, and the Tanya's letter on suffering says it of the sufferer's own life (FRAMEWORK Q39). But it is said by a person about himself, never used to tell another person to be grateful for pain (FRAMEWORK D03).

<a id="e13"></a> ## 13. Doing, not overdoing: effort plus trust (hishtadlus and bitachon)

*This entry is the most our own. The research pieces are real; putting them together around hishtadlus (one's own effort) and bitachon (trust in G-d) is **ours**. What the Jewish sources say about the right measure of each belongs to the other researchers.*

**The idea.** The person holding everything up does all the work and carries all the outcome. The opposite failure is the person who does nothing and waits for G-d. Between them is a third way: do your part fully, and do not carry the part that was never yours.

**The research.** - Pargament and colleagues described three religious problem-solving styles: **self-directing** (the person handles it alone), **deferring** (the person waits for G-d to handle it) and **collaborative** (the person works with G-d as a partner). As reported in later summaries, self-directing went with more sense of personal control and self-esteem, deferring went with lower self-esteem and less planning and problem-solving, and collaborative with the best outcomes (Pargament, Kennell, Hathaway, Grevengoed, Newman and Jones, 1988) [confirmed by search; findings from secondary summaries, the full paper was not read]. *Correlational; Christian samples.* - Being able to let go of goals that cannot be reached, *and* to put one's effort into new goals, both went with higher well-being, and they worked together, in students, older adults, and parents of children with cancer (Wrosch, Scheier, Miller, Schulz and Carver, 2003) [confirmed by search]. *Correlational, three studies.* - The two-process model of control (entry 6) and the finding that coping flexibility helps (Cheng et al., 2014) describe the same balance in secular terms: act where acting works, adjust where it does not. - On the effort side, tying a planned act to a specific cue ("when X, I will do Y") is one of the most reliable findings in behaviour change (Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006) [in our files]. *Meta-analysis.* - On the trust side, trust in G-d went with less worry, and mistrust with more (entries 5 and 11).

**What it explains.** *(Ours.)* In Pargament's terms, the person holding everything up is a self-directing coper stretched to the limit. They have real strengths: control, self-esteem, planning. What they lack is anywhere to set down the part they cannot do. The deferring coper has set everything down, including the part that was theirs. The research favors neither. It favors the collaborative style, and Wrosch's work shows its shape in ordinary life: let go of what cannot be reached, and put the freed effort somewhere it can work.

What changes when a person stops holding everything up, if they do it well: they do not work less where work helps. They stop working at what work cannot reach. That is the difference between doing and overdoing.

**The bridge.** FRAMEWORK's "Control" theme is exactly this: "You do your part with full strength and stop carrying the part that was never yours. Guard: Not passivity. The line goes with an act." And its list of misreadings: "If He holds it, I don't need to act" is wrong, because the home is built by our deeds (Q66). Being given the next moment is the reason to use it. The research agrees that the deferring style, the psychological form of that misreading, goes with worse outcomes.

**Where the bridge stops.** - Pargament's collaborative style pictures G-d as a *teammate*: two agents dividing the work. The teaching goes further. The person's own effort, and the person making it, are being given being right now. It is not a partnership between two independent workers. - The research measures styles and outcomes. It cannot tell anyone where, in their own situation, the line between effort and trust falls. That is a question for the sources and for the person. - The research is correlational and mostly Christian.

<a id="e14"></a> ## 14. Rumination and the default mode network

**The idea.** **Rumination** is going over one's problems, feelings and their causes again and again, usually in general "why" terms ("why am I like this?"). It feels like working on the problem. The **default mode network** (DMN) is a set of brain regions that is most active when a person is not busy with an outside task, and it carries much of our thinking about ourselves.

**The research.** - People who respond to low mood by ruminating stay depressed longer than people who turn to action; rumination lets the low mood bias thinking and gets in the way of doing and problem-solving (Nolen-Hoeksema, 1991) [confirmed by search]. A later review found good support that rumination makes depression worse, deepens negative thinking, harms problem-solving, gets in the way of action and wears away social support; it predicts the *start* of depression more reliably than its length (Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco and Lyubomirsky, 2008) [confirmed by search]. *Theory, lab and field studies; review.* - Abstract, evaluative repetitive thought tends to harm and concrete, specific thought tends to help (Watkins, 2008) [in our files]; a later review covers causes and treatment (Watkins and Roberts, 2020) [confirmed by search; citation only]. Rumination and reflection are different habits, one driven by threat and one by curiosity, and only rumination carries the link to distress (Trapnell and Campbell, 1999) [in our files]. Self-focused attention goes with negative feeling mainly when it is ruminative (Mor and Winquist, 2002) [in our files]. *Review; correlational; meta-analysis.* - The mind wanders about half the time, and wandering usually goes with lower happiness (Killingsworth and Gilbert, 2010) [in our files]. *Large experience-sampling study.* - The DMN was identified as the brain's "default mode" (Raichle et al., 2001) and is also active in rich inner thought such as remembering and imagining (Buckner, Andrews-Hanna and Schacter, 2008) [in our files]. In depression, pooled studies show stronger coupling between the DMN and a region below the front of the brain (subgenual prefrontal cortex), and that coupling often tracks how much people ruminate; the DMN hubs themselves showed no reliable abnormality (Hamilton, Farmer, Fogelman and Gotlib, 2015) [confirmed by search]. Experienced meditators show quieter activity in DMN hubs (Brewer et al., 2011) [in our files], but different kinds of meditation produce reliably different brain patterns (Fox et al., 2016) [in our files]. *Imaging studies; meta-analysis; small samples.*

**What it explains.** Rumination is the thinking form of holding everything up. The self becomes a problem to be solved, full time, by the self. It looks like responsibility, and it is often defended that way ("I have to figure this out"). The research says it mostly makes things worse, and that it blocks the very action it claims to prepare.

What changes when a person stops: the Watkins work suggests the way out is not "stop thinking" but think differently: concretely, about one specific thing, instead of in general, evaluative loops.

**The bridge.** Part-a (step 3) found a close match between Watkins's concreteness finding and the Rashab's rule against contemplating "the world in general": one particular thing at a time, this cup, this hand. *(Ours:)* being held also takes away rumination's hidden premise, that the self is a project the self must finish. If you are being given being right now, you are not a problem you have to solve before you are allowed to rest.

**Where the bridge stops.** - The DMN findings are early, small and debated (SCIENCE.md §1.3). A quieter DMN is not the goal of this teaching, and the teaching is not a brain intervention. Contemplating a truth uses exactly the kind of inner thought the DMN supports. - Holding a line of teaching can itself tip into rumination or into religious scrupulosity. Part-a (step 6) gives the sign: threat instead of curiosity in the repetition.

<a id="e15"></a> ## 15. Learned helplessness and learned hope

**The idea.** **Learned helplessness** was the name for what happens when an animal, or a person, is exposed to bad things it cannot stop, and later stops trying even when it could. **Hope**, in C. R. Snyder's research, is the sense that you have both the will to reach a goal and a way to get there.

**The research.** - Dogs exposed to shocks they could not escape later failed to escape shocks they could (Seligman and Maier, 1967) [confirmed by search]. The idea was carried to humans with an account of how people explain bad events to themselves (Abramson, Seligman and Teasdale, 1978) [confirmed by search; citation only]. *Animal experiments; theory.* - Fifty years on, the original discoverers concluded that their theory "got it backwards". Passivity in the face of long, uncontrollable pain is not learned; it is the default response. What is *learned* is control: a brain system detects that one's actions work, and that detection switches the passivity off (Maier and Seligman, 2016) [confirmed by search]. *Review of animal neuroscience.* - Hope has two parts: **agency** (the will, "I can do this") and **pathways** (the ways, "here is how"), and it can be measured (Snyder et al., 1991; Snyder, 2002) [confirmed by search]. *Measure validation; theory and review.*

**What it explains.** There are two ways "you are not holding it up" can go wrong, and this research names both. - It can be *heard* as helplessness: nothing I do matters. That is the state Seligman and Maier described, and it is harmful. - It can be *feared* as helplessness. The person holding everything up often believes that if they let go, they will collapse into exactly that. Maier and Seligman's revision explains why the fear makes sense: under long stress, passivity is where people fall by default, and what holds it off is the learned sense that their actions work.

*(Ours:)* so what changes when a person stops holding everything up, if it is done right, is not a loss of agency. It is agency without the impossible part. The person still has the will and the ways (Snyder). What they put down is the belief that their will is what keeps the world in existence.

**The bridge.** *(Ours.)* The teaching is the opposite of helplessness. Its own guard says so: the home is built by our deeds (FRAMEWORK Q66), and "being given the next moment is the reason to use it, not to wait" (U1, misreadings). It takes away the burden of *being*, not the power of *acting*. In Snyder's terms, it leaves agency and pathways where they were and puts a floor under both.

**Where the bridge stops.** - The helplessness research is mostly animal work on electric shock. Its neuroscience of "detecting control" says nothing about G-d. - Snyder's hope rests on a person's own will and ways. The hope the teaching gives does not rest only on those. And it does not promise that effort brings the wished-for outcome.

<a id="e16"></a> ## 16. Interoception and safety cues *(description only)*

*This entry describes. It gives no breath, body or attention technique to anyone, and none should be drawn from it. That is the owner's rule for this project, and the rule of part-a and part-b.*

**The idea.** **Interoception** is the brain's sense of what is happening inside the body: heartbeat, breath, tension, gut. **Safety cues** are signals, from outside or inside, that tell the body's alarm system it can stand down. People often feel "holding everything up" in the body before they can name it: tight shoulders, a braced chest, trouble sleeping.

**The research.** - Interoception is the sense of the body's physiological condition, and a foundation of how feelings are felt (Craig, 2002) [confirmed by search]. A large consensus paper sets out how it bears on many mental health problems (Khalsa et al., 2018) [confirmed by search]. *Reviews.* - One theory holds that the stress response is *on* by default and is held back only when safety is perceived, so that ongoing stress often comes from a general lack of perceived safety rather than from any one stressor (Brosschot, Verkuil and Thayer, 2016) [confirmed by search]. *Theory.* - The presence of a trusted person is one of the clearest safety cues measured: holding a husband's hand quieted threat responses in the brain (Coan et al., 2006), and a partner's photo lowered pain and raised activity in a safety-signal region (Eisenberger et al., 2011) [both confirmed by search; entry 1]. *Small imaging studies.* - People accompanied by a friend, or thinking of a supportive friend, judged a hill less steep (Schnall, Harber, Stefanucci and Proffitt, 2008) [confirmed by search]. But a major critique of hundreds of "thinking changes seeing" studies argues that none is compelling, and that such results can be explained by method problems, such as people's judgments or response biases shifting while what they actually see does not (Firestone and Scholl, 2016) [confirmed by search]. *Two small experiments; contested.* - **Polyvagal theory** (Porges, 2007) [confirmed by search] is widely used in popular writing about safety and the body. A detailed critique argues that its basic premises are likely refuted (Grossman, 2023) [confirmed by search]. We do not use its language. *Theory; critical review.*

**What it explains.** The brace people describe is real and physical, and the research gives it a plausible shape: an alarm that stays on until something signals safety. The strongest safety signals measured so far are relational: someone trustworthy is here. *(Ours:)* this helps explain why the teaching is so often described in bodily words. FRAMEWORK U1's "from inside": "The shoulders of the mind come down a little."

**The bridge.** *(Ours.)* When the teaching lands, it may act, at the level of the body, like a safety cue: a sense that something reliable is here. That is a description of a possible side effect, not a method.

**Where the bridge stops.** - The teaching is not a bodily state, and being held is not a feeling of calm. A person can be held and still be tense. A person can be calm and not know anything about it. - Nothing here licenses turning the teaching into a relaxation exercise. That would make the body's calm the point, and the project's rules forbid body and breath techniques for the user. - The perception and polyvagal findings are contested. We report them so builders know not to lean on them.

## The five strongest bridges *(our judgment)*

1. **Felt security frees action (entry 1).** The best-established finding: people who feel held go out, explore and take on challenges more, not less. It matches the teaching's "held, not holding" and its insistence that being held is the reason to act, not a reason to stop.
2. **Trust in G-d eases worry, and mistrust feeds it, through intolerance of uncertainty (entries 5 and 11).** Rosmarin's work is the only evidence here drawn from religious Jews, and it points at the exact mechanism of "holding the future up". It also shows that the *picture* of G-d matters more than how religious a person is.
3. **Sorting what is yours from what is not (entries 4, 6 and 13).** Control research, coping flexibility, goal disengagement and Pargament's collaborative style all say the same thing in different words: full effort where effort works, and no carrying of what effort cannot reach. The self-directing extreme and the deferring extreme both do worse. This is the teaching's own "Control" theme.
4. **Acceptance: the feeling stays, and its grip changes (entry 7).** Acceptance research shows that people who stop fighting their fear still feel it, but it stops standing on them. That is FRAMEWORK U1's "from inside" almost word for word.
5. **Kindness after failure brings repair (entry 8).** The fear that letting go of harsh self-judgment will make a person careless is contradicted by experiments. People treated kindly after failing took more responsibility and tried harder to repair. This is the shame and guilt distinction the teaching already draws.

**Close behind:** the revised helplessness finding (entry 15), as a guard: letting go of holding everything up is not the same as helplessness, and must not become it.

## Where psychology and the teaching part ways

1. **Feeling versus fact.** Psychology measures felt security, beliefs about G-d, and the effects of holding them. The teaching says something about what *is*: everything, you included, is being given its being right now. No study can test that, and none of the studies above tries.
2. **Two versus one.** Psychology's holding is always relational: a person, and someone who holds them. The teaching's holding is the being of the person itself. From His side, nothing stands apart as a second thing (FRAMEWORK U4), and the Tanya refuses even the picture of a soul in a body (Q67). The attachment parallel describes how being held *feels*; it does not describe what the teaching says is true.
3. **Effect versus truth.** Psychology values these ideas by their effects: less worry, more well-being. The teaching is held because it is true, and its effects on mood are side effects. A guide that sells it for its benefits turns it into a technique, and the research shows those benefits are modest.
4. **Earned versus given.** Security in psychology is built through years of good care, or earned later. Self-compassion is something a person gives themselves. The teaching's holding is neither earned nor self-given. It is already true of everyone, whatever their history.
5. **Motive versus truth.** Several theories explain religious belief by what it does for people: compensation for poor attachment, compensatory control, terror management. These describe motives in some believers. They cannot show a belief is false, any more than they could show it true. And two of the key experiments behind them failed to replicate.
6. **Outcomes.** Much psychological relief comes with better expectations about outcomes. The teaching refuses to promise outcomes. It moves the certainty from the outcome to the being of the person facing it. The research on worry shows why that is the sturdier kind of relief, and why "it will all be fine" would break.
7. **Who was studied.** Most of this research was done with Western, often Christian, often student samples. Almost none was done with Jews, and none with the Chassidic teaching itself.

## Misreadings the research helps catch *(for the builders)*

- **"So I don't need to act."** Deferring coping goes with less planning and lower self-esteem (entry 13). The teaching's own guard: the home is built by our deeds.
- **"So it will turn out fine."** That is a new positive belief about the future, and it feeds worry's bargain (entry 5). Being held is about being, not outcomes.
- **"He surrounds me" heard as surveillance.** For someone anxious or avoidant toward G-d, this is how it may land (entry 2). Lead with care, and with "fills from inside".
- **A punishing picture.** Negative religious coping goes with worse outcomes (entry 11). Never use the teaching to explain anyone's pain.
- **An affirmation to repeat.** It backfires for people with low self-esteem (entry 8).
- **A relaxation exercise.** The body's calm is not the point, and body techniques are out of scope (entry 16).
- **Self-erasure.** The small self of awe and the "less of me" of the teaching are not ego dissolution (entry 9; part-a step 7; part-b step 9).
