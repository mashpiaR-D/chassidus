## Tanya

*the Alter Rebbe (R. Shneur Zalman of Liadi, 1745-1812)*

A handbook for the "beinoni", the in-between person: someone whose evil still has its full strength inside but never gets to rule his deeds, speech or thought. Likkutei Amarim maps the two souls and gives a graded method: contemplation that produces love and awe, rules of timing for sadness and bitterness, and ways to handle intrusive thoughts, pride and love of others. Iggeres HaTeshuvah redefines return as leaving the sin, not fasting. Iggeres HaKodesh is pastoral letters: suffering and trust, humility after being saved, charity, anger, and a rebuke of seeking worldly counsel.

- TAN: **core.** Tanya is the founding operating manual. It gives an achievable identity (the beinoni) instead of perfection, rules the garments rather than feelings, and makes timing a clinical tool: sadness now is a trick, bitterness only at a fixed hour, joy always in service. It separates sadness from bitterness, locates self-contempt in the body alone, strips self-torment from teshuvah, and grounds trust in suffering on creation from nothing at every moment.

## Torah Ohr

*the Alter Rebbe (R. Shneur Zalman of Liadi, 1745-1812)*

Maamarim on the weekly portions, delivered by the Alter Rebbe and edited by the Tzemach Tzedek. Each one starts from a verse or a saying of the Sages, builds a Kabbalistic account of the worlds (sefirot, Tohu and Tikun, the surrounding and indwelling light), and then often turns briefly to avodah: prayer, the Shema, Torah study, bitterness and joy, refining one's traits. Its aim is to show that the inner life of a Jew in prayer and Torah mirrors, and brings about, the divine dynamics it describes. Most of the text is cosmology. The therapeutic material is concentrated in short, practical turns, usually introduced with "the advice for this" (עצה היעוצה) or "let a person take to heart" (ישים האדם אל לבו).

- TO1 · part 1 of 2: Bereshit to Bo (lines 12-1625): **some.** Among early Chabad works it gives unusually concrete diagnoses of prayer that fails: love that fades because it is cut off from the mind; the \"narrow throat\" blocking mind from heart; the error of the working man who thinks he cannot pray. It offers graded remedies: the path from below for those who feel nothing, the voice, the red wine of bitterness before the white wine of joy, and compassion for the spark rather than for oneself. It gives an explicit dignity to people absorbed in making a living.
- TO2 · part 2 of 2: Beshalach to Megillat Esther (with additions), lines 1626-3087: **some - most lines are cosmology and Kabbalah, but many maamarim end with short, sharp avodah passages on dead prayer, fleeting inspiration, bitterness and joy, ego, and hidden love.** Esther as a psychology of hiddenness. The love is hidden, not absent, 'not as people think'. The broken, mindless cry from distress is 'above daas'. Arrogant thoughts come from outside ('Haman') and are wept over as garments on the spark. Ego is a protective husk a beginner needs. Two diagnostics stand out: the barren soul that grasps at making its own feelings, cured by becoming an empty vessel, and the gap between knowing G-d ('hearing') and feeling Him ('seeing', daas).

## Likkutei Torah

*the Alter Rebbe (R. Shneur Zalman of Liadi, 1745-1812)*

This is a collection of the Alter Rebbe's maamarim (discourses) on the weekly portions, edited with many cross-references. Each discourse opens with a verse or a saying of the Sages, lays out its Kabbalistic structure (sefiros, the worlds, arousal from below and from above), and then usually turns in a paragraph or two to 'the service of man'. That turn gives short, sharp teachings on prayer, teshuvah, bitterness versus sadness, foreign thoughts, inspiration that fades, anger and desire. The medicine sits in those turns, scattered through a mostly cosmological text.

- LT1 · part 1 of 3: Beshalach, Pekudei, Vayikra to Sh'lach (lines 12-2105): **some.** It tells joyless worry and bitterness about distance apart from sadness, and confines bitterness to a set hour (Tikkun Chatzos) so Torah and joy carry the day. It explains why foreign thoughts attack exactly at prayer. It diagnoses why inspiration fades after weeks. It shows how fervor without Torah sours into anger and ego. It has anger turned on one's own evil so it cools toward others. It has love as will ('avah') ruling the emotions, and the lost-hope penitent reaching the 'rock' above time.
- LT2 · part 2 of 3: Korach to Nitzavim (lines 2106-3793): **some.** It gives the Elul king-in-the-field image: God meets everyone in plain clothes with a smiling face. It treats the lost spark as something sought in the very place it was lost, with bitterness turned into compassion for one's own exiled soul. It diagnoses sadness as coming from demanding greatness, and says humility belongs in the mind, not a numbed heart. It says spoken confession gathers a scattered self, and that only in this world can a person change what he is.
- LT3 · part 3 of 3: Rosh Hashanah, Shabbat Shuvah, Yom Kippur, Ha'azinu, Sukkot/Shemini Atzeret, Shir HaShirim, additions (lines 3794-5300): **some.** Teshuvah here is compassion for the fallen divine spark, not self-concern: praying for oneself is called the lowest level. The desiring power is holy at its root and can be turned into love. A heart broken 'by effort' still counts. Worry about livelihood is strange fire to be burned in morning prayer, while earning is praised. God's hiding is a father's game of hide-and-seek. 'Black yet beautiful': the beauty comes from the darkness itself. Ratzo without shov is pride. Weeping and joy are to be held together.

## Kuntres HaHispaalus

*the Mitteler Rebbe (R. Dovber Schneuri, 1773-1827)*

Two pastoral letters from 1814 to the Chassidim. The first is short: people are worn down by earning a living, many are close to despair, and he offers to travel and hold study gatherings for followers who live far away. The second is the Kuntres itself, a detailed guide to the feelings that contemplation and prayer produce. It separates real divine feeling from counterfeit, lays out five levels in the natural soul and five in the divine soul, sorts people into five types, and names the self-deceptions at each level. It attacks two opposite errors: forbidding all feeling in the name of depth, and chasing the feeling itself. It gives signs a person can use to test himself.

- KH: **core.** The Chabad library's dedicated phenomenology of spiritual feeling. It maps levels of feeling in both souls and gives testable signs for telling real from counterfeit: real feeling is unaware of itself, lasts, flows into action, and survives insult. Its central rule is that the deeper the feeling, the less it feels itself. It defends emotion against intellectual suppression and also exposes the ego in chasing highs. Sharpest of all, it diagnoses self-deception by the fruits a feeling leaves.

## Sha'arei Teshuvah

*the Mitteler Rebbe (R. Dovber Schneuri, 1773-1827)*

A manual in two parts for "people pressed by their business who long to bring their hearts close to their Father in Heaven." It teaches how teshuvah of the heart (bitterness, a broken heart) and prayer of the heart (joy, love, contemplation) depend on each other. Teshuvah comes before prayer as its foundation. The work names the ways the heart is blocked (dullness, desire, worry about livelihood, stray thoughts, coldness) and gives the matching work for each. Its main teaching is set inside long Kabbalistic maamarim on the Gates of Teshuvah, Choice and Prayer, with a part 2 and a section on education (Chinuch).

- MST: **substantial.** It calibrates bitterness and joy against each other and fixes teshuvah before prayer every day. It sorts types of inner dullness and blames some of it on inherited temperament. It says closeness and distance from the body are one motion and that one should not check oneself. It treats stray thoughts as what is left of past will, 'scattering of the soul.' It gives two tools, contemplation (sword) and the cry from the point of the heart (arrows), and places choice in the first point of will.

## Imrei Binah

*the Mitteler Rebbe (R. Dovber Schneuri, 1773-1827)*

The Mitteler Rebbe gathers his father's teachings on the Shema and adds his own explanations. The work is organized as gates: Kerias Shema, Tzitzis and Tefillin. The author states its aim himself: in hard times, give ordinary people a way to settle the unity of God, the higher unity (Havaya) and the lower unity (Elokim), into mind and heart, so their souls can find rest. The introduction and the opening gate press that deep contemplation is required of everyone, each by his capacity. After that, most of the book is Kabbalistic exposition of the letters of the Shema, the sefirot and the worlds. Guidance for inner life comes up only here and there.

- IB: **some.** This work insists that the Shema's commandment is felt knowing (daas as deepened thought, against hirhur), required of every simple Jew. It condemns both false humility and the collecting of Kabbalistic kavanos as ego. It spells out the law that darkness precedes light in everyday terms: money, children, learning, livelihood. It pairs love from distance with awe from nearness. It holds that a desire once born can outrun the mind, which is a lamp and not a ruler.

## Sha'arei Orah

*the Mitteler Rebbe (R. Dovber Schneuri, 1773-1827)*

Discourses for Chanukah and Purim. The author's introduction states the aim: to 'light up the soul with the Divine light' ('the way of life'), so that each reader 'may find rest for his soul according to his measure'. The book is built on two kinds of mesirus nefesh (giving one's life for what matters most) and two kinds of light. Chanukah is light from below, from the receiver: the soul's own lamp. Purim is light from above, from the giver, as at Sinai. Around this frame, long Kabbalistic chapters on the menorah, oil, the wick, incense, sacrifices, and the royal garment and horse carry scattered but precise passages on how the soul's light catches, holds or leaps away in a person.

- SO: **some.** The lamp-oil-wick psychology explains exactly why real inspiration fails to hold (a lack of self-nullification, not a false feeling). The two wills explain why the yoke is steadier than mood-driven choice. Hidden-not-lost light answers the person who feels nothing despite effort, with 'wanting to want' as the first step. 'Eyes down, heart up' warns that humility without the heart's lift paralyzes. The work also gives a pedagogy of beginnings (generous gifts beyond measure).

## Sha'ar HaYichud

*the Mitteler Rebbe (R. Dovber Schneuri, 1773-1827)*

The author calls it the "kuntres of contemplation": a short guide to how a person brings divine unity into a mind and heart that can hold it, across every level from the first tzimtzum to this world. The first eighth teaches the anatomy of contemplation. It covers what real iyun (sustained study) is, how daat binds a person to an idea, and how tevunah carries an idea out into the heart and into prayer. It also asks whether to contemplate in general terms or in particulars, and gives the signs that tell true feeling from self-deception. The rest sets out, in Kabbalistic detail, the material to contemplate.

- GU: **some.** It is the Chabad library's anatomy of contemplation itself. It separates iyun, daat and tevunah, and names tevunah as the missing bridge from the classroom to prayer. It argues for particular over general contemplation, with a concession for beginners. Its sharpest tool is a proportional test of authentic feeling: joy and bittul, and weeping and joy, must come from one contemplation in equal measure. It is openly suspicious of self-deception even among people raised in Chassidus.

## Toras Chaim

*the Mitteler Rebbe (R. Dovber Schneuri, 1773-1827)*

This part is a set of long, closely argued maamarim on the Bereishis and Noach portions. Each one starts from a verse or a saying of the Sages and works it out through Kabbalistic structure: Tohu and Tikun, the divine names Ma and Ban, chochmah and the middos, the four worlds. Now and then it grounds the abstraction in the inner work of the heart, mostly prayer, teshuvah and bittul. Those passages are rich and precise, and in the Noach maamar the theme of earning a living under pressure carries the whole discourse.

- TC1 · part 1 of 4: Bereishis volume 1 (lines 13-1202): **some.** This work offers a full psychology of livelihood pressure as the exile's 'many waters'. That pressure purifies and can lift the soul above its origin, yet cannot touch the hidden love. It also gives a precise diagnosis of why a person falls right after an inspired prayer, and a mapping of spiritual pride to its roots: anger and zeal after prayer as the residue of self-feeling. Finally, it states candidly that the mind reins the traits but cannot remake them.
- TC2 · part 2 of 4: Bereishis volume 2 (lines 1203-1825: Toldos through Vayechi): **some.** Precise inner diagnostics. Joy larger than its cause is really the heart opening up, and it empties into frivolity. Constant delight becomes pain, so learning needs rhythm. A prayer arousal that reaches only the outer heart explains the pious person who cheats without feeling any contradiction. Two wines: 'intoxicating' bitterness for someone who cannot yet reach joy. Holy bitterness is love in disguise, sharply separated from despair that falls into lust and anger. Worry without substance is waste from an empty soul.
- TC3 · part 3 of 4: Shemos volume 1 (lines 1826-3718): **some.** It gives a precise anatomy of spiritual numbness. Egypt is the throat between mind and heart, where understanding gestates, and Pharaoh is a stubborn will above reason that blocks the birth of feeling. Voice is the bridge. The dream model of the post-prayer 'hypocrisy' is distinctive: both sides are true, and only their fusion is exile. So are the reading of exile as earning a living swallowing the inner self, and foreign thoughts as afterbirth.
- TC4 · part 4 of 4: Shemos volume 2 (lines 3719-5446): **some.** A phenomenology of mood cycles: waking in smallness or expansiveness, night-time irritability and illness, rises and falls in spirit and livelihood, all read as ratzo and shov, with explicit instruction against both despair and pride. Also a sharp split between felt ecstasy (possibly self-feeling) and the humble, joyful yoke of plain deeds. Arrogance and chutzpah (Amalek) are made the single root of craving and envy. And toil and despair are shown to produce a greater delight.

## Derech Mitzvosecha

*the Tzemach Tzedek (R. Menachem Mendel Schneersohn, 1789-1866)*

This is the Tzemach Tzedek's book on the inner reasons of the commandments. Each section starts from the halachic definition (Rambam, Chinuch) and asks what the commandment does at its root, mostly in Lurianic terms: sefiros, vessels, drawing down the light of Ein Sof. It ends with a long essay on the root of prayer and Shema, several discourses, and supplements. Most of it is cosmology and Kabbalah. Its therapeutic content comes as the "why" of a few commandments: faith versus knowing, loving God, loving one's fellow, confession and return, and how bitterness and joy work in prayer.

- DM: **some.** This work reaches the soul through the reason behind each commandment. It draws a sharp line between faith and da'as (the thief praying at the tunnel), which explains why belief leaves feeling unmoved. It says love cannot be commanded or chased; only contemplation can be commanded. Confession works in two ways: regret takes the sin's 'soul' and spoken words take its 'body'. Loving one's fellow is a precondition of whole prayer. The love born of distance is like dammed water breaking through. The bitterness of the beinoni is set apart from the awe of the tzaddik.

## Kuntres HaAvodah

*the Rebbe Rashab (R. Shalom Dovber Schneersohn, 1860-1920)*

A short manual (7 chapters, c.1909-10) for young men beginning the "service of the heart", prayer with contemplation. It argues that the aim of prayer's labor is love of G-d, which both binds the soul to G-d and refines the animal soul. Fear (acceptance of the yoke) is required at all times as its foundation. Most of its practical force goes to diagnosing false service: an excitement that leaves no mark, general contemplation that breeds bitterness, going cold at the start, reaching above one's level. It prescribes slow, detailed contemplation aimed at one's own particular unrefined trait. The pages come with the Previous Rebbe's preface and two letters.

- KA: **substantial.** It is a diagnostic manual of false inner work more than a system: imagined excitement that leaves no mark, general contemplation that turns into sadness posing as bitterness, going cold at the start and taking it as unfitness, and reaching above one's level. Its test is behavioral: did your particular trait change after prayer? It pairs this with an unusually humane rule of graded, truthful self-demand, and insists that no soul is unfit.

## Kuntres HaTefillah

*the Rebbe Rashab (R. Shalom Dovber Schneersohn, 1860-1920)*

A short open letter (1900) from the Rebbe Rashab to his Chassidim, who kept asking "how does one pray?" It says prayer, the service of the heart, is the core of inner work. Learning Chassidus is only preparation. The heart and character actually change only when that learning is contemplated at length and in detail during prayer. The work diagnoses several "illnesses" it sees among Chassidim, gives five preparations for prayer, and adds separate tracks for beginners, busy businessmen and everyone. It closes by asking that learning be "in order to do".

- KT: **substantial.** A diagnostic manual for telling real feeling from fake. It separates feeling that is forced, comes from a clever new interpretation, or comes only from the bottom line, from feeling that arises from inside. It defines da'at as feeling, and so ties refinement to feeling rather than to knowledge. It shows why general resolutions fail and particular ones last. It names false humility as a trick of the inclination. It gives separate tracks for beginners, for busy businessmen and for everyone.

## Kuntres Umaayan

*the Rebbe Rashab (R. Shalom Dovber Schneersohn, 1860-1920)*

Twenty-eight short maamarim on the verse "A spring shall come out of the house of G-d and water the valley of Shittim." It reads shittim as shtus, a spirit of folly. The work is a clinical anatomy of how the yetzer fools a person: indulgence, "it won't cut me off", excuses, crediting oneself, pride, and the overbusy merchant. Each folly is exposed by reasoning. For someone whose G-dly soul does not shine and who cannot win by reasoning, the cure is holy stubbornness from the soul's essence, above reason. Each section ends with the author's own summary.

- KU: **substantial.** A graded map of self-deception (shtus). The sinner still knows he sinned. The self-justifier blames others but knows he needs repair. The self-crediter thinks he needs none. The proud man damages himself further, and each is subtler and worse than the last. The cure is itself a folly: holy stubbornness, above reason, for those whose G-dly soul does not shine, as a bridge to understanding later. It also holds that the simple man's pained, pleasureless prayer outranks the scholar's, and it diagnoses workaholism as treating a vessel as the source.

## Sefer HaSichos

*the Rebbe Rayatz (R. Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn, 1880-1950)*

This is a collection of the Rebbe Rayatz's talks, mostly in Yiddish and written down by listeners, from farbrengens in Soviet Russia and later Riga (1920-1931). The talks move between stories of earlier Rebbes and chassidim, replies to people at the table, communal urgings about Torah study under persecution, and short Chassidus teachings. It also contains his first-person diary of his 1927 arrest in Spalerno prison, and biographical notes. It is less a treatise than a live record of how a Rebbe spoke to, corrected and encouraged real people at the table.

- SHS1 · part 1 of 2: 1920-1931 (lines 13-6078): **some.** This part gives lived cases rather than theory. It has a first-person record of mastering terror in a Soviet prison, step by step. It has homely Yiddish parables: the businessman's wheel and paying old debts bit by bit, and the drummer boy crying 'more joy'. It draws a sharp line between sadness and productive bitterness, and anchors joy in the time rather than one's state. It lays out a clear ladder of the mind working on the emotions. And it addresses busy working people directly, lowering the bar with 'darkness becomes light' through small fixed practice, a partner and an elder.
- SHS2 · part 2 of 2: 1946-1950 (lines 6079-11107): **some.** An explicit physician-of-the-soul model: diagnose before prescribing; general remedies (for heat toward desire, coldness to holiness) versus expert-prescribed ones; knowing the remedy without taking it lets illness take root; fantasy and self-image as the core sickness of the spirit. Also: essence as fire in flint for people who have drifted in America; a dark temperament as a tactic of the yetzer hara; the rule of delaying anger an hour and a minute; worry turned into mission.

## Hayom Yom

*the Lubavitcher Rebbe (R. Menachem Mendel Schneerson, 1902-1994), compiling sayings of his father-in-law R. Yosef Yitzchak and earlier Rebbes*

A daily calendar of about 600 very short entries, one per day of the year. The Rebbe compiled it (1942-43) from talks, letters and stories of the previous Rebbes. Each day gives a custom or point of liturgy, a date in Chabad history, and usually one saying: a practical, distilled piece of avodah (inner work). The aim is to put a short line of Chassidus in a person's hand every day and to shape his day, prayer, livelihood and relations with others. It mixes Hebrew with a lot of Yiddish.

- HY: **substantial.** It gives the diagnosis in distilled one-liners, meant as a daily dose. It includes an explicit soul-medicine protocol: locate the illness (body, faculties or habit), wish to heal, believe you can, then repair the weak and strengthen the healthy. It also offers a sharp critique of sighing instead of acting, the 'pious animal soul' test for self-deception, a comparison rule (look below materially, above spiritually), and many vivid Yiddish sayings on second chances and the undefiled core.

## Inyanah Shel Toras HaChassidus

*the Lubavitcher Rebbe (R. Menachem Mendel Schneerson, 1902-1994)*

This is the Rebbe's essay on what Chassidus essentially is. The virtues usually listed for it are waking the world from a faint, going beyond the letter of the law, changing the nature of one's traits, and making Godliness graspable to anyone. The Rebbe says these are only branches of one essential point. Chassidus draws down the Ein Sof itself. It is the yechidah (the essence of the soul) of the Torah, just as Moshiach is the yechidah of the world. He shows this by taking Modeh Ani through the four layers of Torah reading (pshat, remez, drush, sod) and showing how Chassidus brings each one to life. He then argues that the essence shows itself most when it reaches the lowest places. The appendix explains why the later, darker generations receive these revelations.

- INY: **some.** This is the programmatic source for the project's premise. Chassidus is the yechidah of Torah, so it is by definition a teaching about the soul's essence. It adds a clear essence-versus-revelation psychology. The essence is not one more higher faculty: it can never be flawed, it is what all the levels are made of, it alone can transform rather than fight, and it shows most in the lowest places and in action. It also turns this into a daily practice through Modeh Ani.

## Likkutei Sichos

*the Lubavitcher Rebbe (R. Menachem Mendel Schneerson, 1902-1994)*

Edited talks on the weekly Torah portions and the festivals, plus appended letters. A typical sicha raises a precise question about the wording in Rashi, Rambam, a Midrash or a halacha, resolves it, then often ends with a lesson "for our deeds and service now" (הוראה). The lessons mostly direct the reader toward spreading Torah, Ahavat Yisrael, teshuvah and yearning for redemption. The appended letters give direct personal counsel, some of it to people who are frightened about illness.

- LS1 · part 1 of 3: volumes 30-33 (lines 13-15159): **some.** It works through small wording questions in Rashi and Rambam that open onto a direct personal lesson. It gives explicit, practical psychology on worry, including in Yiddish letters to frightened patients: go to the doctor, then stop researching, trust, act on your mission, serve with joy. It explicitly warns that bittul must not make a person a doormat. It insists on quality over quantity in how a person defines himself. And it says a mere inner thought of return transforms a person, so a sense of unworthiness is no reason to hold back from helping others.
- LS2 · part 2 of 3: volumes 34-36: **some.** Vol. 36's health appendix is rare in the corpus: the Rebbe's practical guidance on mental illness and low mood. It insists on medical experts and new treatments, occupation and physical work, distraction through helping others, and building self-confidence through responsibility, alongside trust and mitzvos. It also recasts trust (bitachon) as active inner work that shapes outcomes even for the undeserving, and teshuvah as building outward rather than self-abasement.
- LS3 · part 3 of 3: volumes 37-39 (Vayikra, Bamidbar, Devarim; with Iggeres HaTeshuvah commentaries and appended letters): **some.** The appended letters give concrete, operational tools. Test thoughts by what they breed. Divert attention rather than argue. Answer the urge to introspect with 'I am simple, not an investigator'. Expose despair by noticing it leaves the appetites intact. Treat teshuvah as return to self, not regret. There is also a halachic map of degrees of compulsion (Metzora), the claim that the essence never sleeps (Emor), and practical guidance on grief, prognosis and livelihood.

## Biurei Tanya

*Chassidim of the Chabad school (20th c.): R. Gronem Esterman, R. Yaakov Kaidaner, R. Alter Simchovitz*

Three commentaries on Tanya by chassidim who taught it. Esterman is the longest. It glosses phrase by phrase and often says what a line means "in avodah." Its introduction argues that Chassidus made inner work (the soul's service) the main thing, where earlier musar worked on the body's actions. Kaidaner gives long answers to hard questions in the Tanya, above all on sadness versus bitterness and on who can be a beinoni. Simchovitz's notes are almost all cross-references to maamarim and Talmud sources, with a biography of him as a mashpia (spiritual mentor). The practical psychology is concentrated in their treatment of Tanya ch.12-14 and 26-31.

- BT: **some.** This is the Tanya as working chassidim taught it. It gives practical tests the Tanya only implies: tell sadness from bitterness by the presence of vitality; test for real change by whether your wants have changed; a love like honor shows only when denied. It splits responsibility sharply: the mind rules the garments, not the heart. It says contemplation is useless mid-craving, so the hidden love must be used. Its prayer advice on stray thoughts is concrete, down to 'think about it after prayer'. It unmasks holy anger as temperament.

## Sha'arei Avodah

*R. Aharon HaLevi of Strashelye (1766-1828)*

A systematic manual of avodah by the Alter Rebbe's student. The introduction says that the verse "know today and bring it back to your heart" contains all of service in three parts: knowing (mind), the heart (love, fear, the heart's will) and deed (Torah and mitzvot). None of them works without the others. The author writes because people each seize one part: some take knowledge and reject feeling, some take feeling and reject knowledge, some take deed alone. Five gates follow: the unity of ZuN and the person, the unity of souls, prayer (two loves), Torah and mitzvot, and teshuvah. Its polemical center is a defense of felt emotion in prayer.

- SA: **substantial.** It is Chabad's most sustained defense of felt emotion (hitpaalut) in service against those who suppress feeling as ego. It holds that mixed, imagined devotion is still "whole" and true from its root, drawing on the Alter Rebbe's "yonati tamati." It pairs humility from outside with strength from inside. It gives a graded ladder against temptation, carries prayer through the day by memory, and offers a return in the very moment of craving by crying to what is above will. Its image of the traits melting like wax so they can be reshaped is its own.

## Pelach HaRimon

*R. Hillel HaLevi of Paritch (1795-1864)*

A collection of R. Hillel of Paritch's explanations of maamarim, arranged by the parsha cycle from Vayikra to Behaalosecha. Much of it is his notes of talks by the Mitteler Rebbe and the Tzemach Tzedek, with his own commentary added. Its aim is to lay out the Kabbalistic structure of the festivals and mitzvos (Pesach, the Omer, Shavuos, Yom Kippur) and to trace each one into avodah, the inner work of prayer, teshuvah and bittul. Scattered through it are passages that stop and diagnose a person's inner state and prescribe a remedy. One whole treatise (440-483) does this systematically.

- PR: **some.** It offers graded triage. It matches the depth of the damage (heart, mind, simple will, essence) to the deeper faculty that must be woken: contemplation, then surrender beyond reason (the 'lot'), then the essence-point. It warns that bitterness cannot be sustained. It diagnoses 'lovesickness' as loss of taste and pairs purging in prayer with rebuilding by Torah, like medicine and then food. The divine soul 'explains' itself to the animal soul with compassion for both. It also advises making one's pain known to others, and teaching calmly.

## Chanah Ariel

*R. Yitzchak Eizik Epstein of Homel (1770-1857)*

Derushim on the weekly parsha and the festivals by a leading mashpia who studied under the Alter Rebbe and the Mitteler Rebbe. Most of it is dense Kabbalah: partzufim, Tohu and Tikkun, nogah, the unifications. Scattered through it, often as "and so it is in avodas Hashem", are sharp practical passages. They cover how contemplation matures into feeling, how to hold bitterness and joy together before prayer, what anger is, the blocked mind, pride hiding in religious zeal, the weight of lapses, and when to afflict oneself. They lean on Yiddish phrases and parables from town life.

- CA: **some.** A mashpia's clinical distinctions: a blocked mind versus Tanya's heavy heart; a clean 'Jewish emptiness' versus a desert of pride and anger, each with its own remedy; being held by anger versus holding it; lapses from weakness versus resentful service. It also gives graded remedies by severity, three stages for contemplation reaching the heart, pride hidden in zeal, and the image of a soul that feels alive only through bitterness.

## Sheeris Yehudah

*R. Yehudah Leib of Yanovitch (c.1760-1826), brother of the Alter Rebbe*

A collection of halachic writings: a commentary on the laws of salting meat, then responsa on all four parts of the Shulchan Aruch (many exchanged with the Tzemach Tzedek), the agunah files, notes on the Gemara and teachings heard from the Alter Rebbe. It has no maamarim and no teaching on avodah or bittul. Its human material comes up in passing, in how a posek treats people: a shochet restored through real teshuvah, a frightened woman believed, agunos released, peace kept between husband and wife. There is also one short letter of personal counsel to the Tzemach Tzedek.

- SY: **little.** This is the only work here by the Alter Rebbe's brother. It shows chassidic compassion at work inside halacha rather than in maamarim. It has a worked-out psychology of teshuvah for public failure: past and future are handled as separate acts, genuine humbling must not be conditional, and shame already borne is enough. It measures fear by the frightened person's own heart. It names melancholic doubt as foreign to the sages. It also preserves one private letter of counsel to the Tzemach Tzedek about his lasting 'nature'.
