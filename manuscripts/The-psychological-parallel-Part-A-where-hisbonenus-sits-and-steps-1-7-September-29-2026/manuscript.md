## Read this first: what this is, and what it is not

- **It is a guideline and a check for the builders.** It tells us which parts of the ladder line up with things psychology has measured, what those studies found, how strong the findings are, and what that suggests for how the guide describes a step, words a question, or watches for trouble.
- **It is not the guide's voice.** The guide never speaks in therapy terms, never names these constructs to a person, and never refers anyone to therapy outside danger (the owner's rule; FOUNDATIONS.md §1). Nothing here should leak into a reply as vocabulary.
- **It is not mindfulness, and the guide is not mindfulness.** Mindfulness was deliberately taken out (owner's call D16). Hisbonenus is thinking a truth through until it is real; it is not watching the breath or noticing sensations (manual Part I, "What is not here").
- **Psychology does not prove Chassidus, and Chassidus is not psychology in old clothes.** Where the two genuinely meet, we say so. Where they only look alike, we say exactly how they part. The research below measures what people feel, believe and do. It cannot test whether the world is being said into being now, or whether the soul is a part of God above. Those are the sources' claims, and they stand or fall on their own ground.

**How to read the evidence labels.** Each finding carries one of these:

| Label | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Meta-analysis** | Many studies pooled statistically. The strongest kind we cite, though only as good as the studies in it. |
| **RCT** | A randomized controlled trial: people assigned by chance to a practice or a comparison. Size and duration are noted. |
| **Experiment** | A lab study that manipulates one thing and measures another, usually short and with students. |
| **Correlational** | Two things measured together; shows they go together, not that one causes the other. |
| **Review / theory** | A synthesis or model; useful for concepts, not proof. |
| **Pilot** | Very small or uncontrolled; a hint only. |
| **Replication problem** | Later attempts did not reproduce the finding, or reproduced it only in part. |

## 1.1 The three families of meditation

The most useful modern map is **Dahl, Lutz and Davidson (2015)**. They sort meditation practices into three families by the main mental mechanism each trains:

- **Attentional family.** Trains attention and *meta-awareness*, which they define as heightened awareness of the processes of consciousness (thinking, feeling, perceiving). It targets *experiential fusion*: getting absorbed in the contents of the mind without noticing. Examples: focused attention on the breath or a mantra; open monitoring ("choiceless awareness"); mindfulness-based stress reduction.
- **Constructive family.** Cultivates or strengthens a way of seeing and feeling, mainly through *perspective-taking and reappraisal*. It targets unhelpful self-schemas. Sub-kinds: relationship-oriented (loving-kindness, compassion; their table also lists Centering Prayer, a Christian practice), values-oriented (for example contemplations of mortality) and perception-oriented.
- **Deconstructive family.** Uses *self-inquiry* to loosen *cognitive reification*: taking thoughts and perceptions as accurate pictures of reality. Sub-kinds: object-oriented insight (looking into the nature of things), subject-oriented insight (looking into the one who experiences), non-dual. Their table puts Tibetan **analytical meditation** here, and describes discursive analysis as identifying the assumptions behind reifying an object or experience and questioning their consistency.

They say plainly that many practices contain elements of all three families; a practice is filed by its primary mechanism. They also say the constructive and deconstructive families have been studied far less than the attentional one. An older paper from the same group, **Lutz, Slagter, Dunne and Davidson (2008)**, set up the familiar split between *focused attention* (holding attention on one chosen object) and *open monitoring* (non-reactively watching whatever arises).

A second map comes from data rather than theory. **Matko and Sedlmeier (2019)** had 100 experienced meditators rate how similar 20 popular techniques are, and found seven clusters on two dimensions: how body-oriented a practice is, and how physically active. One cluster is **contemplation**: contemplating a spiritually important question, or holding something contradictory or complex in mind. It sits at the conceptual, non-bodily end.

## 1.2 So where does hisbonenus go?

Our reading (ours, not the papers'): **hisbonenus is a constructive practice with an analytical engine, held in place by attention.** In more detail:

- **Its engine is analytical.** Breadth, length and depth are discursive reasoning about one teaching, carried into particulars (FOUNDATIONS §2; Gate of Unity, "a strong look into the depth of one matter"). In Dahl's table that is the analytical style, which they file under the deconstructive family.
- **Its aim is constructive, and relational.** Analytical meditation in Dahl's sense aims to loosen the sense that things exist solidly on their own. Hisbonenus does something like that at step 3 ("this cup is not a thing on its own") and step 7 ("not a thing on my own"). But what it builds in their place is a relationship: love and awe of a Someone who faces the person, and an act done for Him. That is the constructive family's relational and values sub-kinds, with a theistic object none of the secular practices have.
- **Attention is scaffolding, not the point.** Binding the mind and coming back without arguing (step 6) train attention, and the Piaseczner's minute of watching thoughts (step 1) is the one moment that resembles open monitoring. The manual keeps it to a minute or two and forbids treating the quiet as the goal (manual Part I, Stage 2 and "Where the sources disagree", no. 4).
- **It is not open-monitoring mindfulness.** The object of a sitting is a line of teaching, never a sensation, and the aim is to make a truth *more* gripping, not to hold all contents lightly. This is the single most important difference for builders to keep straight, and it recurs at step 6.
- **In Matko and Sedlmeier's terms** it belongs in the *contemplation* cluster: conceptual, still, and aimed at a question that matters.

## 1.3 What science knows about religious and contemplative meditation

The honest summary: **there is some solid work on religious meditation in general, a little on Jewish religious practice, and none that we could find on Chabad hisbonenus itself.** Everything in the step cards below is therefore evidence *by analogy*: it tests a psychological process that resembles a move of the ladder, not the ladder.

**The benchmark: secular meditation.** A large systematic review of randomized trials with active controls (**Goyal et al., 2014**; 47 trials, about 3,500 people) found moderate evidence that mindfulness programs reduce anxiety, depression and pain by small-to-moderate amounts, low evidence for stress and quality of life, and no evidence that meditation beats other active treatments. **Van Dam et al. (2018)**, written by researchers inside the field, warn that the public claims run well ahead of the data, that "mindfulness" is poorly defined, and that harms are under-measured. *Strength: meta-analysis; critical review.* The lesson for us: even the best-studied kind of meditation has modest, well-bounded effects, and hisbonenus should never be sold as more.

**Does the religious content matter?** - **Wachholtz and Pargament (2005)** randomly assigned people to a spiritual meditation (a phrase about God), a secular meditation, or relaxation, 20 minutes a day for two weeks. The spiritual group showed larger drops in anxiety, more positive mood and spiritual experience, and tolerated a cold-water pain test almost twice as long. *Strength: small RCT, short.* - **Rosmarin, Pargament, Pirutinsky and Mahoney (2010)**: 125 religious Jews with raised stress and worry got a two-week, internet-delivered, spiritually integrated program (built around trust in God), or progressive muscle relaxation, or a waitlist. The spiritual program reduced stress and worry and improved trust in God compared with the waitlist; relaxation mostly did not differ from waitlist. *Strength: RCT, small, short, subclinical sample; the most directly Jewish evidence we have.* - **Knabb, Frederick and Cumming (2017)** built a model of worry around surrender to divine providence (from a Jesuit contemplative text) in two Christian samples, then ran an 8-week contemplative-prayer group for 13 chronic worriers. *Strength: correlational plus a pilot.* - Two meta-analyses of religious and spiritual interventions: **Gonçalves et al. (2015)** (23 RCTs) found benefits mainly on anxiety; **Captari et al. (2018)** (97 studies) found religion-adapted psychotherapy worked better than no treatment and than some secular comparisons, but in the most rigorous "additive" designs it was *equal* to standard therapy on distress and better only on spiritual well-being. *Strength: meta-analyses, with mixed quality of included trials.* Note that these are mostly therapies with religious content, not contemplative practices.

**How prayer trains the mind.** The anthropologist T. M. Luhrmann and colleagues studied evangelical Christians learning to "hear God". **Luhrmann, Nusbaum and Thisted (2010)** found that a trait called *absorption* (the capacity to be caught up in inner experience) went with sharper mental imagery and more unusual spiritual experience, and that practice mattered too: the more people prayed, the more such experiences they reported. **Luhrmann and Morgain (2012)** proposed that prayer is *inner-sense cultivation*: a learned attentional skill. **Luhrmann, Nusbaum and Thisted (2013)** then randomly assigned Christians to a month of imaginative (kataphatic) prayer or to Bible-study lectures; the prayer group reported more vivid mental imagery and more unusual sensory experiences, with large individual differences. *Strength: one small RCT plus ethnography.* This bears on the Piaseczner's use of pictures (step 4) and on the risk of unusual experiences (Part B).

**Brain imaging.** **Newberg et al. (2003)** scanned three Franciscan nuns during a repeated-phrase prayer and reported changes in frontal and parietal blood flow. *Strength: preliminary, n = 3.* We cite it to show how thin the neuroscience of theistic contemplation still is, not to lean on it.

**Chabad contemplation in scholarship.** The Mitteler Rebbe's Kuntres HaHitpa'alut is available in English as *Tract on Ecstasy* (**Jacobs, 1963**). Historical and theological studies of Chabad's contemplative program include **Loewenthal (1990)** and **Elior (1993)**. Broader maps of Jewish meditation include **Eifring (2013)** (meditation in Judaism, Christianity and Islam) and **Persico (2022)** (a phenomenological typology of Jewish meditative techniques). These are humanities works: they describe the practice, they do not measure its effects.

**The gap, stated plainly.** We found no controlled study of hisbonenus or of any Chabad contemplative practice. The closest empirical evidence is (a) Rosmarin's trust-in-God program for religious Jews and (b) the general finding that meditation with religious content can do at least as well as secular meditation for religious people. A study of the ladder would be new work.

## Part 2. Steps 1-7

Each card follows the brief: the step, the psychological terms, what the research says (with strength), measures, where they match and where they part, what it gives the guide, and risks.

<a id="step-1"></a> ### Step 1 · Where you stand: what is already moving

**The step.** Before any idea: name in one sentence what is moving in you now, then a minute of the Piaseczner's quiet (watch your thoughts; ask "what am I thinking now?") until the flood slows. The scatter is "sleep" and "the exile of the Shechinah"; the restlessness is the spark reaching out. You cannot make the feeling; forcing it kills it.

**Psychological terms.** - *Affect labeling*: putting a feeling into words. It works as a quiet form of emotion regulation even when the person is not trying to regulate anything. - *Mind-wandering* and *meta-awareness*: thought drifting from the task, and noticing that it has. - *Decentering*: seeing thoughts as passing events in the mind rather than as facts or as the self. - *Paradox of valuing happiness*: the harder people aim at feeling good, the more likely they are to be disappointed. - *Ironic process*: trying to push a thought away tends to bring it back.

**What the research says.** - Labeling a feeling calms it. In an fMRI study, labeling the emotion in negative pictures reduced amygdala response and raised activity in a right frontal region, compared with other ways of processing the same pictures (**Lieberman et al., 2007**). A later review gathers experiential, bodily, neural and behavioural evidence that labeling acts as *implicit* regulation: it does not feel like regulating (**Torre and Lieberman, 2018**). *Strength: experiments; review.* - In a clinical test, spider-fearful people who labeled their feelings during exposure showed lower skin conductance a week later than those who reappraised, distracted, or did exposure alone. But their *self-reported* fear was no lower (**Kircanski, Lieberman and Craske, 2012**). *Strength: small RCT; mixed outcome.* - The mind wanders a great deal. In a smartphone study, people were thinking about something other than what they were doing almost as often as not, and that usually went with lower happiness (**Killingsworth and Gilbert, 2010**). Reviews treat noticing the drift (meta-awareness) as a separate step from the drift itself (**Schooler et al., 2011**; **Smallwood and Schooler, 2015**). *Strength: large experience-sampling study (correlational); reviews.* - Aiming at a feeling backfires. People who valued happiness more were less happy in low-stress times; when led to value happiness, they reacted less positively to a happy film, through disappointment at their own feelings (**Mauss et al., 2011**). *Strength: two studies (one experimental), women only.* - Suppressing thoughts backfires. The classic "white bear" studies (**Wegner et al., 1987**) found that suppressed thoughts return; a meta-analysis confirms the rebound after suppression and finds the immediate increase mainly under mental load (**Wang, Hagger and Chatzisarantis, 2020**). *Strength: meta-analysis.* - Dwelling is not the same as naming. A meta-analysis of emotion-regulation instructions found that "concentrating" on the feelings of an event made things slightly worse (**Webb, Miles and Sheeran, 2012**; the review's summaries were later criticized in a published comment, **Augustine and Hemenover, 2013**). *Strength: meta-analysis, contested in detail.*

**Measures.** - Mind-Wandering Questionnaire, 5 items (**Mrazek et al., 2013**). - Experiences Questionnaire, decentering subscale (**Fresco et al., 2007**); for the concept see **Bernstein et al., 2015**. - Toronto Mindfulness Scale, decentering factor, a state measure taken right after a session (**Lau et al., 2006**). Use only for the decentering items; the guide is not measuring "mindfulness". - PANAS for positive and negative affect before and after a sitting (**Watson, Clark and Tellegen, 1988**).

**Match.** Real. Naming the scatter in one sentence is affect labeling. Watching thoughts "at, not in" is meta-awareness and decentering. "You cannot make the feeling" is the paradox of valuing happiness. "Don't fight the thoughts" is the ironic-process finding.

**Where they part.** - In psychology, labeling and decentering *are* the intervention. Here they are a door: the sentence is said so the line can be brought into it, and the quiet is "the table cleared for the line". - Decentering in mindfulness-based therapy is an end-state skill held across the day. The Piaseczner's watching lasts a minute and ends in one verse. - Psychology treats the scatter as noise. The Piaseczner reads the unease itself as the soul's cry heard in sleep. No construct carries that; it is Chassidus's own reading.

**What it gives the guide.** - Keep the arrival to **one sentence with a feeling word in it** ("I'm tired and worried about Thursday"). That is enough to get the labeling effect; more than that drifts into dwelling. - Keep the quiet short and never call it the goal. The research gives no reason to extend it, and the manual's rule (a minute or two, then the line) already fits. - Never ask for, or promise, a feeling. The more the person wants it, the likelier the letdown. - When a stray thought comes, the instruction "come back without arguing" is the right one; fighting it brings it back.

**Risks the research flags.** Turning the naming into a long look at the feeling can make it worse (Webb et al.). Quiet watching of thoughts is the one attentional-family moment in the ladder, and that family carries the reported cases of meditation-related difficulty; Part B covers those. For someone in real danger, naming is not enough and the stop rules apply.

<a id="step-2"></a> ### Step 2 · Under the layers: the true self

**The step.** Walk the layers (what I do, what I feel, what I believe about myself), see that each one changes, and look at each from outside. Under all of them, the deepest you is "truly a part of God above". The Godly soul's nefesh serves by acknowledgment (Modeh Ani). The verdict "I am my mistakes" goes quiet. The counterfeits: inflation ("I'm a divine spark, so anything goes") and self-condemnation.

**Psychological terms.** - *True self*: the ordinary belief that under a person's surface there is a real self who they "really are, deep down". - *Self-concept clarity*: how clearly and stably a person's beliefs about themselves hang together. - *Self-compassion* vs *self-criticism*: treating one's failings with kindness and a sense of shared humanity, versus harsh self-judgment. - *Observing self* (sometimes "self-as-context"): the self that notices, as distinct from the contents it notices. We found no validated measure we could confirm for this; *(unverified)*. - *Gratitude practice* (for the act, Modeh Ani).

**What the research says.** - People reliably separate a "true self" from the self in general, and they believe the true self is good and moral. This holds for how people see themselves and others, and across cultures (**Strohminger, Knobe and Newman, 2017**). Observers see a person's true self in their morally good acts more than their bad ones, and *their own* moral values shape what they count as someone else's true self (**Newman, Bloom and Knobe, 2014**). *Strength: review; vignette experiments.* - Having the true self in mind goes with meaning. People for whom true-self traits came to mind more readily reported more meaning in life, and priming those traits raised it (**Schlegel et al., 2009**); feeling that one knows one's true self also predicts meaning (**Schlegel et al., 2011**). *Strength: experiments and correlational studies.* - Self-compassion (measured by **Neff, 2003**) is strongly associated with lower psychopathology: a meta-analysis found a large inverse correlation of about r = −0.54 (**MacBeth and Gumley, 2012**). *Strength: meta-analysis of correlational studies; says nothing about cause.* - Telling yourself you are good can backfire for the people who most need it. People with low self-esteem who repeated "I'm a lovable person" felt *worse*; those with high self-esteem felt a little better (**Wood, Perunovic and Lee, 2009**). *Strength: experiments.* - Gratitude practices help a little. Compared with an activity-matched comparison they raise well-being slightly, but no more than other psychologically active exercises (**Davis et al., 2016**). *Strength: meta-analysis; small effects.* - Practice can inflate the self. Yoga and meditation students showed *more* self-enhancement bias on practice days, because the practice had become central to how they saw themselves (**Gebauer et al., 2018**). A "spiritual superiority" scale correlated about r = .47 with narcissism, and scored higher in some kinds of spiritual training than in mindfulness trainees (**Vonk and Visser, 2021**). *Strength: within-person experiments; correlational.*

**Measures.** - Self-Concept Clarity Scale (**Campbell et al., 1996**). - Self-Compassion Scale (**Neff, 2003**). - Meaning in Life Questionnaire, presence and search (**Steger et al., 2006**). - Spiritual Superiority scale, as a counterfeit check (**Vonk and Visser, 2021**). - True-self accessibility is measured by reaction time, not a questionnaire (**Schlegel et al., 2009**).

**Match.** Partial but useful. The folk belief that the deep self is good is near-universal, which is why "under everything, the spark" lands easily. The quieting of "I am my mistakes" parallels self-compassion replacing self-criticism. Looking at each layer from outside parallels subject-oriented inquiry in Dahl's deconstructive family.

**Where they part.** Sharply, at the root. - The psychological "true self" is a *belief people hold*, studied as a belief; research does not claim it exists. The Chassidic true self is an ontological claim: the soul is a part of God above. It is not a psychological construct, and the guide never presents it as one. - The research shows people project their own values onto the true self (Newman et al.). The Chassidic true self is not whatever the person values; it is found "in reaching toward Him", not by looking in alone, and it is measured against Torah, not preference. - Self-compassion rests on common humanity ("everyone fails"). The Tanya's "do not be wicked in your own eyes" rests on the spark's being, which is not a shared human flaw but a Godly root.

**What it gives the guide.** - Do not hand a low person a positive sentence about themselves to repeat. For them it can backfire (Wood et al.). Describe the spark as a fact about everyone, then turn at once to one small good act, since people read the true self off good deeds (Newman et al.). The manual's act ("one good act done as if the good in you were the real you") already does this. - Offer Modeh Ani or thanks on waking as a daily act, without promising it will lift the mood; the measurable effect of gratitude practice is small. - Keep the "spark" language away from ranking. It is true of everyone equally; the moment it becomes a distinction, the inflation counterfeit starts (Gebauer; Vonk and Visser).

**Risks the research flags.** Spiritual inflation and superiority; positive self-statements backfiring in low self-esteem; the true self becoming a mirror for the person's own preferences.

<a id="step-3"></a> ### Step 3 · Being said, right now (rung one)

**The step.** Each thing exists only because His word is saying it now; if it withdrew, the thing would not fall, it would not be. Binah in breadth: one particular thing at a time (this cup, this window, my hand), never "the world" in general, which the Rashab calls false imagination. For a second it is *so*; then it may go flat. The counterfeit: "nothing is real, so nothing matters".

**Psychological terms.** - *Awe*: the response to something perceived as vast that the mind has to stretch to take in. - *Small self*: the sense of one's own self shrinking in the face of something larger. - *Concrete vs abstract processing*: thinking in specific, sensory "how" terms versus general, evaluative "why" terms. - *Construal level*: psychologically distant things are thought of abstractly, near things concretely. - *Reappraising the stimulus*: seeing the same object under a different meaning.

**What the research says.** - Awe has two core features: perceived vastness, and a need to accommodate (to stretch one's mental frame) (**Keltner and Haidt, 2003**). *Strength: theory.* - Awe shrinks the sense of self. In daily-life, field and lab studies, awe, but not other positive emotions, diminished the sense of self, in both individualist and collectivist cultures, with differences in size and content (**Bai et al., 2017**). *Strength: multiple studies, N ≈ 2,100.* - The famous downstream claims are shaky. **Piff et al. (2015)** reported that awe made people more generous and ethical via the small self. A 2026 series of six replication attempts produced awe successfully but reproduced almost none of the predicted effects of Piff's and two other well-known awe studies: 5 of 33 outcomes were significant (**Graziosi et al., 2026**). *Strength: replication problem.* The awe-shrinks-self finding is sturdier than the awe-changes-behaviour finding. - Concrete beats general. A major review found that abstract, evaluative repetitive thought tends to be unhelpful and concrete, specific thought more helpful (**Watkins, 2008**). In a pilot trial with 60 dysphoric people, training them to think concretely reduced depressive symptoms and rumination more than a sham training or a waitlist (**Watkins and Moberly, 2009**). *Strength: review; pilot RCT.* - Distance and abstraction go together (**Trope and Liberman, 2010**). *Strength: theory with many experiments.* Our inference *(ours)*: the Chassidic move pairs the most distant object there is (the Infinite) with the most concrete handle (this cup). Construal-level theory would predict that the concrete handle is what lets a vast truth feel near rather than remote. - Reappraisal of the thing itself works. In the emotion-regulation meta-analysis, reappraising the stimulus (d ≈ 0.36) and perspective-taking (d ≈ 0.45) changed emotional outcomes more than reappraising one's own response (**Webb, Miles and Sheeran, 2012**). *Strength: meta-analysis.* - Awe has a dark variant. Vast things that are threatening produce a fear-tinged awe with its own appraisals and physiology, and it is linked to lower well-being (**Gordon et al., 2017**). *Strength: multiple studies.*

**Measures.** - Awe Experience Scale (AWE-S), six facets including self-diminishment and vastness (**Yaden et al., 2019**). - Pictorial and verbal small-self measures (**Bai et al., 2017**). - Cambridge Depersonalisation Scale, as a safety check for the "nothing is real" counterfeit (**Sierra and Berrios, 2000**).

**Match.** Good on two points. "For a second it is so" in front of an ordinary thing has the structure of awe (vastness, stretching the frame) and its small-self effect. The Rashab's rule against "the world in general" matches the concreteness research closely: specific beats general.

**Where they part.** - Awe research studies an emotion evoked by vast *stimuli* (mountains, space, trees). Here the object is deliberately small and plain, and the vastness is a truth about it. The feeling the sources name is often not awe at all but "a quiet love like water". - Psychology cannot touch the claim itself (that the cup is being said now). It can only say what happens in people who hold it. - The "flat" moment the Rashab names (going cold just as the real work starts) has no clear counterpart we could confirm. It may relate to habituation *(unverified; no citation)*.

**What it gives the guide.** - **One plain object, in detail.** "This cup, the handle, the chip on the rim, being said now", not "all of creation". Concreteness is where the research and the Rashab agree. - Expect the flat moment and say so beforehand; the manual's "stay, don't switch" has no conflict with the research. - Say both halves together. If someone hears "if the word stopped it would not be" as a threat, answer at once with the other half the step holds: the word is saying it now, with care. That turns threat-awe back toward the kind that holds. - Don't promise that awe will make a person kinder; the evidence for that is weak.

**Risks the research flags.** Threat-based awe (fear rather than wonder). The "nothing is real" counterfeit resembles derealization; if a person describes the world as unreal or themselves as absent for more than a moment, that is Part B territory and a stop signal.

<a id="step-4"></a> ### Step 4 · Held: this particular thing, from inside and all around

**The step.** He fills each thing from inside and surrounds all things. All the eyes see is the King's outer clothing, with someone inside. "You surround me and fill my body and my spirit." From inside: *held, not holding*. The act: say "I have set God before me always" right before the thing you fear. The counterfeit: a cozy general glow, or picturing God and the world as a soul in a body (which the Tanya says fails at the root).

**Psychological terms.** - *Attachment to God*: relating to God as a safe haven in distress and a secure base for going out into the world, with two dimensions of insecurity: anxiety (fear of being abandoned) and avoidance (keeping distance). - *Felt security* and *security priming*: a momentary sense of being safe and held, which can be raised by reminders of caring figures. - *Trust in God*: the belief that God is benevolent and involved; its opposite, mistrust. - *Mental imagery*: picturing, which engages emotion more than verbal thinking does. - *Implementation intention*: an "if situation X, then I do Y" plan tied to a cue.

**What the research says.** - God works as an attachment figure in the mind. Among Israeli Jews, threat primes made God-related words more accessible; "God" primes made secure-base words more accessible; and people high in interpersonal avoidance showed these effects less (**Granqvist, Mikulincer, Gewirtz and Shaver, 2012**). *Strength: lab experiments using subliminal priming; priming methods in social psychology have had broad replication troubles, so treat as suggestive (general caution, not a failed replication of this study).* - Attachment to God has anxious and avoidant dimensions that relate to mood and personality (**Rowatt and Kirkpatrick, 2002**); the Attachment to God Inventory measures them (**Beck and McDonald, 2004**). *Strength: correlational; measure validation.* - Boosting felt security, even briefly, is followed by better mood, more compassion and more tolerance in many studies (**Mikulincer and Shaver, 2007**). *Strength: review of experiments.* - For religious Jews, a two-week trust-in-God program reduced worry and stress against a waitlist (**Rosmarin et al., 2010**). A surrender-to-providence contemplative-prayer group for chronic worriers showed promise in a pilot (**Knabb, Frederick and Cumming, 2017**). *Strength: small RCT; pilot.* - Pictures move feelings more than words. Imagining unpleasant events raised anxiety more than thinking about the same descriptions verbally (**Holmes and Mathews, 2005**); a review argues imagery has a special tie to emotion and to emotional disorders (**Holmes and Mathews, 2010**). *Strength: experiments (tested mainly for anxiety); review.* - If-then plans work. Across 94 tests, forming an implementation intention had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals, d ≈ 0.65 (**Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006**). *Strength: meta-analysis.*

**Measures.** - Attachment to God Inventory (**Beck and McDonald, 2004**). - Trust/Mistrust in God, brief form (**Rosmarin, Pirutinsky and Pargament, 2011**). - State Adult Attachment Measure, security subscale, before and after a sitting (**Gillath et al., 2009**).

**Match.** Strong on experience. "Held, not holding" is very close to felt security, and a God who surrounds is, psychologically, a safe haven that goes everywhere. Saying the verse right before the feared thing is a textbook implementation intention. The Piaseczner's reliance on pictures matches the imagery research.

**Where they part.** - Attachment theory models God on a person: a caring figure one relates to. The Tanya's "filling and surrounding" is not a figure beside you but the being of the thing and of you. The soul-in-body picture is exactly what the Tanya says fails at the root (claim A03). The attachment parallel describes how being held *feels*; it does not describe what Chassidus says is true. - Security priming is often subliminal and passive. Here the person holds the truth deliberately, with the mind, until it is so (the method of step 6 runs through this step too). - The research treats imagery as a lever to be used. The Tanya treats every picture as one that must break ("in truth he cannot grasp a divine matter with no garment at all").

**What it gives the guide.** - Tie the verse to one named cue: "right before the call with your boss, say it". An if-then plan is one of the most reliable findings in behaviour change. - Use a picture when the line stays in the head, and break it every time. Choose images that hold, not images that loom: imagery amplifies whatever feeling it carries. - Listen for a punishing or distant God-image. For someone anxious or avoidant toward God, "surrounded from all sides" can feel like being watched. Go slowly and start from the "fills from inside" half.

**Risks the research flags.** Anxious or avoidant attachment to God can turn "surrounded" into surveillance. Negative religious coping (feeling punished or abandoned by God) is linked to worse adjustment (**Ano and Vasconcelles, 2005**, meta-analysis); spiritual struggles can be measured with the Religious and Spiritual Struggles Scale (**Exline et al., 2014**). Vivid imagery can amplify anxiety.

<a id="step-5"></a> ### Step 5 · Two knowings, and the hiding is His

**The step.** From His side the world is nothing; from ours it really is a separate thing. Both are true, neither erases the other. The sense that the world runs by itself is His own power to hide, and once you know He hides there, it is no longer a hiding. Hold both views open without collapsing them (heart above, eyes below). The counterfeits: "it's all one, so the lines melt"; or using "He is hiding" to explain someone's pain.

**Psychological terms.** - *Explanatory coexistence*: holding a natural and a religious explanation of the same event at once. - *Dialectical thinking*: tolerating apparent contradiction rather than forcing a choice. - *Wise reasoning*: recognizing multiple perspectives, the limits of one's knowledge, and change. - *Meaning-making* and *benevolent religious reappraisal*: understanding a hard event within a larger, good framework; its opposite, *punishing-God reappraisal*. - *Self-distancing*: stepping back to see one's situation from a wider view.

**What the research says.** - People very commonly hold natural and supernatural explanations of the same event together, and this coexistence grows with age rather than fading, across cultures (**Legare, Evans, Rosengren and Harris, 2012**). *Strength: review of developmental studies.* - Cultures differ in handling contradiction: in Peng and Nisbett's studies, Chinese participants were more inclined to accept both sides of an apparent contradiction and Americans to pick one (**Peng and Nisbett, 1999**). *Strength: experiments; cultural comparison.* - Wise reasoning can be measured as a state tied to a concrete situation, and it relates to adaptive emotion regulation (**Brienza et al., 2018**, N ≈ 4,500). *Strength: large psychometric study.* - Meaning-making helps adjustment when meaning is actually *made*; the effort to make meaning on its own is often linked to more distress (**Park, 2010**). *Strength: integrative review; mixed evidence.* - Positive religious coping (including benevolent reappraisal) goes with better adjustment, and negative religious coping (including seeing God as punishing) with worse (**Ano and Vasconcelles, 2005**; coping types defined in **Pargament, Koenig and Perez, 2000**). *Strength: meta-analysis of correlational studies.* - Taking a distanced, wider view lets people reflect on painful things without sliding into rumination (**Kross and Ayduk, 2017**). *Strength: review of experiments.*

**Measures.** - Situated Wise Reasoning Scale (**Brienza et al., 2018**). - Brief RCOPE, positive and negative religious coping (**Pargament, Feuille and Burdzy, 2011**). - Religious and Spiritual Struggles Scale, as a risk check (**Exline et al., 2014**).

**Match.** Moderate. That ordinary minds hold two explanations of one event at once, without strain, is well documented; the step asks for something with that shape. "The hiding is His" is structurally a benevolent reappraisal, and "heart above, eyes below" resembles holding a wide view while standing in the particular.

**Where they part.** - For psychology, coexistence and dialectical thinking are ways of reasoning; it does not ask whether both explanations are true. The Tanya says both knowings *are* true, each from its side, and that their unity lies above the mind (step 11). - Dialectical thinking tolerates contradiction. Chassidus does not call the two a contradiction to be tolerated, and it rejects the relativist version ("it's all one, so the lines melt") outright. - Religious reappraisal in the coping literature is applied to one's own hardship. Step 5 is a truth about the world, contemplated before any hardship, and the guide forbids applying it to someone else's.

**What it gives the guide.** - Give the two sentences side by side and do not merge them. Asking the mind to fuse them is asking it to do what the step's own edge says it cannot. - **Never use "He is hiding" to explain anyone's suffering.** The research agrees from its own side: meaning helps when the person makes it, and a punishing-God reading is linked to worse outcomes. - When someone is in pain, offer the step as a thing to hold, not an answer to "why".

**Risks the research flags.** Meaning imposed from outside; a punishing-God reading; boundary loss in the "all is one" direction (Chassidus's own counterfeit; no psychological study we could confirm maps it exactly).

<a id="step-6"></a> ### Step 6 · It is so: binding the mind until the heart answers

**The step.** "Know today and return it to your heart." Da'at is binding the mind to a truth "with a strong, very firm bond", as firmly as to a thing you see. Fasten the thought; when it drifts, come back without arguing; say one sentence and repeat it only while it widens; stay long. "A person can understand a thing well and still not feel it." Then it is so, and the heart answers, often only a feather. The counterfeits: forcing a feeling; collecting insights instead of binding one.

**Psychological terms.** - *Propositional vs implicational meaning* (Teasdale's Interacting Cognitive Subsystems): specific, statable meanings versus a holistic, felt level of meaning; emotion comes mainly from the second. - *Head-heart dissociation* ("rational-emotional dissociation"): believing something intellectually but not feeling it to be true. - *Sustained attention and the return*: the cycle of focus, drift, noticing, and returning. - *Elaborative vs maintenance rehearsal*: repeating something while working its meaning, versus just keeping it in mind. - *Reflection vs rumination*: curious self-attention versus anxious, repetitive self-attention. - *Habit formation*: behaviour repeated in a stable context becoming automatic.

**What the research says.** - Teasdale's model distinguishes a specific level of meaning from a holistic, intuitive level, and argues the holistic level is what produces emotion; this explains why arguing with a belief often leaves the feeling untouched (**Teasdale, 1993**). *Strength: theory with some experimental support.* - Clinicians widely recognize that a person can accept a belief rationally and not feel it; the phenomenon is common but little researched (**Stott, 2007**). *Strength: theory / clinical analysis.* - In focused meditation, the mind cycles through drifting, becoming aware of the drift, shifting back, and holding; each phase lines up with different brain networks (**Hasenkamp et al., 2012**). *Strength: small fMRI study of experienced meditators.* - Pushing thoughts away makes them rebound (**Wegner et al., 1987**; **Wang et al., 2020**). *Strength: meta-analysis.* - Mere repetition does not deepen. Holding a word in mind longer, or rehearsing it more, did not improve later memory unless the rehearsal worked its meaning (**Craik and Watkins, 1973**). *Strength: classic lab experiments.* - Reflection and rumination are different dispositions: one driven by curiosity, one by threat, and rumination, not reflection, carries the link to distress (**Trapnell and Campbell, 1999**). *Strength: correlational, well replicated as a distinction.* - Habits form slowly and unevenly. People repeating a daily behaviour in the same context took a median of about 66 days to reach peak automaticity, ranging from 18 to a projected 254 days, and missing a single day did not derail it (**Lally et al., 2010**). *Strength: real-world observational study, N = 96.* - Aiming at the feeling undercuts it (**Mauss et al., 2011**, see step 1).

**Measures.** - Rumination-Reflection Questionnaire (**Trapnell and Campbell, 1999**). - Experiences Questionnaire (**Fresco et al., 2007**), read here as a check that holding the line does not collapse into fusion with anxious thoughts. - Penn Inventory of Scrupulosity, as a risk check (**Abramowitz et al., 2002**). - A study of this step could also rate, before and after, how true a line "feels" versus how true one "thinks" it is, following the clinical practice Stott describes *(no validated scale confirmed)*.

**Match.** The strongest in Part A. The Rashab's "a person can understand well and not feel it" is Teasdale's gap between propositional and implicational meaning, and Stott's head-heart dissociation, almost word for word. "Come back without arguing with the stray thought" matches both the return-cycle and the suppression research. "Repeat it only while it widens" is elaborative, not maintenance, rehearsal. "The same line for a week" fits the habit data.

**Where they part.** One difference matters more than all the others. - **The direction is opposite.** In mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, Teasdale's insight is used to *loosen* the grip of implicational meanings ("thoughts are not facts"), because the meanings in question are depressive ones. Da'at uses the same lever to *tighten* the grip of a truth, making it as real as something seen with the eyes. Hisbonenus binds to the thought; mindfulness decenters from it. This is why the guide is not mindfulness, and it is the place builders are most likely to blur them. - In the research, the felt level is the outcome. For the Mitteler Rebbe the heart's answer is not the aim; it "happens by itself", and the deepest answer is the least felt. - Da'at is not rehearsal. The binding is to a truth taken as real, not to a memory item.

**What it gives the guide.** - Keep "say one sentence and repeat it only while it widens". Repetition that does not work the meaning is maintenance rehearsal and does little. - Keep "come back without arguing". Arguing with the stray thought is suppression and it rebounds. - Name a fixed daily moment for the line, and tell the person that missing a day does not undo it. - Never check the heart during the sitting; if nothing moves, the thinking still counts.

**Risks the research flags.** Binding can tip into rumination or into religious scrupulosity (anxious, repetitive fear of having sinned or of God's punishment). The signs are threat, not curiosity, in the repetition. If the line has turned into self-accusation, stop and go back to steps 2 and 4.

<a id="step-7"></a> ### Step 7 · Before Him, nothing: not a thing on my own (rung two)

**The step.** "Before Him all counts as nothing": the creature is like a ray still inside the sun, fully light, with no existence of its own. Depth: the idea in its fewest words, then stillness; Chochmah, seeing. From inside: less full of yourself, not crushed, only less in the way; "not delight and not joy, only the self nullified"; still, like a silent stone. Bitul in existence (bitul bimetzius). The counterfeits: pride in humility; self-hatred dressed as humility. Never on a low day.

**Psychological terms.** - *Quiet ego*: a self-identity that is less defensive and more balanced toward self and others. - *Hypo-egoic state*: lowered self-awareness, with thoughts about oneself more concrete and present. - *Humility*: an accurate view of oneself, acknowledging limits, and low self-focus; not low self-esteem. - *Self-focused attention*: attention turned on oneself. - *Self-transcendent experience*: a passing state with less sense of self and more connectedness. - *Ego dissolution* and *non-dual awareness*: contrast terms; a loss of self-boundaries, and awareness without a subject-object split.

**What the research says.** - Hypo-egoic self-regulation: sometimes the self controls behaviour better by stepping back than by trying harder; hypo-egoic states involve lower self-awareness or more concrete self-thought (**Leary, Adams and Tate, 2006**). *Strength: theory/review.* - The Quiet Ego Scale measures four facets: detached awareness, inclusive identity, perspective-taking and growth (**Wayment, Bauer and Sylaska, 2015**). *Strength: measure validation.* - Humility is distinct from low self-esteem (**Tangney, 2000**). *Strength: review.* Measuring it is paradoxical: saying "I am humble" may itself signal the opposite, so reviews recommend ratings by others (**Davis, Worthington and Hook, 2010**). *Strength: review.* - Awe and humility: awe-prone people were rated more humble by friends, and induced awe led people to credit outside forces more for their own accomplishments (**Stellar et al., 2018**). A small direct replication of one of its experiments did not reproduce the effect (**Bertrand et al., 2024**), and the wider awe literature has replication problems (**Graziosi et al., 2026**). *Strength: several studies; replication problem.* - Self-focused attention goes with negative affect, most strongly when it is ruminative, and not when it is on positive aspects of the self (**Mor and Winquist, 2002**). *Strength: meta-analysis of mostly correlational studies.* - Self-transcendent experiences share lowered self-salience and raised connectedness, and range from mild (flow, awe) to intense (mystical experience) (**Yaden et al., 2017**). *Strength: review.* - Practice can raise the ego instead of quieting it (**Gebauer et al., 2018**; **Vonk and Visser, 2021**, see step 2). *Strength: experiments; correlational.* - Doing kind acts raises the actor's well-being by a small-to-medium amount (δ ≈ 0.28), without signs of publication bias (**Curry et al., 2018**). *Strength: meta-analysis.* Whether *secret* kindness adds anything was not tested there *(unverified)*.

**Measures.** - Quiet Ego Scale (**Wayment et al., 2015**). - Humility by informant rating rather than self-report, as **Davis et al. (2010)** recommend. - Awe Experience Scale self-diminishment facet (**Yaden et al., 2019**) or the small-self measures (**Bai et al., 2017**). - Nondual Awareness Dimensional Assessment, state form (**Hanley, Nakamura and Garland, 2018**), and the Ego-Dissolution Inventory (**Nour et al., 2016**), used as *contrast* measures: bitul should not look like high ego dissolution. - Hood's Mysticism Scale (**Hood, 1975**), for the unity and ineffability items, with the caveat below.

**Match.** Real at the level of experience. "Less full of yourself; not crushed, only less in the way" is close to the quiet ego and to hypo-egoic states. The quiet, low-arousal texture ("not delight, not joy") fits the milder end of self-transcendent experience. The Chassidic warning that bitul sought as a prize turns into pride is the humility paradox and the self-centrality finding, from the inside.

**Where they part.** Sharply, and this is the brief's own example. - **Bitul is not ego dissolution.** Ego dissolution is a loss of the boundary between self and world; its measure was built on psychedelic experience. Bitul bimetzius is a recognition that one has no existence *of one's own* before Him. The person stays fully present, acts, obeys, and is "fully light" like the ray, not erased. A person who reports losing track of who or where they are is not in bitul; that belongs to Part B. - **Bitul is not a low-self-awareness state.** The hypo-egoic literature defines the state by less self-attention. Bitul is defined by what is true (the creature's existence is not its own), and the sources say it can be understood, acknowledged, and even imagined, and that its true form is given from above rather than produced (Sefer HaArachim). - **Bitul faces Someone.** Humility and the quiet ego are about self and others. Bitul is first "before Him"; its humility toward people follows from that. - Hood's scale was built on a general, cross-traditional model of mysticism; its "unity" items do not distinguish bitul from monism, so use it only with that in mind.

**What it gives the guide.** - Describe, never grade. Do not invite the person to rate their humility or "check" the state; the research agrees that self-rated humility is unreliable and that a practice can become a badge. - Pair the step with a hidden act (the manual's "one kindness no one will ever know about"). Kindness has a measurable, modest benefit, and hiddenness guards against self-centrality. - Keep "never on a low day". For someone low, self-diminishing language lands as self-criticism; ruminative self-focus is the kind most tied to distress. On such a day, go to steps 2 and 4.

**Risks the research flags.** Spiritual superiority and pride in humility (Gebauer; Vonk and Visser); self-contempt dressed as humility (the inverse of self-compassion; MacBeth and Gumley); confusing bitul with depersonalization or ego dissolution (Part B).
