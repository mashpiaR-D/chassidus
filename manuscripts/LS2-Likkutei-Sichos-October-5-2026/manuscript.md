*Part of the medicine map · all works*

**What it is.** Edited talks of the Rebbe arranged by weekly portion. Most sichos take a close question in Rashi, Rambam or halacha, solve it, then draw a lesson for inner work (avodah) and a directive (hora'ah). Each volume ends with an appendix of letters. The letters are short, practical answers to individuals, and vol. 36's appendix is a collection on health: body and soul, surgery, medicines, nerves, depression and mental illness. The aim is to turn Torah study into daily practice and to lift each person out of despair or inaction into action, done with joy and trust.

**How much of it is medicine.** some: most of the sichos are close textual and halachic analysis with a short avodah coda, but the letter appendices, especially vol. 36's health letters, give direct and concrete guidance on sadness, worry, grief, despair and mental illness.

**Its model of the person.** A Jew is a divine soul in a body. The body's health is itself a path of God, and body, mood and soul act on each other: trust raises energy and health, and melancholy weakens good deeds. Feelings are judged by what they produce in action. God's love for a person is essential, like a father's love for a small child, and it does not depend on how he behaves. Change comes through deliberate avodah (trust is itself work), through doing rather than brooding, through turning attention outward to helping others, and through the inner point of the soul, which can reach even high states.

**Its philosophy.** God watches over every detail and is Himself goodness. His love and nearness come even to the undeserving, as a "find" (metziah) that no effort earns. Hiddenness is only on our side, since He always sees. Trust is not only a belief. It works on reality ("think good and it will be good"), and good action in the world, not self-absorbed piety, is the purpose of repentance and of life.

**What it adds.** Vol. 36's health appendix is rare in the corpus: the Rebbe's practical guidance on mental illness and low mood. It insists on medical experts and new treatments, occupation and physical work, distraction through helping others, and building self-confidence through responsibility, alongside trust and mitzvos. It also recasts trust (bitachon) as active inner work that shapes outcomes even for the undeserving, and teshuvah as building outward rather than self-abasement.

## I looked honestly at my year and I'm so far off — who am I to come close to God?

After an honest accounting at the new year he sees how far he is from where he should be, and he feels unworthy of nearness and inclined to give up.

**What is really going on.** He assumes closeness to God must be earned by his record. The work teaches that in these days God is 'found' by everyone as a gift, whatever their spiritual state, out of a father's essential love. The right answer to the gift is effort, not despair.

**Program: Ten Days: answering a found nearness with seeking.** (1) Make an honest accounting of the past year's deeds, speech and thoughts. (2) When the gap feels hopeless, recall that in these days God is 'found' by everyone, whatever their state, like a father's love for a small child. (3) Know that He is also 'near', so His love can be felt openly. (4) Answer the gift with intense seeking (drishah): real effort where there was once only casualness. (5) Answer the felt nearness with 'calling out', letting it show in all the soul's powers. *When:* The Ten Days of Repentance from Rosh Hashanah to Yom Kippur, after the new year's accounting. *For:* Someone whose self-accounting tips toward giving up. *Not for:* Someone who would use the unconditional love as license to make no effort; the text requires the effort in response. `LS2·Q01` · `LS2·Q02` · `LS2·Q03` · `LS2·Q04`

**Reframe.** From *"I'm too far gone this year; God can't want me near."* to **In these days God's nearness is a 'find' that doesn't depend on your record, and you can even feel it.** Despair comes from believing closeness is earned. Rooting it in the Father's essential love removes the verdict, and the gift then awakens a matching effort ('as water reflects a face'). `LS2·Q01` · `LS2·Q02` · `LS2·Q03`

**Reframe.** From *"If it's a gift, I don't need to do anything."* to **The unearned gift is what calls out your strongest seeking.** Response mirrors the giver. Receiving freely what was never earned moves a person to effort where before he was casual. `LS2·Q04`

## I'm down and heavy and can't shake the gloom

A low, heavy mood, often tied to illness or a hard situation, that saps the energy for study, prayer and good deeds.

**What is really going on.** The letters call sadness 'baseless from the outset', and bitterness has its place only at set times. Melancholy weakens action and lowers even physical recovery, while trust raises a person's energy and mood.

**Program: Against melancholy: doctor, trust and small daily anchors.** (1) Follow the doctor's instructions. (2) Reject melancholy (marah shechorah) completely, and see that there is reason for contentment and joy. (3) Strengthen trust, which by the doctors' own knowledge raises energy, health and mood. (4) Before morning prayer on weekdays give a few coins to charity; keep the monthly Tehillim portion; have tefillin and mezuzos checked. *When:* Daily, starting before morning prayer. *For:* Someone low in mood, especially with illness in the family. *Not for:* Clinical depression treated by 'rejecting' it alone; the letters always keep medical care first. `LS2·Q17` · `LS2·Q26` · `LS2·Q27` · `LS2·Q21`

**Program: Healing through distraction and doing good.** (1) Take the attention of the person being treated off his condition, as fully as possible. (2) Do it through real activity unrelated to his health, not by lecturing him to stop thinking about it. (3) Choose an activity that gives him inner satisfaction, above all doing a kindness for another person. (4) Where it fits, give him responsibility for others who are weaker, such as leading a group, to build his self-confidence. *When:* Throughout the period of treatment and recovery. *For:* Patients and those caring for them, including in emotional illness. *Not for:* A substitute for professional treatment; it is offered alongside it. `LS2·Q22` · `LS2·Q23` · `LS2·Q24`

**Reframe.** From *"My sadness is just how things are; it's the honest response."* to **Sadness is baseless from the start, and bitterness belongs only at set times; a healthy person is a joyful one.** Sadness is judged by its effect: it weakens good action and recovery. Joy is commanded, so it is possible, and there is real ground for contentment. `LS2·Q16` · `LS2·Q17`

## I can't stop worrying — about health, obstacles, making a living

Constant anxious calculation about whether things will work out and whether he deserves them. Worry spills into prayer and livelihood.

**What is really going on.** He treats trust as a mere belief, or as something only the righteous may hold. The work says trust is its own avodah: relying on God alone, beyond any accounting of merit, and that reliance itself draws the good.

**Program: Trust as avodah.** (1) Do everything within your power by natural means. (2) Cast your lot on God alone, like a prisoner whose fate is wholly in his master's hand. (3) Stop calculating whether you deserve rescue; trust rests on His kindness to the undeserving. (4) Hold to 'think good and it will be good' until you are fully at rest, without worry. (5) Where worry persists, contemplate that God stands over you and is goodness itself, and the worries will lessen. *When:* Ongoing; renewed whenever an obstacle or fear arises. *For:* The anxious and the fearful facing obstacles, illness or hardship. *Not for:* Anyone who would use it to skip natural effort or medical care; the text insists on both. `LS2·Q13` · `LS2·Q14` · `LS2·Q15` · `LS2·Q28`

**Reframe.** From *"Trust is only for the righteous; I don't deserve rescue, so at best I can accept whatever comes."* to **Trust is itself a work of the soul, and relying on God alone draws the good even to the undeserving.** Relying on God without any accounting of merit is answered measure for measure, so trust stops being a reward of merit and becomes something any person can do. `LS2·Q13` · `LS2·Q15`

**Reframe.** From *"Worrying is responsible; I need to calculate every risk."* to **Do what nature requires, then rest completely, without worry: 'think good and it will be good'.** Full rest in God is the substance of the commanded trust, and the letters add that by medical wisdom trust raises energy, health and mood. `LS2·Q14` · `LS2·Q26`

## Grief for someone I lost is eating me and I can't take comfort

A bereaved parent or relative cannot accept comfort, and mourning spreads into sadness and weakened study and action.

**What is really going on.** Grief beyond its measure forgets that the soul lives on. The bereaved can raise the soul through good deeds, and sadness lessens exactly those deeds.

**Program: Turning grief into action for the soul.** (1) Keep mourning within the measure set by halacha. (2) Remember that the soul is eternal and still rises. (3) Add Torah and mitzvos for the soul's sake, in joy at being able to help it. (4) Each weekday morning set aside coins for charity in the deceased's memory; have the mezuzos checked. *When:* Daily, through the mourning year and beyond. *For:* The bereaved who cannot accept comfort. *Not for:* The acute first shock of loss; calling sadness a weakness of faith can wound someone who is freshly bereaved. `LS2·Q18` · `LS2·Q19`

**Reframe.** From *"My grief shows how much I loved him."* to **The soul lives and rises through your good deeds, and those need joy; excess sorrow lessens what you can give it.** Moving love from sorrow to action gives the bereaved a living bond with the soul and a reason for joy. `LS2·Q18`

## I keep thinking the worst — that there's a harsh decree on us

Dark thoughts that the family is doomed, which feed sadness, despair and paralysis.

**What is really going on.** Such thoughts have no basis. They are known by their fruit: they weaken good action and bring sadness and despair, so both holy reason and human reason reject them.

**Program: Test a thought by its fruit.** (1) When unsure whether a thought is healthy, look at what it does to your action. (2) If it brings weakness in good deeds, sadness or despair, uproot it as groundless. (3) Replace it with 'think good and it will be good', and with added good deeds by the whole family. *When:* Whenever a dark or doubtful thought arises. *For:* People caught in foreboding or catastrophic thinking. *Not for:* Intrusive thoughts from OCD or trauma that do not yield to self-correction and need clinical care. `LS2·Q19`

**Reframe.** From *"These fearful thoughts might be true, so I should keep them."* to **Judge a thought by its fruit; one that weakens you in action is groundless.** An outside, practical test ends the argument about content and frees the person to act. `LS2·Q19`

## People let me down; what's the point of all my effort?

A teacher or worker disheartened by others' failures and doubting that her work matters.

**What is really going on.** She measures by numbers and by others' conduct. One person can change everything, as Abraham was one and inherited the land.

**Reframe.** From *"Others failed, so my work is worthless."* to **One person can tip the world; Abraham was one.** Moving the measure from numbers and others' conduct to the weight of a single deed restores meaning and effort. `LS2·Q20`

## My prayer and study are scattered; my mind won't stay put

When he tries to contemplate, stray thoughts crowd in. He repeats words, feels too far from the recommended contemplation, and his mood colors everything.

**What is really going on.** The mind needs anchoring and a pace it can carry. The sense of 'I'm far from this' is itself the inclination's temptation, to be answered by repetition, not argument.

**Program: Anchoring a wandering mind in prayer and contemplation.** (1) Give charity before praying. (2) Pray from inside the siddur, and keep it open even when your eyes are closed, because the letters hold the thought. (3) Learn from the book, keeping it open while you contemplate. (4) Spread the meaning of the prayer's words over one to two weeks, then begin again. (5) If you feel far from the contemplation you were given, repeat it in your mind several times and ignore the inclination's claims. *When:* Daily morning prayer; for someone in this state, about two hours on weekdays. *For:* Those whose prayer or study is scattered or mood-driven. *Not for:* No limit stated beyond that. `LS2·Q29` · `LS2·Q30` · `LS2·Q28`

## My prayer comes only from my own pain, not from lofty awe

He feels his prayer is unworthy because it comes from personal troubles or plain fear, not refined awe.

**What is really going on.** By the sicha's reading, even plain fear of punishment clears the mind enough for prayer, and bitterness over a private lack, like Chana's childlessness, is itself the lowliness that God hears. Every soul can touch the highest level at least slightly.

**Reframe.** From *"My prayer only comes from my own troubles or plain fear, so it isn't real prayer."* to **Even simple fear settles the mind for prayer, and bitterness from a private lack is itself the lowliness God hears.** By lowering the entry threshold and honoring the pain, the sicha makes prayer possible now, while the soul's inner point still reaches toward the highest. `LS2·Q07` · `LS2·Q08` · `LS2·Q09`

## I did wrong and all I can do is cry and feel small

Repentance is stuck in confession, self-abasement and weeping, with no forward motion.

**What is really going on.** Confession and humbling are not enough. Repentance needs an opposite, constructive deed and 'building' in the world outside oneself, a movement opposite to the penitent's lowliness. The higher repentance is in great joy.

**Program: Repentance that builds.** (1) Acknowledge the wrong and confess it. (2) Do not stop at crying and self-abasement. (3) Do a deed that is the opposite of the wrong. (4) Go on to build something in the world outside yourself; this calls for a movement opposite to lowliness. *When:* After a failing; ongoing. *For:* Penitents stuck in guilt and humiliation. *Not for:* No limit stated. `LS2·Q11` · `LS2·Q12`

**Reframe.** From *"True repentance means crying and feeling low."* to **Repentance is completed by building: an opposite deed and good done in the world, and the higher repentance is in joy.** Turning the energy of guilt into construction outward breaks the cycle of self-focus. `LS2·Q11` · `LS2·Q12` · `LS2·Q05`

## God doesn't see what I'm going through

In suffering, God feels absent and unseeing.

**What is really going on.** Hiddenness is only on the receiver's side. In truth God sees the distress and never looks away, and when a person turns to Him the hiding ends at once.

**Reframe.** From *"God has turned away; He doesn't see my pain."* to **He sees fully; the hiding is only on your side and ends the moment you turn to Him.** The sense of absence becomes a matter of perception, not abandonment, so turning toward Him becomes possible and immediate. `LS2·Q06`

## My success is going to my head

When his service shines and influences others, he begins to feel his own importance.

**What is really going on.** The light itself should not be lessened. Only the feeling of self needs to be diminished, as the moon wanes after it is full, and that waning is a higher stage.

**Program: The waning moon: keep the light, shrink the self.** (1) Do not lessen any light or influence you give. (2) After a peak of success, begin working on lessening the feeling of self and of your own worth. (3) Let that feeling shrink step by step until it disappears, like the moon before the new moon. *When:* Monthly rhythm: the second half of each month after the full moon. *For:* Active, successful people at risk of self-importance. *Not for:* Someone already crushed or self-effacing; the text addresses the successful. `LS2·Q10`

## It's my surroundings' fault; if I moved I'd finally grow

A yeshiva student blames his environment and friends for his lack of study and prayer and wants to leave.

**What is really going on.** The work reads these arguments as the inclination's cunning, and says the move would bring the same trouble. The answer is to drop the arguments entirely and to influence his friends rather than be influenced by them.

**Program: Dropping the blame arguments.** (1) Recognize that 'my surroundings are to blame' is the inclination's cunning. (2) Put the arguments completely out of mind. (3) Throw yourself into study and prayer within the setting you are in. (4) Influence your friends toward good rather than be influenced by them. *When:* Now, when the urge to leave arises. *For:* A student blaming his environment. *Not for:* Settings that are truly harmful or abusive; the letter addresses a sound yeshiva. `LS2·Q31`

## Someone I love has broken down mentally; what do we do?

Families write about a child or spouse who is depressed, in a fantasy world, or hospitalized, and about doctors who give no hope.

**What is really going on.** The letters say such illness is common and treatable, and recoveries are known. The answer is medical care joined with occupation, distraction from the illness, purposeful activity and responsibility for others, which builds self-confidence.

**Program: Healing through distraction and doing good.** (1) Take the attention of the person being treated off his condition, as fully as possible. (2) Do it through real activity unrelated to his health, not by lecturing him to stop thinking about it. (3) Choose an activity that gives him inner satisfaction, above all doing a kindness for another person. (4) Where it fits, give him responsibility for others who are weaker, such as leading a group, to build his self-confidence. *When:* Throughout the period of treatment and recovery. *For:* Patients and those caring for them, including in emotional illness. *Not for:* A substitute for professional treatment; it is offered alongside it. `LS2·Q22` · `LS2·Q23` · `LS2·Q24`

**Program: Family care in mental illness.** (1) Consult expert doctors in the field and follow their directions. (2) Keep in constant contact with experts, asking regularly about new treatments and medicines. (3) Keep the patient's time full, with things that do not call for deep brooding. (4) Include some physical work. (5) Strengthen his trust in God and help him put the past out of mind. *When:* Ongoing through treatment and after the return home. *For:* Families of someone who has had a breakdown. *Not for:* No limit stated. `LS2·Q25` · `LS2·Q32`

## Cautions

- 'Sadness is baseless' and 'reject melancholy entirely' could shame a clinically depressed person; the letters themselves put the doctor first. - Calling a mourner's sadness 'weakness of faith in the soul's survival' can wound someone who is freshly bereaved; do not use it in acute grief. - 'Think good and it will be good' can turn into self-blame if things go badly (the sicha says a lack of trust caused Moshe's trouble); never imply that a person's fear caused his illness or loss. - Distraction and keeping busy are offered alongside treatment, not instead of it; do not use them to avoid needed therapy or processing of trauma. - The segulos (mezuzah, tefillin, tzedakah) are given together with medical care, never as a substitute. - Waning the self is addressed to the successful, not to the already crushed or self-effacing. - The letters are wary of therapists hostile to faith or to honoring parents; do not generalize this into distrust of mental-health care, which the same letters demand.
